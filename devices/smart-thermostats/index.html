    Smart Thermostats

    A wall computer whose entire job is closing the same 24-volt relays a 1950s mercury switch closed — plus a radio that lets your utility reach into the loop. The hardware is simple; the record is about who else holds the switch.

    FCC ID

    FCC IDs vary by model. Google’s Nest thermostats file under Google’s grantee code A4R (the Nest Mini on this bench is A4R-H2C; the thermostat models file under the same code), and Ecobee files under its own. The filings document the Wi-Fi/Bluetooth radios and, in the internal photos, the relay board and the wire terminals that are the actual product.

    Every radio a device carries — Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, ultra-wideband, NFC — needs an FCC equipment authorization before the device can be sold in the United States. To get one, the manufacturer files photographs and test reports with the FCC, and those filings are public record. Check the label on your specific unit and look it up at fccid.io.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    • Internal photos — the two-board split: a display/compute board and a base plate carrying the wire terminals and relays — the thermostat’s real architecture in one picture
    • The filed manual — wiring diagrams for the terminal letters (R, C, W, Y, G, O/B) that are the industry’s shared language
    • Test reports — radio output; the thermostat is a low-duty-cycle radio and the reports show it

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (withheld under confidentiality requests where the maker files them), firmware, and the full bill of materials.

    Exploded view — the assemblies in build order

    This is the centerpiece of the page: the device taken apart into its assemblies, in the order they go together, every part named in plain language with what it does. An exploded view is the drawing a manufacturer makes showing every part separated along the path it assembles — the single most useful document in repair, and the one most often kept behind a dealer login.

    The source

    Parts book: none published. FCC internal photos plus the makers’ own installation guides (which are genuinely public and genuinely good — HVAC is a trade that documents wiring) are the sources. A thermostat is two assemblies: the base on the wall (terminals + relays) and the head (display + computer) that clicks onto it.

    Coverage: PARTIAL — no public parts book; FCC internal-photo basis, with manufacturer installation guides (public) for the wiring layer.

    Build order

    Step 1 — The base plate — terminals and relays

    The base stays with the house. It is a terminal strip and a set of small relays (or solid-state switches) that connect the furnace’s 24 VAC control lines.

    PartWhat this part does
    Terminal block (R, C, W, Y, G, O/B, ...)Screw or spring terminals labeled with the trade’s letters: R = 24 VAC power, C = common, W = heat call, Y = cooling call, G = fan, O/B = heat-pump reversing valve. Every thermostat and every furnace board in North America speaks these letters.
    Relays / switching stageThe actual product. A ‘call for heat’ is the thermostat connecting R to W. Everything smart is decoration on this switch.
    C-wire power pathSmart thermostats power themselves from R and C. Homes wired without a C wire are the category’s defining problem: older stats ran on batteries or stole power in trickles (see Faults).
    Fuses & protectionThe furnace’s control board carries a 3–5 A automotive-style fuse that blows when thermostat wires short — the cheapest part in the system and the cause of many ‘dead thermostat’ calls.

    Step 2 — The head — display, sensors, radio

    The part you look at. It draws its power from the base and thinks about schedules; it contains no part of the heating circuit’s authority beyond asking the base to switch.

    PartWhat this part does
    Display & input ring/dialNest’s rotating ring or Ecobee’s touchscreen — input devices over a small Linux-class computer.
    Temperature / humidity / occupancy sensorsThe thermostat knows the temperature at the thermostat — a hallway number the whole house is judged by. Remote room sensors exist because this is a real limitation, sold back to you as an accessory.
    Wi-Fi / Bluetooth / Thread radioThe remote-control path — and the demand-response path (see the record).
    Backup battery (some models)A small cell that rides through power blips and, on C-wire-less installs, shares the load with power-stealing.

    What is missing, in the open: boards and relays are not sold as parts; a dead head is a whole-thermostat replacement. But the layer that actually fails in homes — wiring, fuses, C-wire power — is fully documented by the makers’ public installation guides, because HVAC contractors demanded paper. This is what a trade-documented category looks like.

    Faults — which part fails, and how you know

    Symptom first. Most ‘smart thermostat failures’ are power or wiring wearing a smart costume:

    • Screen blank, furnace silent — check the furnace control board’s 3–5 A fuse and the C-wire path before the thermostat. A tripped float switch (clogged condensate line) cuts R power on many air handlers and presents identically.
    • Thermostat reboots when the furnace fires — power stealing starving: the voltage dips when the load it sips through energizes. The real fix is a C wire or the maker’s adapter kit, not a new thermostat.
    • Short cycling / relay chatter — the same power-stealing pathology, or a heat anticipator setting fighting a high-efficiency furnace. Documented in every maker’s install guide because it is that common.
    • House temperature wrong by degrees — the sensor is in a hallway with sun on it, or wall heat from the hole behind the base (seal the wire hole — draft air from inside the wall is a classic).
    • ‘It changed my setpoint by itself’ — it did: learning schedules, utility demand-response enrollment, or a household member’s app. The thermostat’s event history (and your utility program enrollment) is the diagnosis. See the record.

    Safety, before you open anything

    Thermostat wiring is 24 VAC — low voltage that will not shock you, connected to a furnace that contains 120/240 VAC, gas, and a control board that costs real money. Kill power at the breaker before pulling the thermostat off its base: shorting R to C with the power on is how the little board fuse (and occasionally the board) dies.

    Never jumper terminals experimentally on a live system to ‘see what happens.’ R-to-W is a heat call; R-to-the-wrong-terminal on a heat pump can run heat and cooling against each other. The makers’ wiring guides are public — use them, photograph your old wiring before touching a wire, and label every wire by terminal letter, not by color (colors lie; letters are the contract).

    Did you know

    • Your thermostat’s authority is one relay: heat is the furnace’s own 24 volts connected to the W terminal. Every smart feature ever shipped is a way of deciding when to close that switch.
    • The C wire is the category’s original sin: homes wired for dumb thermostats often lack the common wire a smart stat needs for power, and an entire accessory industry (adapters, add-a-wire kits) exists to forgive the omission.
    • Demand-response events are not hacks or malfunctions: they are the product working as enrolled. The documented surprise is that enrollment is forgettable and the event is not.
