What the FCC authorization tells you as a hardware owner. The authorization certifies that the device's radio emissions stay within legal limits. It is not a statement about software behavior, remote management, or what traffic the device sends home. Those are separate questions that the FCC does not answer. This page tries to answer them from the hardware side.

What this thing is

The XB3 is the box Comcast handed to millions of internet customers. Its real name is the Arris TG1682G, and one box does three jobs: it is a cable modem (it talks to Comcast through the coax cable in your wall), a router (it shares that connection with your home network), and a Wi-Fi access point (the wireless part). It also has two phone jacks, because Comcast sold home phone service through the same coax line.

The FCC filing UIDTG1682 describes it as a 4-port DOCSIS 3.0 Gateway made by ARRIS. That description is technically precise: four gigabit Ethernet ports, DOCSIS 3.0 cable standard, made by ARRIS. "Telephony Gateway" is the industry term for a modem that also handles telephone calls over the internet connection.

If you had Xfinity internet in the late 2010s, there is a good chance this exact box — tall, black, with a single glowing strip on the front — was sitting in your living room.

Whose box is this / legal basis for this report

This unit is privately owned, purchased outright, opened on a private bench. That distinction matters at law.

Federal law — CFAA, 18 U.S.C. § 1030. The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), codified as 18 U.S.C. § 1030, is the primary federal cybercrime statute used to prosecute computer-related offenses. Enacted in 1986 and periodically amended to address evolving digital threats, the CFAA criminalizes unauthorized access to computers, data theft, system sabotage, password trafficking, and cyber-extortion. The operative word is unauthorized. Authorization and scope of access — establishing that the defendant had express permission, implied authorization, or a good-faith belief that their actions fell within authorized boundaries — is a recognized defense framework under the CFAA. Opening and reading from hardware you own, without accessing any third-party network or service, does not constitute unauthorized access under any reasonable reading of the statute. Nothing in this report accesses Comcast's network, extracts credentials, modifies firmware, or touches any device that isn't ours.

Texas law — Texas Penal Code § 33.02 (Breach of Computer Security). Texas independently criminalizes accessing a computer, network, or system without the owner's effective consent. The operative question is identical to the federal one: consent and ownership. This is our hardware. We have effective consent by ownership. Reading a boot log off a device you own is not a breach of computer security under Texas law. This report documents read-only observation of a privately owned device.

What this report does not do:

  • Access any Comcast network, infrastructure, or service
  • Modify, extract, publish, or distribute device firmware
  • Extract passwords, keys, or credentials
  • Perform any operation on a rented or leased device
  • Instruct anyone to access hardware they do not own

Everything documented here is observation of our own hardware using publicly available tools and techniques. The legal ceiling for each procedure is stated explicitly in the procedure section.

A short history

The chip inside this box has a backstory that includes a fraud lawsuit and years of documented latency problems.

One note first: "XB3" was Comcast's service tier branding, not a single hardware design. Arris made this one; Cisco made another (the DPC3941B). Same service tier, different hardware, different chips.

2010 — Intel acquires Texas Instruments' cable modem chip business. TI's DOCSIS SoC line — the Puma series — becomes Intel's property. The Puma 6 chip at the heart of this box is born from that acquisition. [Intel press release, 2010]

2013 — The Intel DHCE2652, part of the Puma 6 family, is introduced as a 1.2 GHz Intel Atom-based SoC.

September 2015 — Arris announces the TG1682G for Comcast. It ships as a 4-port DOCSIS 3.0 Gateway with 24 downstream / 8 upstream bonded channels, two phone lines, and dual-band Wi-Fi.

December 2016 — The Puma 6 latency defect goes public. Users document severe, periodic lag spikes under real traffic conditions — enough to ruin gaming and VoIP sessions. Two firmware updates from Intel never fully resolve it. [The Register, December 2016 — archived]

March 2017 — A class-action lawsuit is filed against Arris over the defective chip. [The Register, March 2017]

May 2017 — Comcast launches Xfinity xFi. At launch, Comcast stated that 10 million customers already had compatible gateways — but that figure covered the XB3 and its successor the XB6 combined, not XB3s alone. [Comcast, May 2017; TechCrunch]

Fall 2017 — This specific board unit is manufactured. Board date code: week 46 of 2017. Shell mold date: October 26, 2017. Ethernet magnetic date code: week 41, 2017. Every date stamp on this unit points to the same six-week window in autumn 2017.

August 2018 — Intel issues CVE-2017-5693, a formal security advisory for the Puma 6 latency flaw. [Intel Security Advisory; The Register notes the flaw traces back to the chip's TI lineage.]

April 2019 — CommScope acquires Arris for $7.4 billion. [SEC filing, April 2019 — SEC EDGAR]

2020–2022 — The XB7 (Wi-Fi 6) and XB8 (Wi-Fi 6E) successors take over Comcast's active rental lineup. [Light Reading; Comcast newsroom]

Today — The XB3 is retired from Comcast's primary rental lineup but continues in service as the gateway for Xfinity NOW (prepaid) customers. Second life instead of landfill.

The outside — physical layout

The enclosure is a tall black ABS plastic tower, vented on the sides, with a single LED status strip on the front face and a WPS push-button on the top panel.

Front panel: Single LED status strip. The strip segments correspond to: PWR (power), US/DS (upstream/downstream cable signal lock), ONLINE (internet connectivity), 2.4G, 5G, TEL1, TEL2. Status is communicated by color and blink pattern, not by individual labeled indicators.

Top panel: One WPS button. WPS (Wi-Fi Protected Setup) allows a compatible device to join the Wi-Fi network without typing a password, using a push-button or PIN exchange instead.

Rear panel (left to right):

  • 2× USB-A ports (USB 2.0)
  • 2× RJ-11 phone jacks (TEL1, TEL2)
  • 4× RJ-45 Ethernet jacks (gigabit)
  • 1× F-type coaxial connector (cable input)
  • 1× power barrel jack (12 V DC)
  • 1× Reset pinhole (recessed)

Reading the plastic: the shell's birth certificate. Molded into the interior surface of the side panel: "TG1682 KD#5" — the model designation stamped at the factory. Beside it, four injection-mold date wheels. Reading this unit: Year 2017, Month October, Day 26. This is the date the plastic shell was injection-molded. The board date code — stamped directly on the PCB — reads 1746 (week 46 of 2017). The Ethernet magnetics are stamped 1741 (week 41, 2017). All three dates are internally consistent and point to a single manufacturing window in autumn 2017, during the height of the Comcast xFi rollout.

The main board — ARCT04129 REV G

One circuit board runs the whole device. Board part number: ARCT04129 REV G. Date code: 1746 (week 46, 2017).

What the boot log says — listening to the hardware wake up

The J3 header is a UART serial console: the chip's internal text output channel, narrating everything it does from power-on to full boot. Think of it as pressing your ear to a wall: you hear everything on the other side, but you don't interact with it.

The boot sequence runs: Boot ROM memory/parameter dump → U-Boot loading from eMMC → kernel decompression → Linux 3.12.14 boot on the Puma 6. U-Boot reports Board-Type: harborpark-mg. The machine boots the UBFI1 image from eMMC.

"harborpark-mg" is Arris's internal engineering codename for this gateway platform. "MG" = Multimedia Gateway. This identifier appears in U-Boot's environment and allows engineers (and researchers) to identify the exact platform without needing access to internal Arris documentation.

The kernel build date is July 24, 2020 — three years after this board's manufacture date. Comcast continued pushing software updates to XB3 units long after the hardware stopped shipping, keeping the software current on deployed units. This is why the board date (2017) and the software date (2020) are different.

The Flash: 0 kB entry in the boot log is significant. Earlier public hardware records for the TG1682G family list a raw NAND flash chip. This 2017 board revision reports zero raw NAND and boots entirely from eMMC. That is a documented internal hardware revision between the FCC filing generation (2014–2015) and this unit's manufacture date (2017).

The Puma 6 runs two operating systems simultaneously. The Puma 6 runs two Linux instances simultaneously — one on the ARM core (DOCSIS modem stack, inherited from the TI lineage) and one on the Atom cores (routing, NAT, Wi-Fi management, web interface). The ARM Linux and the Atom Linux communicate over an internal IPC bus. When U-Boot says "Linux 3.12.14 (ARM)," it is reporting the ARM-side kernel. The Atom-side may run a different kernel version. The boot log capture covers the ARM side.

What we found: the bridge mode problem

Bridge mode is a setting in the gateway's web interface that is supposed to turn the box into a plain modem — disabling the router and Wi-Fi and letting your own router handle everything. In theory.

In practice: putting this unit into bridge mode did not disable the Wi-Fi radios. The box continued broadcasting hidden (non-broadcasting SSID) wireless networks after bridge mode was enabled. There is no software switch in the Comcast-provided firmware to force the radios off.

This is not a theoretical problem. Hidden networks still transmit beacon frames, participate in Wi-Fi channel negotiation, and can cause co-channel interference with your own router's networks. If you run an XB3 in bridge mode next to your own router, you are running four Wi-Fi radios (XB3's 2.4 + 5 GHz hidden networks, plus your router's 2.4 + 5 GHz networks) instead of two. Depending on your router's channel selection, this can measurably degrade Wi-Fi throughput.

The hardware-level answer to this, documented as reference — not performed on this unit: The board carries two shielded Atheros Wi-Fi modules fed by an 8-pin TPS54328 buck regulator. Grounding the enable pin (Pin 8) of the TPS54328 prevents the regulator from supplying power to the Wi-Fi modules. The modem, routing, and wired Ethernet functions continue working. The modification is reversible (the pin can be ungrounded). This is documented here as technical reference because it explains the root cause of the interference problem — not as a modification guide and not as something we performed.

The physical separation answer: place the XB3 in a location physically distant from your router. The hidden networks' effective range is limited by the inverse square law the same as any other radio. Distance attenuates interference.

How to communicate with this device — tools, procedures, and legal limits

This section documents every legitimate, legal, non-destructive method of communicating with the TG1682G at increasing levels of depth. Each procedure is bounded by the legal framework stated above: these procedures apply only to hardware you own outright.

Tier 1 — Web interface (no tools required)

What it accesses: Router configuration, Wi-Fi settings, DHCP table, firewall rules, basic diagnostics, bridge mode toggle, firmware version string.

How to reach it:

  1. Connect a computer to one of the four Ethernet jacks on the back of the XB3 with a standard Cat5e or Cat6 Ethernet cable.
  2. Open a web browser. Navigate to http://10.0.0.1 (the default gateway address for this device).
  3. Default credentials: username admin, password printed on the label on the bottom of the unit (or password if the label is missing or the unit has been factory reset).
  4. If the web interface is inaccessible, the unit may be in a failed state, or Comcast's firmware may have changed the management address. Try http://192.168.100.1 for the cable modem diagnostic page (no login required on most firmware versions — shows signal levels, channel bonding status, and event log).

What you can read without credentials at 192.168.100.1:

  • Downstream channel lock status, frequency, power level, SNR, and corrected/uncorrected error counts for all 24 bonded channels
  • Upstream channel lock status, frequency, power level, and symbol rate
  • Cable modem event log (including T3/T4 timeout errors, which indicate upstream problems with the coaxial plant)
  • DOCSIS provisioning status

Tools needed: Any Ethernet-capable computer. Any web browser. A Cat5e or Cat6 patch cable.

Tool brands for patch cables: Monoprice, Cable Matters, or AmazonBasics all work. Any cable rated Cat5e or higher will work for gigabit speeds.

Legal ceiling: Reading the web interface of your own device is fully authorized under federal and Texas law. No statute is implicated.

Tier 2 — Coax signal level diagnostics (no tools required)

What it accesses: RF signal quality on the cable plant between your wall and the CMTS (Comcast's head-end equipment). This is the fastest way to diagnose whether an XB3 problem is software or hardware/RF.

How to read it:

  1. Navigate to http://192.168.100.1 from any device on the LAN.
  2. Click "Signal" or navigate to the Signal tab.
  3. Read the downstream power levels (target: −7 to +7 dBmV), upstream power levels (target: 38–48 dBmV), and downstream SNR (target: ≥33 dB for QAM256, ≥27 dB for QAM64).

What out-of-range values mean:

  • Downstream power below −15 dBmV or above +15 dBmV: cable plant problem (splitters, corroded connectors, damaged coax). Not a modem problem.
  • Upstream power above 50 dBmV: the modem is compensating for a lossy upstream path. Cable plant problem.
  • T3/T4 timeouts in the event log: upstream ranging failures. Almost always a cable plant problem.

Tools needed: None.

Legal ceiling: Reading signal levels from your own modem is fully authorized. No statute is implicated.

Tier 3 — UART serial console capture (tools required — read-only)

What it accesses: The complete boot log of the device from power-on through full Linux boot. No credentials required. No software modifications. Read-only.

Legal ceiling: Establishing that you had express permission, implied authorization, or a good-faith belief your actions fell within authorized boundaries is a recognized defense framework under the CFAA. Passively listening to output from your own device's UART port — without attempting to interact with or modify the device — does not constitute unauthorized access. You are reading output the device is actively transmitting. Texas Penal Code § 33.02 requires lack of effective consent; you have effective consent as the owner. This procedure has been performed and documented publicly with no legal challenge. This applies only to hardware you own outright. Do not perform this procedure on a rented, leased, or borrowed device.

Safety before you start:

  • Never connect the VCC (3.3 V) pin of the J3 header to anything. Pin 1 is VCC. Connecting it to your adapter's VCC can damage the board. Only connect RX, TX, and GND.
  • Confirm your adapter is 3.3 V logic level. The J3 header operates at 3.3 V. A 5 V adapter will damage the board's UART input. Glasgow, CP2102, and FT232RL breakouts are all 3.3 V by default or selectable.
  • Power the XB3 from its normal power supply. Do not attempt to power the board through J3.
  • Connect GND first. Always establish ground before connecting signal lines.

Step-by-step procedure:

Step 1 — Open the enclosure
Remove the four Phillips screws on the underside of the XB3. The top half of the enclosure lifts away. The main board is in the lower shell. Do not force anything. The board is secured by two more screws to the lower shell; it does not need to be removed for UART access.

Step 2 — Locate J3
J3 is a 4-pin through-hole header, unpopulated (no pins installed from the factory), located on the top surface of the main board adjacent to the main SoC (the Puma 6 chip). The silkscreen label "J3" is printed next to it. If no pins are installed, you will need to either solder a 4-pin 2.54 mm male header or use a pogo-pin clip that makes contact with the bare pads.

Step 3 — Solder or clip the header (if unpopulated)
Option A — Solder: Insert a 4-pin 2.54 mm breakaway male header strip into the J3 pads. Apply solder with a fine-tip (T18-B or equivalent) soldering iron set to 320–360°C. Hold for 2–3 seconds per pin. Let cool. Use flux if needed.

Recommended iron: Hakko FX-888D (professional, $110) or Pinecil V2 (open hardware, ~$28 at pine64.org).

Option B — Clip: Use a pogo-pin test clip (2.54 mm pitch, 4-position). Hold it in place during the capture. No soldering required. Less reliable contact but non-destructive.

Step 4 — Connect the adapter

RX and TX are crossed: the board's transmit connects to the adapter's receive, and vice versa. This is standard for UART connections.

Step 5 — Configure the terminal emulator

Settings for this device (confirmed from boot log capture):

  • Baud rate: 115200
  • Data bits: 8
  • Parity: None
  • Stop bits: 1
  • Flow control: None

In PuTTY: Connection type = Serial, Speed = 115200, Port = your COM port (Windows) or /dev/ttyUSB0 (Linux) or /dev/cu.usbserial-XXXX (macOS).

In screen (macOS/Linux): screen /dev/ttyUSB0 115200

In minicom (Linux): minicom -D /dev/ttyUSB0 -b 115200

What you type — connect with screen:

$ screen /dev/ttyUSB0 115200

What you type — connect with minicom (install it first if your system does not have it):

$ apt install minicom
$ minicom -D /dev/ttyUSB0 -b 115200

Step 6 — Power on the XB3 and capture

Plug in the XB3's power cable. The terminal window will immediately begin displaying text. The full boot sequence — from Boot ROM through U-Boot through Linux kernel initialization — takes approximately 30–60 seconds. No console access is obtained. U-Boot is configured with a 0-second autoboot delay, and the UART never drops to a shell — capture is strictly receive-only.

Save the terminal output to a text file for review. Most terminal emulators have a logging option (in PuTTY: Session → Logging; in screen: Ctrl-A, H).

What you will see:

  1. Boot ROM initialization messages from the Puma 6
  2. U-Boot banner: version, build date, board identification ("harborpark-mg")
  3. Memory initialization and DRAM size report ("DRAM: 256 MB")
  4. eMMC detection and partition table scan
  5. Kernel load and decompression
  6. Linux boot messages: kernel version, driver initialization, filesystem mount
  7. Final: application software initialization. The console goes quiet. No login prompt appears.

Expected output — the same boot sequence as it appears in the terminal, in order (the whole sequence takes about 30–60 seconds, then the console goes quiet):

Step 7 — Disconnect cleanly
Power down the XB3 (unplug power). Remove the jumper wires. Remove the adapter. Replace the enclosure cover.

Source for this procedure: This procedure and its output were documented publicly at github.com/JamesMarcusAnderson/xb3, covering the UART boot log capture of the TG1682G using a Glasgow Interface Explorer running the UART applet at 3.3 V.

Tier 4 — JTAG (mapped, not used — documented as reference only)

What it accesses: Direct access to the processor's debug architecture. JTAG (Joint Test Action Group, IEEE standard 1149.1) allows reading and writing processor registers, halting execution, and in some implementations reading/writing memory directly. It is substantially more powerful than UART.

Why we did not use it: The JTAG header J4 was mapped but no JTAG session was ever established. The risk-to-reward ratio does not favor it for an educational documentation project. Establishing a JTAG session on a running Linux system — particularly one where the JTAG is not documented by the vendor — risks corrupting the device's flash storage if a write operation occurs inadvertently. The UART capture already answered the questions this project needed to answer.

Legal ceiling: JTAG access to your own hardware is not prohibited by any federal or Texas statute. The CFAA requires unauthorized access; you cannot be unauthorized on your own device. However, if a JTAG session were used to extract firmware and that firmware were then distributed, separate intellectual property law (the DMCA, 17 U.S.C. § 1201) would become relevant — specifically the anti-circumvention provisions. This report does not extract, store, or distribute firmware.

Tools required (for reference):

  • OpenOCD (open-source JTAG software, free): openocd.org
  • JTAG adapter: J-Link EDU Mini (~$20, Segger), Bus Pirate v4 (~$30), or Glasgow Interface Explorer (same tool as UART, different applet)
  • JTAG probe cables: 10-pin or 20-pin ARM CoreSight cable depending on the J4 pinout

The J4 pinout on this board has not been published. Without the pinout, a JTAG session cannot be established without risking shorts. The FCC filing does not include a schematic. Arris has not published a service manual. This is a documented gap.

Should you still use it?

Short answer: as a learning platform, absolutely. As your daily internet gateway in 2026, probably not — and here's why, honestly.

Reasons to keep one

  • It's a perfect teaching device. UART header, JTAG pads, dual-OS boot, well-documented board — everything a hardware student needs, for the price of e-waste.
  • Xfinity NOW (prepaid) still provisions them. If you're on prepaid Xfinity, this may be what they hand you.
  • Wired performance is fine. 24×8 DOCSIS 3.0 delivers real-world speeds adequate for most households. The Puma 6 latency flaw affects latency-sensitive traffic (gaming, VoIP), not bulk throughput.

Reasons to retire it

  • The Puma 6 latency defect (CVE-2017-5693) was never fully fixed. If you game or video-call, you'll feel it.
  • DOCSIS 3.0 is legacy. Comcast's network is moving to DOCSIS 3.1/4.0; this box can't use the new capacity.
  • Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac) only. No Wi-Fi 6/6E/7. Fine for browsing, limiting for a house full of devices.
  • No more firmware updates. The 2020 kernel build was likely the last. Unpatched gateway software facing the internet is a risk that grows with time.

What to do with a retired one

  • Learn on it: UART capture, board mapping, component identification — everything in this report.
  • Use it as a wired-only modem behind your own router (bridge mode + the radio caveat above).
  • Recycle it through an e-waste program. Don't landfill the lead solder and brominated flame retardants.

Frequently asked questions

Is the XB3 the same as the TG1682G?
Yes. "XB3" was Comcast's service-tier branding; TG1682G is Arris's hardware model. Same box. (Cisco's DPC3941B was also sold as an "XB3" — different hardware, same tier.)

Can I install custom firmware (OpenWrt, DD-WRT) on it?
No. The bootloader is locked, the firmware is signed, and no third-party firmware exists for the Puma 6 platform. The UART gives you read-only boot logs, not a shell.

Does bridge mode really leave the Wi-Fi on?
On the firmware versions we tested, yes — the radios keep broadcasting hidden networks. See the bridge mode section above. This is the single most useful thing to know about this box.

What's the difference between the XB3, XB6, XB7, and XB8?
XB3 (this box): DOCSIS 3.0, Puma 6, Wi-Fi 5. XB6: DOCSIS 3.1, Wi-Fi 5. XB7: DOCSIS 3.1, Wi-Fi 6. XB8: DOCSIS 4.0-ready, Wi-Fi 6E. Each generation fixes the previous one's sins; the XB3's sin was the Puma 6.

Is it legal to open mine?
If you own it outright (not rented/leased), yes — see the legal basis section above. If Comcast rents it to you, don't open it.

Where can I buy one for learning?
eBay, thrift stores, e-waste recyclers. They sell for almost nothing because they're obsolete as gateways — which is exactly what makes them perfect bench hardware.

Words to know

DOCSIS — Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification. The standard protocol that cable modems use to communicate with the cable company's head-end equipment. Version 3.0 (in this box) supports up to 24 bonded downstream channels and 8 upstream. Version 3.1 (in the XB6 and later) uses OFDM and achieves multi-gigabit speeds. [CableLabs DOCSIS 3.0 specification — publicly available at cablelabs.com]

SoC (System on a Chip) — An integrated circuit that combines multiple functional blocks — processor, memory controller, modem hardware, network hardware — into a single piece of silicon. The Intel Puma 6 is a SoC. The alternative is a discrete design where each function is a separate chip, connected by traces on the board.

U-Boot — A widely used open-source bootloader for embedded Linux systems. It initializes the hardware, finds the kernel on storage, loads it into RAM, and hands off execution. Source code publicly available at denx.de/wiki/U-Boot.

UART — Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitter. A hardware serial communication interface used for debug output and console access in embedded systems. Operates at a configured baud rate (115,200 bps on this device).

JTAG — Joint Test Action Group. IEEE standard 1149.1. A debug and test interface wired directly into a chip's boundary scan architecture. More powerful than UART; also more dangerous to use incorrectly.

eMMC — Embedded MultiMediaCard. A type of flash storage soldered directly to the board, using the MMC bus interface. Functionally similar to the storage in a smartphone. Different from raw NAND flash: eMMC includes its own controller chip in the same package.

Buck regulator — A switching power supply that steps voltage down (for example, from 12 V to 1.8 V). More efficient than a linear regulator. The TPS54328 is a buck regulator.

Bridge mode — A modem operating mode that passes the public IP address through to a connected device (typically a separate router), rather than performing NAT itself. Intended to eliminate double-NAT.

MoCA — Multimedia over Coax Alliance. A networking standard that uses the same coaxial cable as the cable TV signal to create a home network. The XB3 has a MoCA indicator LED on its silkscreen; the specific MoCA chip is unidentified on this board revision.

CVE-2017-5693 — The Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures identifier assigned to the Intel Puma 6 latency defect. Formally issued August 2018 by Intel, acknowledging a periodic high-latency issue under certain traffic conditions. Full advisory: intel.com/content/www/us/en/security-center/advisory/intel-sa-00058.html

CFAA — Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1030. The primary federal cybercrime statute, enacted in 1986 and periodically amended, that criminalizes unauthorized access to computers, data theft, system sabotage, password trafficking, and cyber-extortion. Full text: law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1030

Texas Penal Code § 33.02 — Texas's independently enacted computer breach law. Prohibits accessing a computer, network, or system without the owner's effective consent. Full text: statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.33.htm
