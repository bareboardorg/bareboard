    GoPro

    A camera built to be crashed — and the one American company that tried to fight DJI in the air and got recalled back to the ground. Inside: a sensor, a stabilization brain, and a battery door that is the whole repair story.

    FCC ID

    FCC IDs vary by model. GoPro files under its own grantee code CNF (cameras with Wi-Fi/Bluetooth/GPS); the Karma drone system filed as controller + aircraft + gimbal separately. The filings’ internal photos show the HERO board stack and, on the Karma set, the drone that the CPSC would later recall.

    Every radio a device carries — Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, ultra-wideband, NFC — needs an FCC equipment authorization before the device can be sold in the United States. To get one, the manufacturer files photographs and test reports with the FCC, and those filings are public record. Check the label on your specific unit and look it up at fccid.io.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    • Internal photos — the image-sensor board stacked on the main (GP-chip) board, the battery bay’s contacts, and the antenna placement that lets a camera this small stream a live preview
    • Test reports — Wi-Fi/Bluetooth output; GPS models show the receiver’s filed characteristics
    • The manual — filed publicly, including the battery and door instructions that are the extent of sanctioned owner service

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (withheld under confidentiality requests where the maker files them), firmware, and the full bill of materials.

    Exploded view — the assemblies in build order

    This is the centerpiece of the page: the device taken apart into its assemblies, in the order they go together, every part named in plain language with what it does. An exploded view is the drawing a manufacturer makes showing every part separated along the path it assembles — the single most useful document in repair, and the one most often kept behind a dealer login.

    The source

    Parts book: none. GoPro sells doors, batteries, lenses (protective lens covers), and mounts — the consumable shell — and no internal part at all. FCC internal photos are the source for the stack below. A HERO is a sensor sandwich: lens → sensor board → main board → battery, in a rubberized unibody that is waterproof to 10 m because it is sealed, not because it has serviceable gaskets you can replace.

    Coverage: PARTIAL — no public parts book; FCC internal-photo basis. Owner-serviceable layer is real but shallow: batteries, doors, lens covers.

    Build order

    Step 1 — Housing, lens & doors

    The waterproofing strategy is a sealed body with exactly one opening — the side door — and its gasket is the camera’s life expectancy.

    PartWhat this part does
    Unibody housingRubberized polycarbonate shell, waterproof to 10 m (33 ft) bare on current flagships. The rating dies with any cracked door seal.
    Protective lens coverA twist-off glass cover over the actual lens — the one sacrificial optical part, designed to be replaced after the crash that would otherwise total the camera.
    Side door & gasketCovers USB-C and the battery/SD bay. A torn gasket or grit in the seal is how ‘waterproof’ cameras drown; the door is sold as a part for this reason.
    Mounting fingersThe folding two-prong mount built into the base — the interface to the entire GoPro mount ecosystem.

    Step 2 — Imaging stack

    Sensor on its own board, processor on the main board, joined by a connector that crash forces love to unseat.

    PartWhat this part does
    Image sensor (Sony-class CMOS, 1/1.9″ on recent flagships)The light sensor. Sensor size, not megapixels, is why the footage looks like it does at dusk.
    GP2 processor (HERO10 onward; GP1 in HERO6–9)GoPro’s in-house SoC: image pipeline, HyperSmooth electronic stabilization (gyro-driven horizon leveling + crop), and the encoder. No public datasheet.
    Gyroscope / IMUThe motion sensor whose data is the stabilization: HyperSmooth is computed from this part, not from a gimbal.
    microSD slotThe only storage. Card speed class is a system spec — a slow card presents as a ‘freezing camera’ fault (see Faults).

    Step 3 — Battery & boards

    A removable pack in a sealed camera — the design decision that keeps HEROs repairable at exactly one layer.

    PartWhat this part does
    Enduro battery packA slide-in lithium pack (1720 mAh class). Removable, swappable, sold everywhere — the correct way to build a sealed device, and the reason a HERO outlives an AirPod.
    Main board & radiosWi-Fi/Bluetooth (+ GPS on equipped models) on the main board; antennas at the shell edges per the FCC photos.
    Front & rear displaysTwo LCDs (status front, touch rear) on flex cables — the rear screen is the most-cracked part on the camera and is not sold separately.

    What is missing, in the open: any internal part, any diagram, any calibration procedure for the sensor stack. GoPro’s service model is warranty exchange. The parts it does sell (doors, lens covers, batteries) are exactly the parts its design makes sacrificial — a coherent philosophy, documented here because it is the exception in this wave that proves design intent matters.

    Key chips & components

    • GoPro GP1 / GP2 — in-house camera SoCs; no public datasheet (collection target logged). Their existence is the documented break from the Ambarella parts earlier HEROs used.
    • Image sensors (Sony CMOS) — identified per model from FCC photos and maker specs; sensor datasheets for the exact SKUs are not public. Collection target logged.

    Faults — which part fails, and how you know

    Symptom first. The HERO fault tree is short because the camera is honest about what it is:

    • Freezing / corrupted files — the microSD card, first and almost always: an under-rated or counterfeit card. A card from the maker’s published compatibility list is the fix and the diagnosis in one step.
    • Won’t power on after water exposure — check the door gasket for grit/tears before assuming the board: the seal is the system. Dry the bay fully; a camera that got wet inside is a corrosion clock, not a brick — yet.
    • Overheating shutdowns (static, high-res modes) — documented behavior: a sealed, fanless camera recording 5.3K in still air is a thermal design at its edge. Airflow (the mount on a moving bike is the cooling system) and lower modes are the mitigations; GoPro publishes thermal guidance because this is physics, not defect.
    • GPS never locks / position garbage — the GPS needs sky: first lock after travel or storage can take minutes. A unit that never locks with clear sky has an antenna/board fault.
    • Battery drains in the case, camera warm — a stuck-on radio or a camera that didn’t actually power down (voice control wake is a setting, and a battery cost).

    Safety, before you open anything

    Swollen Enduro packs get the lithium rule: out of the camera, into battery recycling, no exceptions for ‘one more shoot.’ The pack is removable precisely so this decision is easy.

    A HERO that has taken water inside is not made safe by rice or time at the board level — powering a corroding board is how a recoverable camera becomes a dead one. Dry, unpowered, and decide with the board visible or not at all.

    The full record — dated, sourced, nothing smoothed over

    GoPro’s record is a product record: one famous recall, one exit from a market, one patent war with the rival that replaced it.

    DateEventSource
    2016-11-08GoPro recalls the Karma drone (~2,500 units sold) after reports of power loss mid-flight; the CPSC record attributes the failures to a battery that could disconnect — a mechanical latch issue. Karma owners are refunded in full.CPSC Recall No. 17-034 (GoPro Karma)
    2018-01-08GoPro discontinues Karma and exits the drone business entirely, citing a hostile regulatory environment and thin margins — the American challenge to DJI ends after one recalled product.GoPro announcement, Jan. 8, 2018
    2024-03GoPro files a USITC complaint against Arashi Vision (Insta360) alleging its action cameras infringe GoPro patents — the action-camera fight moves to the same forum as the Apple Watch case on this site.USITC complaint, March 2024 (institution of investigation followed)

    Did you know

    • The Karma’s camera gimbal was designed to detach and become a handheld stabilizer — the drone was a vehicle for a camera system, which is why the company survived the recall that killed the drone.
    • GoPro’s stabilization is computed: there is no motor. The gyro’s record of the shake, cropped against in software, is the whole trick — the opposite engineering religion from DJI’s three-motor gimbal.
    • The battery is still removable after a decade of redesigns — proof, from the one company here that ships sealed waterproof hardware, that sealed and swappable are a choice, not a constraint.
