Parts, Not Assemblies

Dealers sell assemblies: the whole wheel, the whole module, the whole unit. Underneath every assembly is a part — a standard, buyable, nameable part. This page names them.

A $6 inner tube became a $300 charge to Medicaid to replace both wheels, with a six-to-eight-week wait. The tube was the part. The wheels were the assembly. That gap is the business model.

Inside the telematics box

The parts that actually wear out

  • Caster bearings — 608RS or 6001RS sealed bearings, about $3–$8 each. Standard industrial bearings, sold everywhere bearings are sold. The size is printed on the old bearing’s seal. Replacing them is Procedure 5.
  • Batteries — sold in matched pairs, by group size. U1, 22NF, Group 34, Group 24. The chair does not care about the dealer’s brand; it cares about size, voltage, and a pair that matches. Testing them is Procedure 3.
  • Inner tubes and tires — sized like bicycle parts, because they are bicycle-class parts. A flat is a tube, not two wheels.
  • Contact cleaner — one can covers the charger socket and every bus connector on the chair (Procedures 2 and 4).

The warranty clock vs. the repair clock

Manufacturers warranty the electronics for a year or two. Medicare will not replace the chair for five. (Table compiled from each maker’s own published warranty insert.) The electronics’ real-world median life — 32.2 months, from 1,268 chairs in the Wheelchair Repair Registry (Kim et al., 2025) — lands in the gap. Knowing which clock covers your failed part is the difference between a warranty repair and a bill.
