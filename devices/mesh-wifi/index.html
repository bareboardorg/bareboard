    Mesh Wi-Fi

    One network name, many radios, and a business model hiding in the wiring closet: mesh systems replaced the router you owned with a fleet you subscribe to. The boards are simple. The record is about the paywall.

    FCC ID

    FCC IDs vary by brand, model, and node. Each mesh node is a full radio system and files separately (Google’s Nest Wifi files under grantee code A4R, the same code as the Nest Mini at A4R-H2C; Eero and TP-Link Deco file under their own codes). Mesh filings are a gift to the reader: to certify multi-node systems, makers file detailed internal photos and RF reports for every node variant.

    Every radio a device carries — Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, ultra-wideband, NFC — needs an FCC equipment authorization before the device can be sold in the United States. To get one, the manufacturer files photographs and test reports with the FCC, and those filings are public record. Check the label on your specific unit and look it up at fccid.io.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    • Internal photos — the antenna array (6–8 antennas on Wi-Fi 6E nodes), the heatsinking (these are passively cooled computers that run hot), and the board’s radio sections under their shields
    • Test reports per band — 2.4 / 5 / 6 GHz output and DFS (radar-sharing) behavior — the paper trail that explains real-world throughput better than the box’s ‘AX5400’ arithmetic (the class number sums theoretical rates no client will ever see)
    • Node variants documented — router vs. point/satellite hardware differences, which marketing renders invisible

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (withheld under confidentiality requests where the maker files them), firmware, and the full bill of materials.

    Exploded view — the assemblies in build order

    This is the centerpiece of the page: the device taken apart into its assemblies, in the order they go together, every part named in plain language with what it does. An exploded view is the drawing a manufacturer makes showing every part separated along the path it assembles — the single most useful document in repair, and the one most often kept behind a dealer login.

    The source

    Parts book: none. No mesh maker sells parts or publishes a diagram. FCC internal photos are the source. A mesh node is a small passively-cooled computer: radio board, big passive heatsink, antennas around the shell, one or two Ethernet ports, and a power supply that is the most common failure in the whole system.

    Coverage: PARTIAL — no public parts book; FCC internal-photo basis.

    Build order

    Step 1 — Shell, antennas & heatsink

    The pretty fabric or plastic tower is an antenna farm wearing a heatsink as a hat.

    PartWhat this part does
    Antenna arrayMultiple printed/flex antennas for each band, arranged around the shell for spatial diversity (MIMO — multiple-input/multiple-output, using several antennas to multiply throughput). Antenna placement is the product; the shell is shaped around it.
    Heatsink & thermal pathFanless nodes dump SoC heat into a metal mass and the case. A node in a closed cabinet or direct sun is a node cooking itself — thermal throttling presents as ‘the Wi-Fi got slow.’
    Status LED & touch/button surfaceThe node’s entire local interface. When the app is the only window, the LED’s blink codes are the fault table.

    Step 2 — Radio board

    One board: SoC, radios, Ethernet, memory.

    PartWhat this part does
    SoC (Qualcomm / Broadcom / MediaTek)The networking processor — the same families that power conventional routers, documented at family level in this site’s Datasheet Index; exact SKU per node from FCC photos.
    Radio chains (2.4 / 5 / 6 GHz)Separate power amplifiers and front ends per band. Tri-band nodes may dedicate one 5 or 6 GHz radio to backhaul (the node-to-node link); dual-band nodes share client radios for backhaul and halve under load.
    Ethernet switch & portsOne WAN + one LAN on most nodes (more on gateway-class models). A node is also a small switch — wiring a node to the gateway (‘ Ethernet backhaul’) removes the wireless hop entirely.
    Flash & RAMSoldered. Firmware and the config live here — and the config’s real home is the maker’s cloud account (see the record).

    Step 3 — Power supply

    The wall adapter is a part of the system, and its most mortal one.

    PartWhat this part does
    AC adapter (USB-C or barrel, model-specific)Node adapters are not interchangeable across generations even within a brand. A weak/substitute adapter presents as random node reboots under load.
    Internal regulatorsBoard-level power conversion of the classes indexed in the Datasheet Index; a node that dies after a storm surge usually dies here.

    What is missing, in the open: everything below the whole-node level. Mesh hardware service is: replace the node, replace the adapter. The repair that actually exists for mesh is architectural — placement, wired backhaul, and knowing which settings your subscription rents you (next sections).

    • The XB3 on this bench is the control case: the Xfinity XB3 gateway shows what a fully-autopsied home-network board looks like when a site does the work — the mesh nodes deserve, and have not yet had, the same treatment.

    Faults — which part fails, and how you know

    Mesh faults are mostly physics and arithmetic wearing app costumes:

    • One node always slow / always red — placement: it is too far, or behind the refrigerator/ductwork/mirror (metal and water are Wi-Fi poison). The fix is moving it a room closer, not a firmware update.
    • Whole network degrades at busy hours — dual-band shared backhaul saturating. Wiring the busiest node (Ethernet backhaul) is the single highest-value mesh repair that exists.
    • Random reboots of one node — its power adapter, heat, in that order. Swap adapters between nodes: if the fault follows the adapter, you have your answer and your cheapest fix.
    • New device ‘can’t see’ the network — band steering: legacy 2.4 GHz-only IoT gear confused by a unified SSID. The fix is a guest/IoT SSID or a setup mode — buried, on some systems, behind the subscription.
    • Settings you set are gone — the config lives in the cloud account; a factory reset or an app migration re-homes it. Know which account your network belongs to before you need it.

    Safety, before you open anything

    Nodes are low-voltage devices; the live risks are mundane and real: heat (don’t seal a passively cooled node in an unventilated box), and the power adapter (replace like-for-like; a bargain adapter that sags under load creates faults that look like firmware bugs).

    The bigger safety surface is administrative: your mesh admin app is the key to every device in the house. A strong account password and 2FA on the network’s account matters as much as any router setting on this page.

    Did you know

    • An Ethernet cable is a mesh upgrade: wired backhaul removes the wireless hop that halves dual-band throughput — the best-performing ‘node’ is a wire.
    • The speed on the box (e.g. AX5400) is a sum across radios and bands that no single device can use — the filed FCC test reports show the per-band reality.
    • Your mesh network’s configuration lives in a cloud account. Selling the house’s router now means transferring an account, not handing over a password card.
