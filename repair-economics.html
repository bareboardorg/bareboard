    Repair economics: why fixing what you have is now the rational move

    For years, the honest advice about a broken graphics card or an old laptop was often “buy a new one — it’s cheaper than the repair.” That advice is out of date. AI data centers have eaten the memory supply, and the prices of new hardware have moved so far that repair is now the economical choice in cases where it was not two years ago. This page lays out the numbers — all of them from November 2026 tracking and reporting — and what they mean for the decision to repair or replace.

    What happened: the “RAMpocalypse”

    AI data-center construction has diverted DRAM and HBM wafer capacity — the memory chips that go into both data-center accelerators and consumer devices — away from consumer memory. JP Morgan’s estimate: DRAM prices up 400% since 2024 by the end of 2026. TrendForce expects DRAM to stay tight through 2027; Intel points to 2028 for normalization. There is no relief in sight this year, and probably not next year either.

    The numbers, November 2026

    • Graphics cards: Tom’s Hardware tracking puts the RTX 5090 street price at about $4,920 to $4,999 against a $1,999 manufacturer’s suggested price — roughly 2.5 times. The RTX 5080 is at $1,899 against $999. NVIDIA removed the 5090 from its U.S. store in October. Chinese RTX 50 prices rose by up to 3,300 yuan (about $500) per card in the first week of November alone.
    • Memory: a 2x16GB DDR5-6000 kit went from $59 in April 2025 to $429 on November 3, 2026 — more than seven times. GDDR6 spot prices went from about $2.50 per gigabyte in September 2025 to $12.45 on October 5, 2026 — about five times.
    • Everything downstream: the Steam Deck OLED went from $549 to $789; the Raspberry Pi 5 16GB rose $80 to $385 on November 2, with the maker citing a “lack of alternative supply for 16GB DRAM”; Xbox, PlayStation 5, and Switch 2 prices have risen too. MSI told investors gaming hardware prices rise 15 to 30% across 2026; AMD confirmed a 10% or larger Radeon price increase effective August 2026. Amazon cancelled GPU orders — Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney said his were among them.

    What this does to the repair-or-replace decision

    Work the arithmetic on a concrete case. A graphics card with a melted 16-pin connector (see our failure analysis) is a soldered-connector replacement at a board-level shop — a repair measured in tens or low hundreds of dollars. The replacement card, at street prices, is $1,899 for a 5080 or about $4,900 for a 5090. The repair was always cheaper; the gap is now so large that the repair is cheaper by an order of magnitude.

    The same logic reaches further down the stack:

    • Fans, paste, and pads — the Level 1 service on our GPU Repair page — cost tens of dollars in parts and an afternoon. They restore a card that would cost thousands to replace.
    • Memory-chip faults that once meant “scrap the card” are now worth a shop’s labor to isolate, because the card itself is worth so much more than the labor.
    • Used cards are worth verifying, not fearing. At these prices, a secondhand card is a serious purchase — which is why our fake-GPU guide matters: the scams exist precisely because the prices make them profitable.
    • Old hardware gains a second life. A machine that was marginal at 2024 prices is worth keeping at 2026 prices. The same logic applies beyond GPUs: a laptop with a failing fan or a dead battery is a repair, not a replacement, when its replacement costs 30% more than it did a year ago.

    The honest limits

    Repair is not always the answer, and the prices do not change the cases where it is not:

    • Some failures are genuinely uneconomical — a dead GPU die, or heat damage spread across the board, can cost more to fix than the card is worth even now.
    • Replacement parts have risen too: memory chips and modules cost more, and a repair that needs new RAM may cost more than the same repair did last year. Get a quote before committing.
    • Prices are volatile. The figures on this page are November 2026 tracking; check current street prices before deciding. The direction of travel — tight supply through 2027 — is the part analysts agree on, not the exact price of any one card next month.

    Why this page is here

    This site’s mission is to put repair knowledge in everyone’s hands, free. For most of its life, that mission fought an economic headwind: new was cheap, repair was dear, and the rational consumer replaced. The memory shortage has reversed the headwind. Repair is now, for a large class of failures, the rational economic choice — not only the principled one. That is a window, and it will not stay open forever: if supply normalizes in 2028, the arithmetic shifts back. While it is open, the knowledge on this site is worth more than it has ever been. Learn it now.
