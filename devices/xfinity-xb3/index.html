    Xfinity XB3 (Arris TG1682G)

    Comcast’s workhorse DOCSIS 3.0 gateway — a 24×8 cable modem, dual-band Wi-Fi router, and telephone adapter in one rented box. Powered by Intel’s controversial Puma 6 chipset. One framing note: “XB3” was Comcast’s tier name for boxes from several makers — this page documents the Arris TG1682G; Cisco’s DPC3941T and a Technicolor model were also sold as XB3s, with different hardware inside.

    FCC ID

    FCC ID: UIDTG1682 (Grantee: UID = Arris Group). This is the single most useful public document set for this device.

    • FCC approval date: May 19, 2014
    • Available: Internal photos, external photos, test reports, user manual
    • Withheld: Schematics, block diagrams, and theory of operation were filed under confidentiality request

    BareBoard teardown: Full autopsy report — chip-by-chip identification, board mapping, and UART analysis.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    What you can typically find:

    • Internal photos — board layout, chip placement, shielding, antenna design
    • Block diagrams — sometimes included, showing the RF chain architecture
    • Test reports — conducted and radiated emissions data, which can reveal operating frequencies and power levels
    • Label placement — where the FCC ID lives on the device

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (usually withheld under confidentiality requests), firmware, bill of materials, or debug interface documentation.

    Specifications

    • SoC: Intel DHCE2652 (Puma 6) — Atom 652 (1 core / 2 threads) 1.2 GHz x86 + ARM1176-class ARMv6 (~450 MHz, DOCSIS side)
    • RAM: 512 MB (2× Samsung K4B2G1646B DDR3)
    • MoCA: 2.0
    • Flash: 128 MB (Spansion S34ML01G200TFI00)
    • DOCSIS: 3.0, 24 downstream × 8 upstream channels
    • Wi-Fi: 2.4 GHz (Qualcomm Atheros AR9381) + 5 GHz (Qualcomm Atheros QCA9880), 802.11ac
    • Ethernet: 4× Gigabit (Broadcom BCM53124S switch)
    • Voice: 2× telephone ports (DSP Group DCX81 DECT/VoIP SoC)
    • USB: 2× USB 2.0 ports
    • Power: TI TPS54328 buck regulator

    Controversies & Legal

    The Puma 6 latency defect

    The Intel Puma 6 chipset at the heart of the XB3 has a documented hardware flaw: under certain traffic patterns (bursts of small TCP/UDP packets), an internal lookup table fills up, causing severe latency spikes and jitter. This ruins online gaming, VoIP calls, and other real-time applications.

    In 2017, a class action lawsuit (Reyna v. ARRIS International plc, Case No. 3:17-cv-01834, N.D. Cal.) was filed over the Arris SURFboard SB6190 — a retail modem using the same Puma 6 chip. The suit alleged Arris knew about the defect but sold the modem as “the fastest and most reliable” anyway. Intel released firmware mitigations, but the underlying hardware limitation remained.

    Note: The class action specifically concerned the SB6190 retail modem, not the TG1682G gateway. However, the Puma 6 latency flaw is a property of the chipset itself and affects all devices built on it, including the XB3.

    Sources: The Register, Class action complaint (PDF)

    Comcast rental fees

    The XB3 is a rented gateway — Comcast charges subscribers a monthly fee (historically around $14–15/month) for the device. Critics have long argued that the cumulative rental cost far exceeds the retail price of an equivalent modem, and that Comcast has made it difficult for customers to use their own equipment for voice service (which requires the telephony features of gateways like the XB3). Specific fee amounts and policies change over time; check Comcast’s current terms.

    Default credentials

    The TG1682G shipped with well-known default admin credentials (admin/password), documented in the FCC filing and widely published. While standard for ISP equipment of this era, default credentials on millions of deployed gateways represent a significant attack surface.

Comcast’s filing with Maine’s Attorney General put the number affected at 35,879,455 people; the data concluded to be involved included usernames and hashed passwords and, for some customers, names, contact information, last four digits of Social Security numbers, dates of birth, and secret questions and answers. The XB3 is rented, not sold: the monthly gateway fee has crept upward over the years (tracked at $14, then $15, per month in recent bills), so a long-held gateway costs its subscriber many times the hardware’s value, and using your own modem for voice service has historically been difficult because voice requires a telephony gateway.     Unverified: “The home hotspot costs you $20–30 a year and slows your own Wi-Fi.” Those figures come from the Grear complaint (above), which cited a Speedify test alleging the second signal would cost a subscriber $20–30 a year in electricity if nobody used it, and 30–40% more power under heavy hotspot use. The cost claim remains an allegation, recorded here as unverified.

    Did you know

    • Puma was Texas Instruments’ chip before it was Intel’s. Intel bought the Puma family with Texas Instruments’ cable modem product line in 2010. The latency flaw’s lineage runs through all three owners — TI, Intel, and (since 2020) MaxLinear. Source: The Register — Puma 6 latency, December 2016.
    • The lag had a rhythm: a stall every couple of seconds. The Register’s reporting, from affected users’ measurements, put the mechanism plainly: every couple of seconds a high-priority maintenance task hogged the Puma 6’s processor, pushing latency up by at least 200 ms and, over time, dropping about six per cent of packets — regular enough to graph, which is how gamers noticed it before any vendor admitted it. Source: The Register — Puma 6 latency, December 2016.
    • Arris’s next gigabit modem quietly switched sides. For its DOCSIS 3.1 SB8200, Arris chose a Broadcom chip (the BCM3390) instead of Intel’s successor Puma 7 — the modem maker at the center of the Puma 6 lawsuit sourced its flagship replacement from Intel’s rival. Source: The Register — the lawsuit, April 2017.
    • Your “modem” is also a two-line telephone system. Inside the TG1682G, alongside the cable modem and Wi-Fi radios, sits a dedicated voice processor (a DSP Group DCX81) driving two telephone jacks, plus MoCA 2.0 networking over the home’s coax wiring — the box was Comcast’s internet, TV-networking, and landline plant in a single rental unit. Source: TechInfoDepot — Arris TG1682G.
    • Puma’s newest descendant abandoned Intel’s architecture entirely. MaxLinear’s Puma 9, announced in 2026, is built on ARM rather than the Intel x86 platform that ran every earlier Puma generation — the design line that began at Texas Instruments ends, architecturally, somewhere completely different. Source: Light Reading — MaxLinear Puma 9, September 2026.
