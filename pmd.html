PMD — Power Mobility Devices

PMD is the official Medicare name for a power wheelchair or a power scooter. This section teaches you how to take back your own chair.

Chair stopped right now? Start here — no tools, no experience: Your Chair Stopped.

Your chair’s settings, its fault logs, and a backup of the fit that took your clinician years to build all sit behind programming tools ordinarily available only through the dealer channel, not by direct sale to owners. The industry’s power is that it holds the knowledge. We publish it, in plain language, free.

Your legs shouldn’t need a dealer’s permission.

The dealer lock

Almost everything that makes your chair yours lives behind that lock:

  • How fast the chair goes, and how hard it launches and stops.
  • Whether your hand tremor is filtered out — or fed straight into the motors.
  • Whether the seat still works after a part is swapped.
  • The fault codes your chair already displays on its own screen — shown to you, explained to no one.

And when a dealer swaps a module, your configuration goes with the old one. The clinical fit built over years reverts to factory defaults, and you were never given a backup. Settings like tremor dampening can vanish silently — and a chair set up for somebody else’s hands can be uncontrollable in yours.

The numbers

  • 63.8% of full-time wheelchair users need at least one repair within six months. (Toro 2016, spinal-cord-injury users, manual and power chairs.)
  • The median repair takes 14 days — and 14% of users who needed repairs got none of them completed at all. (Worobey 2025.)
  • 62% of users in U.S. PIRG’s “Stranded” survey estimated their average repair wait at four weeks or more; 40% said seven weeks or more.
  • A $6 inner tube became a $300 charge to Medicaid to replace both wheels — with a six-to-eight-week wait. (PIRG Colorado storybank.)
  • A chair’s electronics have a median survival of 32.2 months (Kim 2025) — against Medicare’s replacement clock, which will not replace a chair inside its five-year reasonable useful lifetime except for loss, theft, or irreparable damage (42 CFR §414.210(f)). The electronics die, on median, with more than two years left on the clock.

What we publish

  1. Fault codes, translated. The owner-actionable codes for the systems covered here, in plain language: what it means, what to check first, and in what order.
  2. The settings, in plain language. The core dealer-menu settings rewritten for the person who rides the chair — what each one changes, what symptom it fixes, the programmer’s ranges. The few genuinely dangerous ones are flagged, not hidden: for example, seating actuator acceleration/deceleration must stay at 100% on current-sensing endstops, or the actuator destroys itself. The dealer’s safety argument is real for a handful of settings. The lock covers all of them — including just reading and backing up your own.
  3. The cheap fixes. Step-by-step repairs for the failures that strand people: freewheel/brake reset, charger-inhibit pin cleaning, battery-pair load testing, bus-cable contact restoration, caster bearing replacement.
  4. Parts, not assemblies. The service parts covered here, identified and published — what each is, what it does, where to get it — so you buy the $6 tube, not the $300 assembly.

Why the lock can be beaten — our findings

  • There is no cryptography to break. The chair’s bus — the cable network its parts use to talk to each other — is standard CAN — the project’s captured dealer-tool sessions ran at 125 kbps — with no encryption and no cryptographic authentication. The lock was never a cipher. It is a tool they won’t sell you.
  • The gate is the software, not a cipher. The proven gate is the dealer software and its access tiers. The project record contains incompatible accounts of what is inside the manufacturer’s dongle, so this page does not state the dongle’s internal hardware as settled fact.
  • We decoded the configuration protocol. A chair’s configuration moves as data objects, each with a checksum (a number that proves the data arrived unchanged). We read 16 configuration objects straight off the bus in captured dealer-tool sessions. Those reads came from captured dealer-tool sessions. A generic owner adapter that can make the same backup without the dealer tool is not yet proven.

How these findings were made — and how to listen to your own chair the same way — is published as a bench procedure: Reading the Chair.

The owner tool

The owner tool is a goal, not a finished product: a proposed physical adapter that would plug into your chair’s own control bus and connect it to this website. Its intended first job would be to back up your chair’s configuration and show you the backup. Then it would read your fault codes and walk you through the repair for your model, step by step. Reading captured dealer-tool sessions is proven today. Backup through this proposed adapter is not yet proven, and the project’s first bench session with it is pending hardware. Nothing is written to your chair.

The proposed adapter is deliberately dumb hardware. Everything intelligent would live here on the website, so the adapter would not need updating when a new model is supported.

Honest limits

  • Proven today: reading captured dealer-tool sessions and understanding them. Backup through the proposed owner adapter, and the write/restore path, are not proven. The write/restore path is not proven. The shortcut the community assumes — that configuration-file byte offsets equal live bus addresses — is one our own captures contradict, so we do not ship a restore claim. It settles only with a live power module on the bench.
  • The cheap fixes on this site run on a few dollars in parts — the $10 class of failures: lockouts, dirty contacts, worn bearings. Battery pairs, motors, and power modules are real money.
  • Education does not fix Medicare’s replacement clock or suppliers’ repair economics. Those are policy fights. Ours works without permission: the knowledge stops being theirs alone.

The law

  • Colorado got it right. Its powered-wheelchair right-to-repair law (HB22-1031) expressly requires manufacturers to provide the firmware, the program access, and the tools to reset security functions. The only carve-out is trade secrets. Washington, Oregon, and Nevada followed the same pattern.
  • Texas owners are carved out. Texas has no wheelchair right-to-repair law — and its consumer-electronics repair law (HB 2963, effective September 1, 2026) expressly excludes medical devices.
  • The remaining federal gap is the DMCA. Section 1201’s ban on circumvention tools is what still stands between owners and third-party tools, even where state law grants the right to repair.
