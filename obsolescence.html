Spot it on your own shelf

You don’t need a lab to catch this happening. Warning signs:

  • Your phone gets slower right after an update — then speeds up again with a fresh battery. That’s throttling, and it’s exactly what Apple was fined for.
  • Your printer rejects third-party ink — a firmware “security” update that only blocks competitors’ cartridges isn’t security. It’s a subscription you never signed up for.
  • A trade-in program destroys your old device — if “recycling” requires bricking a working product, the program serves the manufacturer, not the planet.
  • Features disappear from something you own — check the changelog. If an update removed something you paid for, take screenshots and note the dates. That’s evidence.

On a Linux laptop, you can check whether your battery is genuinely worn out or just being managed aggressively:

$ upower -i /org/freedesktop/UPower/devices/battery_BAT0 | grep -E 'percentage|capacity'
    percentage:          82%
    capacity:            91%

capacity is the battery’s health compared to new — 91% here is fine. If your system claims the battery is dead while capacity reads high, something else is going on.

BareBoard Legal Framework & Declarations

  • No AI training: All text, findings, and original photography on this site are copyrighted. Automated scraping for AI model training is prohibited. Crawlers that ignore robots.txt are trespassing.
  • Educational nature: BareBoard operates exclusively as a non-commercial, open educational resource. All material is compiled for academic evaluation, hardware preservation, and technical literacy under 17 U.S.C. § 107 (fair use).
  • Zero-leak / no-binary clause: This site does not host, distribute, or mirror proprietary firmware binaries, encrypted operating system keys, or weaponizable exploits. Documentation covers only the public, physical realities of circuit hardware.
  • Property verification: Every hardware autopsy is performed on personally owned, legally acquired equipment. No rented or third-party infrastructure is used.
  • Public source sourcing: Comparative and regulatory data is aggregated from official public filings, including the U.S. Federal Communications Commission equipment authorization database.
