    PlayStation Family

    Five generations of Sony consoles — and some of the biggest security and consumer-rights battles in gaming history.

    FCC ID

    FCC IDs vary by model and revision. Sony’s grantee code is AK8. Check the label on your specific console and look it up at fccid.io — the filings include internal photos that are invaluable for hardware research.

    Specific model-to-FCC-ID mappings are being verified and will be listed here.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    What you can typically find:

    • Internal photos — board layout, chip placement, shielding, antenna design
    • Block diagrams — sometimes included, showing the RF chain architecture
    • Test reports — conducted and radiated emissions data, which can reveal operating frequencies and power levels
    • Label placement — where the FCC ID lives on the device

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (usually withheld under confidentiality requests), firmware, bill of materials, or debug interface documentation.

    What we reveal

    The FCC tells you what a device emits. We tell you what’s inside and how it works.

    • Chip-level identification — every major IC named, with datasheet links where public documentation exists
    • Board mapping — what connects to what, which test pads matter, where the debug interfaces hide
    • What the manufacturer won’t tell you — undocumented features, hidden radios, silent telemetry, repair blockers

    Check our teardowns for full board-level analysis.

    Key Chips

    • Cell Broadband Engine (PS3) — IBM/Sony/Toshiba co-design. 1 PPE + 8 SPEs. Exotic, powerful, notoriously hard to program.
    • Emotion Engine (PS2) — MIPS-based, 294 MHz. Sony’s custom silicon peak.
    • AMD APUs (PS4/PS5) — semi-custom x86-64. The industry converged on PC architecture.

    Controversies & Legal

    The 2011 PlayStation Network hack

    Between April 17–19, 2011, attackers breached Sony’s PlayStation Network and Qriocity services, compromising personal data from approximately 77 million accounts — names, addresses, emails, birth dates, passwords, and payment details. Sony shut down PSN on April 20; the outage lasted 24 days.

    Government officials worldwide criticized Sony’s week-long delay in notifying users. The UK Information Commissioner’s Office fined Sony £250,000 for breaching the Data Protection Act. Sony estimated the breach cost $171 million. A US class action settled with Sony offering up to $15M in compensation (free games, PS Plus time, and up to $2,500 for proven identity theft).

    At the time, it was one of the largest data breaches in history.

    Sources: Wikipedia, Silicon UK

    OtherOS removal (2010–2017)

    The original “fat” PS3 shipped with OtherOS — the ability to install Linux alongside the PlayStation OS. The US Air Force famously built the Condor Cluster, a 1,760-console supercomputer, from PS3s. On April 1, 2010, firmware update 3.21 removed the feature, citing security concerns (a hacker had demonstrated a fundamental exploit).

    A class action (In re Sony PS3 “Other OS” Litigation) was filed in 2010, dismissed in 2011, revived on appeal in 2014, and settled in 2016: $55 for users who proved they ran Linux, $9 for those who bought the PS3 valuing the feature. The initial settlement was rejected by the judge over attorney fees; a revised settlement followed in 2017, paying claimants up to $65.

    Sources: Wikipedia, GamesIndustry.biz

    PS5 repair restrictions

    The PS5 uses software pairing for certain components — notably the optical drive, which is married to the motherboard. Replacing a failed drive requires Sony’s proprietary calibration, limiting independent repair. This is part of a broader industry trend of parts pairing that right-to-repair advocates oppose.

    Specific pairing mechanisms vary by revision; this reflects widely reported teardown findings.

Sony said it strongly disagreed and planned to appeal; it later withdrew the appeal. (Details in Controversies & Legal above.)Computerworld — ICO fine

In July 2014 Sony agreed to a preliminary $15 million settlement of the U.S. class actions over the 2011 breach — free games, themes, and PlayStation Plus time for affected users, plus identity-theft reimbursement — while continuing to deny the allegations; Sony said it settled to avoid the cost of lengthy litigation. (Details in Controversies & Legal above.)Polygon — $15M settlement
    2021“CBOMB”: the PS4’s clock-battery time bomb, documented and defused. Preservation testers documented in 2021 that if a PS4’s internal CMOS battery (the small cell that keeps its clock) dies or is removed and the console cannot reach PSN to re-sync its clock, the console refuses to launch games — disc and digital alike — because the clock check guards the trophy system. That made every PS4 dependent on Sony’s servers staying up forever. Sony never announced a fix, but testers found that firmware 9.00 (September 2021) had quietly removed the block: games now launch with a dead battery, with trophy dates left blank.GameSpot — firmware 9.00 fix
    2021–2023DualSense drift lawsuit, filed and then dropped. Turner v. Sony Interactive Entertainment, filed in February 2021, alleged the PS5’s DualSense controller has a drift defect Sony knew about and did not disclose. The plaintiffs used Sony’s own paper opt-out — a letter sent within 30 days of first using the console — to escape its arbitration clause and demand a jury trial. In October 2023 all of the plaintiffs voluntarily dismissed their claims. No court ever ruled on whether the DualSense is defective.Top Class Actions — dismissal
    2025PSN goes dark for about a day. On February 7–8, 2025, PlayStation Network failed worldwide for roughly 24 hours — the longest PSN outage since 2011 — blocking sign-in, online play, and the PlayStation Store. Sony attributed it only to “an operational issue,” apologized, and automatically gave every PlayStation Plus member five extra days of service. It never published a cause.Tom’s Guide — outage coverage

    Unverified: “Iraq stockpiled 4,000 PlayStation 2s to build a military supercomputer.” Evidence for: in December 2000 a widely repeated report, citing unnamed intelligence sources, claimed as many as 4,000 PS2s had been bought in the U.S. and shipped to Iraq, and said U.S. Customs and the FBI were investigating; the PS2 was briefly subject to Japanese export controls because of its graphics performance. Evidence against: UK intelligence sources dismissed the claim as “nonsense” at the time, ordinary PCs of the era were more capable and easier to network, and no Iraqi PS2 weapons program has ever been confirmed in the public record. Consoles really were clustered into supercomputers — by the U.S. Air Force and a U.S. university (see Did you know) — but the Iraq story itself remains unproven.

    Did you know

    • The U.S. Air Force built a supercomputer out of 1,760 PlayStation 3s. The Condor Cluster, unveiled December 1, 2010, linked 1,760 PS3s with 168 graphics processors for 500 trillion operations per second, cost about $2 million — a tenth to a twentieth of a conventional machine — and used less than a tenth of the power. It ran on the very OtherOS/Linux feature Sony had removed that same year. Source: Air Force Research Laboratory.
    • The PS2 is the best-selling console in history. Sony’s own 30th-anniversary history puts PlayStation 2 sales at more than 160 million units — a record no console, including the Nintendo Switch, has passed. Source: Wikipedia — PlayStation 2.
    • GeoHot donated his leftover defense fund to the EFF. After the 2011 settlement, George Hotz gave the $10,000 left over from donations to his legal defense to the Electronic Frontier Foundation, as he had promised donors he would. Source: The Register — EFF donation.
    • Researchers clustered PS2s years before the Air Force clustered PS3s. A team at the University of Illinois strung 70 PlayStation 2s into a working supercomputer — proof, long before the Iraq rumor could be checked, that game consoles really could do the job. Source: Game Developer — PS3 cluster.
