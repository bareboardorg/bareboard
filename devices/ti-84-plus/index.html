    TI-84 Plus Family

    The graphing calculator that ate American education. Powered by a Zilog Z80 — a CPU from 1976 — and protected by one of the most aggressive legal strategies in consumer electronics.

    FCC ID

    FCC ID: Not verified. Graphing calculators without wireless radios are not intentional radiators and typically do not carry FCC IDs. If you find an FCC ID printed on a TI-84 variant, let us know.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    There is no FCC filing for the TI-84 Plus family to reveal. These calculators carry no wireless radio, so they are not intentional radiators and never went through the FCC’s equipment authorization program: no FCC ID, no internal photos, no RF test reports, no filed manual.

    Compliance for a device like this runs under FCC Part 15 Subpart B — unintentional radiators, placed on the market under the manufacturer’s own declaration rather than an FCC grant. The public record for the TI-84 is Texas Instruments’ own documentation, not a regulator’s file. Everything under “What we reveal” comes from the physical device and TI’s published material.

    Key Chips

    • Zilog Z80 (original Plus) — 8-bit CPU from 1976, clocked at 15 MHz. The same architecture as the Sinclair ZX Spectrum.
    • Zilog eZ80 (CE models) — 24-bit enhanced Z80, clocked at 48 MHz.
    • TI proprietary ASIC — integrates memory controller, display driver, and USB. TI does not publish datasheets for these.

    Controversies & Legal

    The 2009 signing key DMCA battle

    In 2009, hobbyists factored the 512-bit RSA keys TI used to sign calculator firmware, allowing custom operating systems on TI calculators. TI responded with DMCA takedown notices against bloggers and websites — including United-TI, Reddit, and Wikipedia — claiming the keys were circumvention devices.

    The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) intervened pro bono, representing three bloggers. On October 13, 2009, the EFF sent TI a letter warning that publishing the keys did not violate the DMCA and that TI could face liability for misrepresentation. TI continued sending notices briefly but stopped after late 2009 and never pursued legal action.

    The incident became a textbook example of the Streisand Effect — TI’s attempts to suppress the keys ensured their widespread distribution, including on WikiLeaks.

    Sources: Wikipedia, EFF, The Register

    The calculator monopoly

    TI dominates the US graphing calculator market, with the TI-84 series effectively mandatory in many American classrooms. Critics note that the hardware has barely changed in two decades — the original TI-84 Plus used a CPU architecture from 1976 — while prices remained above $100. The College Board and ACT’s approved-calculator lists have been cited as entrenching TI’s position, as schools standardize on TI models to ensure test compliance.

    Exam mode and lockdown

    Newer TI calculators include “exam mode” features and increasingly locked-down firmware, limiting what users can do with hardware they own. This runs directly counter to the right-to-repair and device-ownership principles BareBoard stands for.

    2009The signing-key DMCA fight. In July 2009 hobbyist Benjamin Moody published the factors of the 512-bit RSA key TI used to sign calculator operating systems, and a volunteer distributed-computing project (the RSA Lattice Siever) factored the remaining keys, including the TI-84 Plus’s — making custom operating systems possible on calculators people owned. TI answered with DMCA takedown notices against sites including United-TI, Reddit, and Wikipedia, and against individual bloggers. The Electronic Frontier Foundation took three bloggers’ cases pro bono and wrote to TI on October 13, 2009, warning that posting the keys did not violate the DMCA and that TI risked liability for misrepresentation. TI sent a few more notices, stopped after late 2009, and never sued anyone; the bloggers reposted their material. (Details in Controversies & Legal above.)Wikipedia — signing key controversy
    201493 percent of the market, at a 2004 spec. The Washington Post, drawing on NPD Group figures, reported that TI took 93 percent of U.S. graphing-calculator sales in the year to June 2014 — about 1.6 million calculators — while the TI-84 Plus, launched in 2004 with a 96×64 screen and 24 KB of RAM, still sold for roughly $90–$125. Barclays analyst Blayne Curtis estimated the calculator cost $15–20 to manufacture, implying a profit margin above 50 percent. That cost figure is an analyst’s estimate; TI has never confirmed it.     2020TI removes native programming from the CE. In May 2020, TI’s OS 5.5.x for the TI-84 Plus CE (OS 5.5.5 in the U.S., 5.5.1 in Europe; the same machine is sold in France as the TI-83 Premium CE) removed the ability to run programs written in assembly language or C — the languages of decades of community software — and blocked downgrading to an older OS that still could. TI said the change was made to “prioritize learning for students” and reduce the risk of exam cheating, after a video showed how to bypass test mode on an old OS version (5.2.2). TI-BASIC programs still ran; native code did not.Wikipedia — TI-84 Plus CE series
    2020arTIfiCE: the community restores what TI removed — unofficially. About four months after the OS 5.5 removal, the calculator community released arTIfiCE, an exploit that uses a flaw in one of TI’s own pre-installed apps to run assembly and C programs again on the new OS versions. TI never restored native support itself; it later patched the arTIfiCE flaw in OS 5.8.3, continuing the cat-and-mouse pattern. TI’s official answer to demand for a modern language was a separate model, the TI-84 Plus CE Python (2021), which adds Python while native code stays locked out.Wikipedia — TI-84 Plus CE series

    Unverified: “The TI-84 costs over $100 because exam boards require TI by name.” Evidence for: the College Board and ACT publish approved-calculator lists on which the TI-84 appears, schools standardize lessons and textbooks on TI models, and TI held about 93 percent of U.S. graphing-calculator sales in 2013–14, so families often have no practical alternative. Evidence against: the approved lists also name Casio, HP, and other brands — no board mandates TI — and no court or regulator has found that TI fixed prices or colluded with exam boards. The $15–20 manufacturing-cost figure behind the “price gouging” version of this claim is one analyst’s 2014 estimate, never confirmed by TI or by any audit. The dominance is documented; the mechanism commonly blamed for it is not proven.

    Did you know

    • The original TI-84 Plus’s CPU outlived almost everything. Its Zilog Z80 was launched in July 1976 — it was already 28 years old when the TI-84 Plus shipped in 2004 — and Zilog kept the standalone chip in production until June 2024, a 48-year run. Source: All About Circuits — Z80 discontinued.
    • In France, the TI-84 Plus CE is a TI-83. The same color CE hardware is sold to French students as the TI-83 Premium CE, with a French exam mode — which is why the 2020 programming removal landed first and hardest in France. Source: Wikipedia — TI-84 Plus CE series.
    • The jailbreak that restored assembly hid in a geometry app. arTIfiCE works by exploiting a bug in Cabri Jr, the geometry application TI itself pre-installs on the CE — the locked door was opened with a key TI shipped in the box. Source: Cemetech — arTIfiCE discussion.
    • Volunteers factored TI’s lock with spare computer time. After the first 512-bit signing key fell in 2009, the community set up the RSA Lattice Siever, a BOINC distributed-computing project, and factored the rest of TI’s calculator signing keys on donated home computers. Source: Wikipedia — signing key controversy.
