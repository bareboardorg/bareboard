    “Hello Again”: the attack that breaks your laptop’s PIN lockout

    Your laptop’s short PIN — the four digits you type to unlock Windows Hello, or to unlock BitLocker disk encryption — is protected by a lockout: guess wrong too many times, and the chip refuses to try again. Researchers at Technische Universität Berlin have shown how to defeat that lockout with a small board, a voltage glitch, and physical access to the machine. A 4-digit PIN that should have taken 69 days to guess now takes 40 hours at worst. A 6-digit BitLocker PIN that should have taken 19 years now takes 20 days at worst. There are no reported attacks outside the lab. But the fix is yours to make now: a longer PIN, and a firmware update when your maker ships one.

    First, the pieces, defined

    • TPM (Trusted Platform Module): a security chip (or, on modern machines, a piece of firmware running inside the processor — a firmware TPM, or fTPM) that stores secrets such as the keys that protect your disk encryption, and that counts wrong PIN guesses.
    • Windows Hello: Microsoft’s sign-in system — face, fingerprint, or PIN — that uses the TPM to protect your credentials.
    • BitLocker: Windows’ built-in disk encryption. A BitLocker PIN unlocks the key that decrypts the drive.
    • Lockout (anti-hammering): after a set number of wrong guesses, the TPM refuses further guesses for a long time. This is what makes a 4-digit PIN safe: there are only 10,000 possible 4-digit PINs, so without a lockout an attacker could try them all in minutes. With a working lockout, trying them all takes 69 days.
    • SPI flash: a small memory chip on the motherboard that holds firmware, including the fTPM’s saved state — among other things, its wrong-guess counter.
    • RPMC (replay-protected monotonic counter): a counter stored in the flash chip that only ever goes up, and whose value is protected by a cryptographic signature (an HMAC — a keyed checksum that proves a value is genuine). Its job is to stop rollback: an attacker copying an old saved state of the fTPM back onto the flash to reset the wrong-guess counter to zero.

    How the attack works

    The attack, presented at the ACM Conference on Computer and Communications Security (CCS ’26) in The Hague in November 2026 and covered by The Hacker News on November 18, 2026, is called “Hello Again.” It breaks the RPMC protection in two ways:

    1. The counter is never checked (AMD). AMD’s Secure Processor firmware — the code that runs the fTPM on AMD machines — never verifies that the RPMC counter actually increased. So an attacker can put a small interposer board (a thin circuit board fitted between the processor and the flash chip) in the way, block the counter update, and report success. The fTPM believes the counter moved forward; it did not. The attacker can then replay an old fTPM state with a fresh wrong-guess counter, as many times as they like. This flaw is CVE-2026-43613 (a CVE number is the standard public ID for a security flaw; CVSS 5.1, AMD bulletin AMD-SN-7079).
    2. The counter’s signing key can be stolen (Winbond flash). A brief voltage glitch — a precisely timed dip in the power supplied to the flash chip — makes Winbond RPMC flash leak its HMAC signing key in about a second. With the key, an attacker can forge any counter value they want, so the counter’s signature proves nothing.

    With the lockout defeated, the attacker simply tries PINs until one works. In the lab, a 4-digit Windows Hello PIN was cracked in 57 minutes; the worst case, trying every combination, is 40 hours instead of 69 days. A 6-digit BitLocker PIN was cracked in under 7 minutes in the lab; the worst case is 20 days instead of 19 years.

    What was tested, and what was not

    The researchers tested an HP OmniBook 3 (Ryzen AI 5 330) and a Framework Laptop 13 (Core Ultra 5 125H). AMD has confirmed the flaw and plans firmware fixes for most affected chips between November 2026 and February 2027.

    The physical-access caveat, stated plainly. This is not a remote attack. Nobody can do this to your laptop over the internet. The attacker needs the machine in their hands, needs to open it, and needs to fit hardware to it (or glitch its power supply). If your laptop is stolen, that is exactly the scenario that matters — a thief has physical access. If your laptop never leaves your sight, this attack does not reach you. And no attacks outside the lab have been reported. The point of this page is not panic; it is that the lockout you were relying on is weaker than advertised, so your PIN needs to be longer than you thought.

    What to do

    1. Make your PIN longer — today. This is the fix that works regardless of firmware. A 4-digit PIN has 10,000 possibilities; a 6-digit PIN has a million; an 8-digit PIN has a hundred million. The researchers’ own numbers show the difference: even with the lockout broken, a 6-digit BitLocker PIN takes up to 20 days to brute-force, and longer PINs scale far beyond that. Use at least 8 digits for a BitLocker PIN, and at least 6 for Windows Hello — longer if you will remember it. A passphrase (words, not just digits) is stronger still where the system allows it.
    2. Install the firmware update when it arrives. AMD plans fixes for most affected chips between November 2026 and February 2027. Your laptop maker (HP, Framework, Lenovo, and others) will ship them as BIOS/UEFI updates — install them the way you would any other firmware update. Check your maker’s support page for your exact model rather than assuming you are covered.
    3. Keep treating a stolen laptop as compromised. Physical access has always been the boundary of laptop security: a thief with time and tools can read a drive that is not encrypted, and can now also attack a short PIN faster than before. Full-disk encryption (BitLocker, with a long PIN) plus a long PIN is the honest defense. If a laptop is stolen, change your passwords and treat the data as exposed.

    Why this matters beyond laptops

    The lesson is general, and it is why this page is on a repair site. The TPM’s lockout depended on a counter stored in a separate flash chip, protected by a signature — and the protection failed in two different places at once: the firmware never checked the counter, and the flash chip leaked the signing key. Security that lives in firmware is only as good as the firmware’s handling of its own state, and no software scan or parts list (no SBOM) can catch a flaw like this, because the flaw is in how the platform security processor treats a counter. The people who find these flaws do it the same way repair people work: by reading the hardware, probing the bus between two chips, and testing what the documentation claims. SPI flash, interposer boards, and voltage glitching are bench skills — and they cut both ways.
