The Keys: Programmers, Dongles and Access Tiers

The settings page showed you the numbers that make your chair yours. This page names the tools that read and write those numbers — and the tier system that decides who is allowed to hold them.

Every control system has the same three-part lock: software (the program that talks to the chair), a key (a physical dongle — a small plug that carries the permission — or a card), and tiers (levels of access: the same tool shows more, or fewer, settings depending on which key is plugged in). The software is rarely the secret. The key is.

Provenance: compiled from the manufacturers’ own published material — Curtiss-Wright’s programming support pages and R-net manuals, the Curtis Instruments 1313/1314 programming manual, Dynamic Controls’ LiNX Access documentation and SHARK/DX2 manuals, and Invacare’s MK6i Field Reference Guide (part no. 1141471). Where a fact comes from the owner community instead of a manual, it is not stated here.

Read the table once and the industry makes sense: the tool is never the product. The permission is the product.

Curtiss-Wright — the dongle is the level

For R-net, VR2 and VSI chairs, programming happens two ways. A hand-held programmer — Curtiss-Wright’s PP1A or PP1B — plugs into the chair and works through menus on its own small screen. Or a PC runs the R-net PC Programmer software, and the PC connects to the chair through the charging socket — the same round 3-pin socket the charger uses (Curtiss-Wright’s own support pages describe the programming cable landing in the charging socket of the controller).

The PC software is one program. What it will show you is decided by the dongle. Curtiss-Wright’s support pages distinguish ‘A’ level tools (the dealer level) from ‘B’ level tools (the engineering level, held by chair manufacturers), and state plainly that some parameters — their examples include certain drive and profile settings — can only be changed with a ‘B’ level hand-held or PC programmer. If you do not have ‘B’ access, their answer is to send the controller back to them.

One door is already in your hands: on-board programming. R-net chairs with a colour-screen joystick (CJSM2) carry a programming mode built into the joystick itself (documented in Curtiss-Wright’s On-Board Programming manual, SK78571). It covers a subset — display and user settings, and drive adjustments where the manufacturer has enabled them. Whether that door is open on your chair was decided by whoever programmed it.

Curtis Instruments — four versions of the same tool

Quantum’s Q-Logic 3 electronics are built by Curtis Instruments (PG Drives’ own fault-code listing names it: “Quantum Q-Logic 3 (Curtis Instruments)”). Curtis sells two programming tools that do the same job: the 1313 hand-held programmer and the 1314 PC Programming Station. Its manual states the rule in one breath: both programmers are available in User, Service, Dealer, and OEM versions, and each can perform the actions available at its own level and the levels below it — a User programmer works at User level only, while an OEM programmer has full access.

Nothing about the hardware changes between versions. The version is the permission, sold as a different product at a different price to a different customer.

Dynamic Controls LiNX — the free app and the paid key

LiNX is programmed from the LiNX Access app (iPhone/iPad, or the PC tool). Dynamic Controls gives the app away free. The app is not the tool: it works through a LiNX Access Key, a hardware key that connects to the chair through its XLR (charger-style) port, and Dynamic’s own documentation defines three levels:

  • Default (no key). You can receive a chair’s configuration file (a .lci file — the chair’s whole setup as one file) by email or file transfer, and view its distributor-level configuration and diagnostics, read-only. You cannot connect to a live chair.
  • Distributor — key DLX-HKEY01-A. Connects to a live chair; reads and configures the basic performance parameters. This is the dealer/provider key.
  • Manufacturer — key DLX-HKEY02-A. Connects and configures the high-level parameters. This is the chair-maker key.

Dynamic defines the authorised users in its own listing: original equipment manufacturers and their approved distribution channels — dealers, providers, therapists and service technicians. The owner of the chair is not on the list. Note the one crack in the wall, though: the Default level means a dealer can email you your own .lci file and you can open it, read every setting in it, and keep it forever — free, with no key. Ask for that file by name. It is your backup, and the hub page explains what happens to owners who never got one.

Dynamic Controls DX, DX2 and Shark — the kit with no key in the box

The older Dynamic systems are programmed from a PC running Wizard 5 software, or with the DX Hand Held Programmer (DX-HHP). Dynamic’s own SHARK installation manual lists the programming accessories by part number, and the list is the whole story:

  • Wizard 5 Kit (DWIZ5-KIT) — software, cables and adapter. The manual notes, in parentheses: no dongle.
  • Wizard 5 software only (DWIZ5-SW).
  • Wizard Dongle — OEM version (DWD-OEM, or USB DWD-OEM-U) or Dealer version (DWD-DLR, or USB DWD-DLR-U), sold separately.
  • SHARK Programming Adapter (DK-ADAPT) — the adapter a Shark system needs.

You can buy the software, the cables and the adapter and still program nothing, because the dongle is a separate line item in two tiers — and Dynamic’s DX2 documentation says it in four words: “Access level dongle also required.”

Invacare MK6i — your settings live on a card

Invacare’s MK6i electronics (the TDX chairs) are programmed with the MK6i Easy Remote Programmer, a hand-held unit; Invacare’s Field Reference Guide also documents using the older MK5 programmer on MK6i electronics. What makes this system different is where the programming lives: on a removable memory card. The guide gives the memory card its own section — programs are saved to the card and restored from it.

There are two kinds of card, and the difference is the tier. With a user card, settings can be saved and restored. But Invacare’s guide states that a professional memory card is required in the programmer to view the fault log’s error codes together with their causes and troubleshooting steps. Your chair records what went wrong, dates it, and timestamps it (on the colour MPJ+ joystick) — and the page that explains it is behind the professional card.

The owner’s move on this system is real, though: because the whole setup is a file on a card, a copy of your card is a complete backup of your fit. If your chair is MK6i, ask for that copy before any module is swapped.

What to demand before anyone swaps a module

You may never hold the key. You can still hold the backup. Before a dealer replaces any electronic module on your chair, ask for four things — every one of them is a button-press in the tools above, and none of them costs the dealer anything but minutes:

  1. Your settings, exported, handed to you. The R-net program file, the LiNX .lci file, or a copy of the MK6i memory card — whichever your system uses. This is the fit your clinician built, in a file you own.
  2. The fault log, read before it is cleared. Codes and history first; erasing them destroys the evidence of what actually failed.
  3. Your settings restored after the swap — loaded from your backup, not factory defaults — and verified with a ride before the technician leaves. A swapped module at factory settings is how tremor dampening and torque silently vanish (the settings page names what to check).
  4. The old module’s settings read before it goes back. If the replacement arrives wrong, the old module may be the only other copy of your fit.
