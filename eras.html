The Five Eras

How hardware went from open to sealed — and what we do about it. Hardware history moves in decades: each one redraws what a repairer can see, probe, and document.

Eras are a lens, not a cage. Every entry on BareBoard carries three facets — Era (this page), Domain (medical, civic, infrastructure, transport, financial, consumer, industrial), and Status (public / public-research / sealed / excluded — the Registry). And one rule stands above all of them: an era never makes a sealed document fair game.

📂 The 1980s — The Open-Silicon & Blueprint Era (1980–1989)

What changed: chips shipped with full public datasheets; computers came with schematics in the box (the IBM PC Technical Reference included complete circuit diagrams); repair manuals were public; parts were through-hole and probe-able.

What it means for repair: repair was normal, expected, documented. The golden age.

What BareBoard does: preserves this documentation before it vanishes. The Library's first shelf exists because of this era.

📂 The 1990s — The Obfuscation & Encapsulation Era (1990–1999)

What changed: surface-mount components, multi-layer boards, PCI configuration spaces; game consoles add security chips; firmware becomes a replaceable blob; service manuals stop shipping with the product and go internal.

What it means for repair: the schematic stops coming in the box. Repair becomes a specialty instead of a given.

What BareBoard does: autopsies the transition — the last devices you could fully understand, and the first you couldn't.

📂 The 2000s — The Hyper-Integration & Mobile Dawn Era (2000–2009)

What changed: whole boards collapse into system-on-chips; baseband processors appear in phones; SSD controllers seal their flash translation layers; probing live signals needs real (and expensive) gear.

What it means for repair: the "chip" you could read a datasheet for is now a city you can't map.

What BareBoard does: the Watchmen board — audit the tools that can still see inside, and trust only the open ones.

📂 The 2010s — The Multi-Ring & SoC Era (2010–2019)

What changed: hidden management cores run beneath the OS (Intel CSME, AMD PSP, Apple's Secure Enclave); hardware parts-pairing locks components to boards; the sealed register map becomes a business model.

What it means for repair: documentation isn't just missing — it's deliberately withheld. The Registry's 🟠 column is mostly this decade.

What BareBoard does: compiles original references from public research (the SEP, PSP, and CSME manuals in the Library). We document the phenomenon honestly. We never chase the sealed documents.

📂 The 2020s — The Multi-Die & Security Mesh Era (2020–today)

What changed: chiplets and interposers, security modules welded to boards, real-time sensor silicon (e.g. the Apple Vision Pro's R1), anti-circumvention design as a default.

What it means for repair: the bench needs better tools — and audited ones.

What BareBoard does: the Most Wanted board lives here. Nominate a device, autopsy a retired unit, publish verified findings.
