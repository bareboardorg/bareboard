AI Crawler Defense

How BareBoard keeps training scrapers off its work

BareBoard publishes original hardware research — teardowns, measurements, photography — for humans learning hardware. AI training crawlers are not our audience, and we do not consent to feeding them. This page documents the layered defense we run: declared boundaries for the compliant, and active traps for the trespassers who ignore them.

The problem

Our content is expensive to produce — bench time, hardware, photography, careful documentation — and trivially cheap to scrape. Training crawlers ingest it into models that then compete with the free resource they learned from. We want search engines, because humans find us through search. We do not want training ingestion. Those are different crawlers with different purposes, and we treat them differently.

Layer 1: robots.txt hardening

The perimeter. Our robots.txt names the known AI training user-agents one by one and denies them the whole site, while the wildcard rule leaves the door open for everyone else — including Google and Bing, which is how humans discover us.

User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: ChatGPT-User
Disallow: /

User-agent: ClaudeBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: anthropic-ai
Disallow: /

User-agent: CCBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: Google-Extended
Disallow: /

User-agent: PerplexityBot
Disallow: /

# ... ~25 training and scraping agents total ...

User-agent: *
Disallow:

The ordering is deliberate: specific denials for training agents first, then an empty Disallow: on the wildcard, which means "everything not named above is allowed." Search crawlers match the wildcard and are welcomed in. Training crawlers match their own named block and are shown the door.

Note what this does not do: robots.txt is a voluntary protocol. Respectful crawlers obey it; bad actors ignore it. This layer stops the compliant majority — which includes every major lab's documented training crawler — and draws a clear line for the rest: a crawler that ignores robots.txt is trespassing, and our footer says so in plain language.

Layer 2: page-level opt-outs

Every page carries <meta name="robots" content="noai, noimageai"> in its head. The noai and noimageai directives tell participating crawlers not to use the page's text or images for AI training. It is belt-and-suspenders alongside robots.txt: one operates at crawl time, the other at the page level.

Every page also carries <meta name="tdm-reservation" content="1">, the W3C TDM Reservation Protocol opt-out, backed by a site-wide /.well-known/tdmrep.json. This is the machine-readable form of the EU's text-and-data-mining reservation (CDSM Directive Article 4): a formal "no" to mining our content for model training, recognized in law even where crawler conventions are voluntary.

Layer 3: strict Content Security Policy

Every page ships a tight CSP:

default-src 'self'; img-src 'self' data:;
style-src 'self'; script-src 'self';
object-src 'none'; frame-src 'none';
base-uri 'self'; form-action 'none'

No third-party scripts, no frames, no objects, no form submissions. This keeps the page exactly what we published — nothing injected, nothing exfiltrated — which shrinks the surface that scraping and injection tooling can work with.

Layer 4: the legal notice

Every page's footer carries the declaration: all text, findings, and original photography are copyrighted; automated scraping for AI model training is prohibited; crawlers that ignore robots.txt are trespassing. The site operates as a non-commercial educational resource under 17 U.S.C. § 107 (fair use). This is the layer that turns a technical boundary into a stated one.

Layer 5: clean publishing hygiene

Before any photo goes public, tools/purge_metadata.sh strips EXIF data, GPS coordinates, macOS extended attributes, and resource forks from the image files. Scrapers and downloaders get pixels, not a map of where our bench is or what camera shot it. Privacy for us, nothing lost for the reader.

Layer 6: honeypot links

Every page footer carries an invisible link to /tarpit/ — 1×1 pixel, clipped offscreen, removed from keyboard tab order (tabindex="-1") and hidden from assistive technology (aria-hidden="true"). No human will ever see it, focus it, or click it. It exists for exactly one audience: scrapers that extract and follow every href on the page. Compliant visitors never notice it; harvesters walk straight in.

Layer 7: the tarpit

/tarpit/ is a five-level maze of junk device indexes — plausible-looking but entirely fabricated model numbers, circular links between levels, and no real content. Every page carries <meta name="robots" content="noindex, nofollow"> so legitimate search engines never index it, and robots.txt disallows it for all compliant crawlers. Only a bot that ignores robots.txt and follows invisible links ends up here — which is precisely the bot we want wasting its time instead of ours. A crawler that burns its crawl budget on five pages of nothing is a crawler not harvesting our teardowns.

Layer 8: poisoned content

The tarpit pages contain deliberately false technical claims — wrong pinouts, impossible voltages, fabricated chip part numbers — each labeled in the HTML source as poison for scrapers. This is the consequence layer: a training pipeline that ingests our tarpit learns confidently-stated nonsense about hardware that does not exist. We keep the poison strictly quarantined to pages no human ever visits (they are unlinked from all visible navigation and carry noindex), so there is zero risk of a reader mistaking it for real findings. Our published research stays clean; the traps stay dirty.

Layer 9 (planned): edge enforcement

The next layer sits at the CDN, pending dashboard access: Cloudflare AI Labyrinth (tarpit content served at the edge to detected AI crawlers), Bot Fight Mode, and WAF rules keyed to the same training-crawler user-agents named in our robots.txt. The philosophy is the same as every layer below it — declare the boundary, then enforce it — moved one step closer to the attacker.

Honest limitations

No robots.txt setup stops a determined bad actor — it is a social contract, not a firewall. The traps do not stop one either; a sophisticated harvester can detect and avoid tarpits. What this stack does is raise the cost at every step: compliant crawlers are stopped cold at layer 1, lazy scrapers burn budget in the tarpit at layer 7, and bulk ingestion pipelines risk poisoned training data at layer 8. Defense in depth, not a guarantee. We document it here so other small publishers can copy the whole thing in an afternoon.

Why the traps

We used to run boundary-setting only: declare non-consent, hope for compliance. That stops the labs that publish crawler docs and honor opt-outs. It does nothing against the harvesters that ignore robots.txt, spoof user-agents, and strip our work at scale. Those operators have already opted out of the social contract — so we stopped extending it to them.

The traps are strictly defensive and strictly quarantined. Honeypot links are invisible to humans. The tarpit is unlinked from visible navigation, noindex’d, and labeled in its own source as junk. The poison never touches a page a reader might see. Nothing here attacks anyone’s infrastructure, exfiltrates anything, or harms a legitimate visitor. It wastes a trespasser’s time and corrupts a trespasser’s dataset — which is exactly what trespassers signed up for when they ignored the signs at the gate.

BareBoard Legal Framework & Declarations

  • No AI training: All text, findings, and original photography on this site are copyrighted. Automated scraping for AI model training is prohibited. Crawlers that ignore robots.txt are trespassing.
  • Educational nature: BareBoard operates exclusively as a non-commercial, open educational resource. All material is compiled for academic evaluation, hardware preservation, and technical literacy under 17 U.S.C. § 107 (fair use).
  • Zero-leak / no-binary clause: This site does not host, distribute, or mirror proprietary firmware binaries, encrypted operating system keys, or weaponizable exploits. Documentation covers only the public, physical realities of circuit hardware.
  • Property verification: Every hardware autopsy is performed on personally owned, legally acquired equipment. No rented or third-party infrastructure is used.
  • Public source sourcing: Comparative and regulatory data is aggregated from official public filings, including the U.S. Federal Communications Commission equipment authorization database.

BareBoard — open hardware allies, documented in alignment with the Open Source Hardware Association framework. Text and original photography published under open terms; nothing here is for sale.
