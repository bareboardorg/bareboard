    Spot a fake or stripped GPU before you pay

    Graphics cards cost more than ever — an RTX 5090 sells on the street for around $4,900 against a $1,999 manufacturer’s price — and where the price goes, the fakes follow. In November 2026, buyers reported receiving empty shells, cards with their chips harvested out, and a “broken RTX 3090” that turned out to contain a decade-old Radeon board sawed in half. This page is the inspection guide: the tells that give a fake away, and the in-person test that catches what your eyes cannot. It builds on the counterfeit record in our GPU Repair hub; everything here is new evidence from the November 2026 wave.

    Why now

    Three things collided. First, the memory shortage (see our repair-economics page) pushed street prices to 1.6 to 2.5 times the manufacturer’s price, so a single card is worth thousands. Second, AI-server operators have been harvesting GPU chips and memory off boards, leaving “corpse cards” — boards with the valuable silicon removed — that get sold on as faulty or for repair. Third, the secondhand market runs on trust and photos, and a photo cannot show you what is inside the shroud. Engadget published a full “how to spot a fake GPU” guide on November 10, 2026, after buyers reported receiving pasta and rice, photos of GPUs, and empty shells instead of cards.

    The sawed Radeon inside a 3090

    The clearest case of the month came from repair creator Blighted Agent, who bought a “$50 broken RTX 3090 Founders Edition” on Facebook Marketplace and opened it to find a decade-old Gigabyte Radeon R9 280 circuit board, physically sawed in half, wedged inside the NVIDIA frame. The tells, in order of how easy they are to spot:

    • The NVLink cover was glued shut. NVLink is the bridge connector on top of high-end NVIDIA cards. On a real 3090 Founders Edition it is a proper fitted cover. On this fake it had been glued shut — because there was nothing underneath it to show.
    • Hollow display and power bays. The display output sockets and the power connector area were empty shells: no circuit board behind them, nothing to plug into.
    • The solder mask gave it away. Solder mask is the colored coating on a circuit board, usually printed with the maker’s name and model. The board inside read “GIGABYTE GV-R928XOC-3GD REV: 2.0” in glossy print — a Radeon R9 280, a card from 2013, not a GeForce RTX 3090 from 2020. Any board whose printed model does not match the card being sold is a fake, full stop.
    • The weight was wrong. A real 3090 Founders Edition is a heavy card — a large heatsink, a full board, memory chips all around the die. A hollow shell with half a donor board inside weighs noticeably less. If a card feels light for its size, open it or walk away.

    The stripped “corpse cards”

    A second pattern is harder to spot and more expensive. Russian repair specialist Vik-On reports counterfeit RTX 4090 “corpse cards” sold as faulty or for repair, with fake GPU cores and fake memory chips soldered on — convincing enough that even a professional inspecting the die by eye may not spot it. In September 2026, Tom’s Hardware reported two German buyers who lost about $5,000 on RTX 5090s whose GPU cores and memory had been physically removed. An Instagram reel of an empty INNO3D RTX 5090 shell drew 15,000 likes on November 3, 2026.

    The story sellers use is always the same: “sold as faulty, for repair.” That listing type is the cover. A genuine faulty card still has its chips on it; a harvested card never works again, and no repair can fix a missing die. Treat every “for repair” listing of a high-end card as a harvested card until proven otherwise.

    The tells, before you pay

    1. Read the board, not the shroud. The plastic cover and the backplate are the easiest parts to fake or swap. Ask for a photo of the bare board, or open the card yourself before paying. Check the printed model number on the solder mask against the card being sold, and check that the GPU die markings match the model.
    2. Check the NVLink cover and the ports. A glued NVLink cover, ports with nothing behind them, or a power socket with no board under it are immediate walk-away signs.
    3. Weigh it in your hand. Compare against the manufacturer’s stated weight for the model. A card that is much lighter than it should be is hollow or harvested.
    4. Look for the older tells too. From the earlier counterfeit record: screws showing tool wear on a “new” card, broken or missing seals, and memory chips whose printing looks oddly uniform or wrong for the model — the painted GDDR memory scam of 2023, where ex-mining cards were sold as new after their memory chips were painted to hide heat yellowing. Relabeled cards — RTX 3080 and 3090 boards re-marked and sold as RTX 4090s — were documented in 2025.
    5. Price is a tell. A 5090 at a fraction of the $4,900 street price is not a bargain; it is a warning. Scammers price just low enough to make you hurry.

    The in-person test procedure

    Never buy a used high-end card sight unseen if you can avoid it. If you can test in person, do this, in order, before money changes hands:

    1. Buy through a channel with returns, and test immediately, inside the return window. This is the single most important rule. A seller who refuses a return window is telling you something.
    2. Run GPU-Z (free, from TechPowerUp). Since version 2.12 it flags a known class of counterfeits — old chips whose BIOS lies about what they are — with a [FAKE] tag. It does not catch everything: it will not catch a harvested board or every re-marked die. So also compare the die name, memory type, and memory size it reports against the specification for your exact model.
    3. Run a sustained load test. A benchmark or game at full load for at least 15 minutes, watching for visual artifacts (wrong pixels, repeating patterns, garbage textures), crashes, and abnormal temperatures. A harvested or re-marked card fails here if it produces a picture at all.
    4. If the card produces no picture at all, stop and return it. “It just needs a driver” is not an explanation for a card with no output. Harvested boards exist, and the return window is shorter than the argument.
    5. Check the 16-pin connector while you are there. A melted or discolored power socket is a separate failure — see our 16-pin failure analysis — and a card sold “for repair” with a burnt connector may be a melted card being passed on.

    What about the rest of the market?

    The scam wave is not only GPUs. How-To Geek’s November 20, 2026 Black Friday scam roundup lists RTX 5090 bricks, fake Ryzen CPUs (an AMD heatspreader fitted to an old Intel CPU), DDR2 memory sold in DDR5 kits, and counterfeit Samsung 990 Pro SSDs. The defense is the same in every case: buy through a channel with returns, verify the part against its specification, and test it before the return window closes. A deal that cannot survive a test is not a deal.
