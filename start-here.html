Start Here

Never opened a device? Never typed a command? Perfect — this page was written for you. You will not open, break, or damage anything. By the end, you will have updated the software on a tiny computer with your own hands — and you will understand what is inside your gadgets.

What is this site?

BareBoard is a free school for hardware. Every device you own — your phone, your smart plug, your game console — is a small computer. The companies that sold them to you would prefer you never look inside. We disagree.

Here is the principle this site is built on: this is your device. You have the right to update it, fix it, and remove software you don’t want on it. When a manufacturer stops updating your gadget, or bundles in software you never asked for, you should be able to do something about it — because it is yours.

On this page, everything is non-destructive: no enclosures opened, nothing pried apart, nothing broken. You will learn what firmware is, how to replace it on hardware designed to let you, and how to talk to a device over a cable. Everything here is about hardware you own, documented for learning and repair. Nothing here is for sale, and nothing here is about anyone else’s property.

You do not need to be an engineer. You do not need to know how to code. You just need curiosity and about fifteen dollars.

What do I need to buy?

Everything on this list is cheap, and most of it you will use for years. Total cost to get started: under $15.

Item 1 — about $4

Raspberry Pi Pico

A tiny computer on a circuit board — and your first project subject. It is designed to be reprogrammed over USB with no tools at all. Buy the plain Pico (not the Pico W) to start — it is the cheapest.

Item 2 — about $3

USB-to-serial adapter

A translator between your computer and gadgets: one end plugs into USB, the other end speaks the simple serial language that device firmware understands. Search for CH340 USB to TTL or CP2102 USB to TTL. Either works. Make sure it has a 3.3V / 5V switch and set it to 3.3V.

Item 3 — you probably own one

Micro USB cable

The small, trapezoid-shaped plug — the kind older Android phones used. It carries both power and data. If you have a drawer of old cables, you already own this. Make sure it is a data cable, not a charge-only cable (charge-only cables have no data wires inside).

Item 4 — about $5, for later

Small screwdriver set

You will not need this today — nothing on this page requires opening anything. But when you are ready for the Repair Guides, gadgets use tiny screws, and a precision bit set (Phillips, Torx, pentalobe, tri-point) will last you years.

Item 5 — about $6, your second patient

ESP32 development board

The chip inside most smart plugs, bulbs, and sensors — on a board you can reprogram over USB, with no mains voltage and nothing to open. Once you finish the Pico project below, this is your next step: the same skills, applied to the chip that runs the smart home.

Your first project: update the firmware on your Pico

Goal: replace the software on your Raspberry Pi Pico with MicroPython, then talk to it. No screwdriver. No opened enclosures. Nothing gets broken. About 30 minutes.

Why this matters: manufacturers update — or abandon — the software on your devices all the time. Here you will do it yourself, on hardware you own, and see exactly what “firmware” means. If software you don’t want ever ships on your device, this is the skill that lets you remove it.

Step 0 — What is a terminal?

A terminal is a window where you type text commands instead of clicking icons. It looks old-fashioned, but it is the most direct way to talk to hardware.

  • Windows: press the Windows key, type PowerShell, press Enter.
  • Mac: press Cmd + Space, type Terminal, press Enter.
  • Linux: press Ctrl + Alt + T.

A window opens with a blinking cursor. That cursor is waiting for you. Everything in the gray boxes below is typed there — lines starting with $ are what you type, and the lines after are what the computer prints back.

Step 1 — Put the Pico in bootloader mode

Hold down the small white BOOTSEL button on the Pico, and while holding it, plug the micro USB cable into the Pico and your computer. Then let go of the button.

A new drive appears on your computer called RPI-RP2 — the Pico is pretending to be a USB flash drive. That is the bootloader: a tiny built-in program whose only job is to accept new firmware. You have just met the first piece of software on the chip, and you didn’t open anything to reach it.

Step 2 — Install MicroPython (this is a firmware update)

Go to micropython.org/download/rp2-pico in your browser and download the latest .uf2 file. Drag it onto the RPI-RP2 drive — the same way you’d copy a photo onto a USB stick.

The drive disappears and the Pico reboots. Congratulations: you just updated firmware. The old software is gone, replaced by MicroPython — your choice, on your hardware. If a manufacturer ever ships software you don’t want, this is the same class of operation that removes it.

Step 3 — Find the Pico’s serial port

MicroPython is now running, and the Pico is listening on a serial port. Ask your computer where it is:

$ ls /dev/ttyACM*
/dev/ttyACM0

/dev/ttyACM0 appearing means your computer sees the Pico. (On a Mac, look for /dev/tty.usbmodem*. On Windows, open Device Manager, look under “Ports” for a COM number like COM4, and use PuTTY instead of the next command.)

Step 4 — Talk to it

Open a serial window onto the Pico:

$ screen /dev/ttyACM0 115200
MicroPython v1.24.0 on 2024-10-25; Raspberry Pi Pico with RP2040
Type "help()" for more information.
>>>

screen opens the window; 115200 is the speed (baud rate). If you see gibberish, try unplugging and replugging the Pico. (To quit screen later: hold Ctrl, press A, then press K.)

The >>> prompt is MicroPython waiting for instructions. Type these lines one at a time and press Enter after each:

>>> from machine import Pin
>>> led = Pin(25, Pin.OUT)
>>> led.on()

The tiny green LED on the Pico lights up. You just controlled hardware with three lines of typed text. Try led.off() to turn it off, and led.toggle() to flip it.

Now ask the Pico what software it is running — this is how you check firmware on any device:

>>> import sys
>>> sys.implementation
(name='micropython', version=(1, 24, 0), mpy=6543)

That version string is the firmware identifying itself. When you want to know whether your devices are running current — or trustworthy — software, this is the question you ask.

Step 5 — What you just learned

  • Bootloader: the BOOTSEL button woke a built-in program that accepts new firmware. Every smart device has one.
  • Firmware: the MicroPython file you dragged over is firmware — the device’s entire operating personality in one file, replaceable by you.
  • Flashing: installing firmware is called flashing. You did it by drag-and-drop; professionals use cables, but it is the same operation.
  • Serial terminal: the >>> prompt is a direct line to the chip. This skill works on routers, cameras, and thousands of other devices.

You updated a device, verified what it runs, and controlled its hardware — with $4 of parts and no disassembly. That is the whole game. Everything else on this site is the same ideas, applied to bigger devices.

Glossary

Every field has jargon. Here is ours, in one sentence each.

Firmware
The software stored inside a device that tells its chip what to do — replaceable, if you know how.
Microcontroller
A tiny, complete computer on one chip — processor, memory, and inputs/outputs together. The Pico’s RP2040 is one.
SoC (System on a Chip)
A microcontroller’s bigger sibling: a whole computer system — CPU, graphics, Wi-Fi — on a single chip, like in your phone.
PCB (Printed Circuit Board)
The flat board that chips and components are soldered onto.
Bootloader
The first program that runs when a device powers on — it can also accept new firmware, like the Pico’s BOOTSEL mode.
Flashing
Installing firmware onto a device — what you did by dragging the .uf2 file onto the Pico.
Flash memory
The chip where firmware is stored. It keeps its contents when power is off, and it can be rewritten.
UART / serial
A simple two-wire conversation between chips — one wire talks, one listens. The “direct line” to a device’s firmware.
TX / RX
Transmit and Receive — the two wires of a serial conversation. TX on one side connects to RX on the other.
GND (Ground)
The shared electrical reference point. Both sides of a serial connection must share ground, or nothing makes sense.
Baud rate
How fast the serial conversation goes, in bits per second. 115200 is the most common speed.
Terminal
A text window where you type commands directly to your computer instead of clicking icons.
REPL
“Read, Evaluate, Print, Loop” — the >>> prompt that runs each line you type immediately. A conversation with the chip.
Datasheet
The manufacturer’s official manual for a chip — what every pin does, how to power it, how to talk to it.
Teardown
Opening a device to document what is inside: which chips, how it is built, how hard it is to repair.
FCC ID
A code the U.S. government requires on wireless devices. You can look it up to find official test photos of a device’s insides — without opening it yourself.

A note on staying safe and legal

Nothing here bypasses copy protection or anyone else’s security, and nothing here is intended to.
