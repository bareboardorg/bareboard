    Battery fire warning — read this first. The e-bike battery is the most dangerous energy store most people own: hundreds of lithium cells, often from the least-regulated corner of the industry, charged overnight in apartments. The FDNY counted 268 lithium-ion battery fires and 18 deaths in New York City in 2023 alone. Never charge a damaged, swollen, or water-exposed pack; never charge an uncertified pack unattended; and if a pack hisses, smells sweet/chemical, or gets hot standing still, get it outside and away from anything that burns — and call the fire department, not a repair forum.

    E-Bike Controllers

    Pedal, sensor, controller, motor — and between you and the battery, one small box of MOSFETs deciding how much of a 52-volt pack becomes motion. The controller is where an e-bike’s manners, limits, and locks live. It is also the most documented part of the machine, because hobbyists got here first and one flagship design is completely open.

    FCC ID

    FCC IDs apply to the connected layers, rarely the controller itself. A plain controller is not a radio and has no filing. Connected e-bike systems (Bosch’s smart system, app-linked displays) file their radios under the makers’ grantee codes. The controller’s public record is instead: the UL 2849 system standard, the CPSC’s warnings and recalls, and — for the open designs — published schematics. This page follows those sources.

    Every radio a device carries — Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, ultra-wideband, NFC — needs an FCC equipment authorization before the device can be sold in the United States. To get one, the manufacturer files photographs and test reports with the FCC, and those filings are public record. Check the label on your specific unit and look it up at fccid.io.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    • Published schematics (open designs) — the VESC project publishes full controller schematics and firmware: the only complete parts-level documentation in this category, and the reference for the stack below
    • UL 2849 / CPSC records — the system-level safety standard (electrical + fire) and the regulator’s battery-warning file: the sources for Safety
    • Maker documentation (varies wildly) — Bafang’s mid-drive ecosystem is documented by a decade of public configuration practice; Bosch’s diagnostic layer is dealer-only (see the record)

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (withheld under confidentiality requests where the maker files them), firmware, and the full bill of materials.

    Exploded view — the assemblies in build order

    This is the centerpiece of the page: the device taken apart into its assemblies, in the order they go together, every part named in plain language with what it does. An exploded view is the drawing a manufacturer makes showing every part separated along the path it assembles — the single most useful document in repair, and the one most often kept behind a dealer login.

    The source

    Parts book: none from the volume makers. Complete schematics: VESC (Benjamin Vedder’s open-source motor controller project) — board designs, firmware, and the configuration tool are public, and VESC-based controllers are sold by multiple vendors for e-bikes, boards, and robotics. The stack below is the anatomy every brushless e-bike drive shares; the open design is cited because it is the one you can verify line by line.

    Coverage: FULL for the open design (VESC: published schematics + firmware); PARTIAL for sealed OEM controllers (Bosch/Shimano/Yamaha/Bafang integrated units) — no public parts book. Shared architecture below is standard brushless-drive engineering.

    Build order

    Step 1 — Sensors & inputs — how the controller knows you pedaled

    An e-bike’s legality and feel are decided here: what counts as ‘pedaling,’ and how much help follows.

    PartWhat this part does
    Torque sensor (mid-drives & quality hub systems)Measures how hard you push the pedals and meters assistance proportionally — the part that makes a good e-bike feel like legs, amplified. Lives in the bottom bracket (mid-drive) or rear dropout.
    Cadence sensor (budget systems)A magnet ring + pickup counting pedal revolutions: assistance as an on/off suggestion. The price-tier tell.
    Throttle (Class 2)A twist/trigger input asking for power without pedaling — legal class is defined by whether this exists and how fast it goes (Classes, below).
    Speed sensor & brake cutoffsA spoke magnet tells the controller the wheel’s speed (the 20/28 mph assist cutoff is enforced from this signal); brake-lever switches cut motor power instantly — a safety input, wired in series with everything.

    Step 2 — The controller — inverter in a finned box

    The controller is a three-phase inverter: it chops battery DC into rotating AC for the brushless motor, hundreds of times per second, under firmware’s command.

    PartWhat this part does
    MCU + firmwareRuns field-oriented control (FOC) on good units (quiet, efficient, sinusoidal) or square-wave commutation on cheap ones (the audible whine and harshness are this choice). Firmware holds the current limits, speed limits, and class behavior — which is why ‘tuning’ is a software act (see the record).
    MOSFET power stageThe switching transistors — the part that burns. Undersized or badly cooled MOSFETs are the #1 controller death: the failure smells of hot electronics and presents as a dead or stuttering motor (Faults).
    Capacitors & current shuntBulk capacitors smooth the battery’s delivery; a shunt resistor measures current so firmware can enforce its limits. In VESC’s published design, every one of these is a named, sourced part.
    DC-DC / 5 V rail & display linkPowers sensors, display, and lights from the pack; the display’s UART/CAN chatter with the controller is the system’s nervous system — and, on Bafang’s open UART ecosystem, an owner-accessible configuration port (below).

    Step 3 — Motor & battery — the endpoints

    The controller’s two dance partners, each with its own protection brain.

    PartWhat this part does
    Brushless motor — hub or mid-driveHub motors package the motor (often with planetary gears) in the wheel; mid-drives (Bafang BBS series, Bosch, Shimano) drive the cranks and use the bike’s gears. Hall sensors inside report rotor position to the controller — a failed Hall is a classic stutter/no-start fault.
    Battery pack + BMS36/48/52 V packs of 18650/21700 cells under a Battery Management System that balances cells and cuts off over-current/over-temperature. The BMS is the pack’s only conscience: the fire record in Safety is overwhelmingly a story of cheap packs, cheap chargers, and damaged cells — not of the controller.

    What is missing, in the open: Bosch, Shimano, and Yamaha publish nothing at this level and route diagnosis through dealer software (Bosch’s DiagnosticTool is a licensed channel tool). Bafang sits in the middle: its UART mid-drives are configurable through a public, community-documented programming cable and settings file — the owner-programmable exception among volume systems. VESC is the fully open pole. The spectrum on this page is the industry in one category: open design, owner-configurable, dealer-locked.

    The legal frame (US three-class system, adopted in most states) and the maker spectrum:

    Class 2Throttle-equipped20 mphThe throttle is the whole distinction.
    Class 3Pedal-assist only, higher limit28 mphSpeedometer required; access rules vary by state/trail.

    Key chips & components

    • VESC (open design) — an STM32-class MCU + gate-driver + MOSFET stage whose exact parts are published in the project’s schematics (see the Datasheet Index for the STM32 and driver families). The one controller here with a real paper trail.
    • Gate drivers & MOSFETs — commodity power parts of the classes indexed in the Datasheet Index (the same driver families that appear in the site’s NCP302150 datasheet copy (PDF)’s application class).
    • BMS protection ICs — the pack’s guardians; cell-balancing and protection parts are catalog components, named in better packs’ documentation and absent from the cheap packs’ entirely — which is the point of the fire record.

    Faults — which part fails, and how you know

    Symptom first. Battery symptoms live under the fire rules in Safety — a pack fault is a safety event before it is a repair ticket.

    • Motor stutters, jerks, or won’t start under load; fine unloaded — a Hall sensor in the motor or its connector: the controller is guessing rotor position. Hall/phase connector seating is the first check; the sensors are the second.
    • Cuts out on hills, restarts after a pause — thermal or current limiting doing its job (controller hot to the touch confirms) — or a battery sagging under load (age, or a weak cell group the BMS keeps rescuing). Which one is data: does the display die too (battery/BMS) or stay lit (controller limit)?
    • Dead, with a hot-electronics smell from the controller — MOSFET failure, the classic. On open/modular systems the controller is a swappable unit; on integrated systems it’s the dealer’s bench.
    • Error codes on Bosch/Shimano systems — the code is real diagnostics, but the resolution path is the dealer tool; anti-tuning codes (speed-signal manipulation detected) are a documented feature, not a malfunction.
    • Display on, motor silent, brake icon stuck — a brake cutoff switch held closed (a bent lever or a magnet out of place): the safety input is doing exactly what it’s wired to do.

    Safety — the battery is the story

    The fire record, from the regulators: FDNY reported 268 lithium-ion battery fires, 18 deaths, and 150 injuries in NYC in 2023; the CPSC has issued specific warnings (e.g., January 2024) urging consumers to stop using particular uncertified battery brands after fire reports. New York City’s Local Law 39 (2023) responded by requiring UL 2849 certification for e-bikes and their batteries sold in the city. The pattern across the CPSC’s file: uncertified packs, mismatched or damaged chargers, aftermarket ‘upgraded’ batteries, and charging in egress paths and bedrooms.

    The rules that follow from the record: charge the pack the bike came with, with its own charger, on a non-flammable surface, awake and present, away from the exit path. Never charge a pack that was dropped hard, crushed, water-soaked, swollen, or repaired by anyone but its maker. A pack that hisses, swells, smells, or heats at rest is an emergency: outside, away from structures, fire department. Do not carry it through the apartment to ‘deal with it later.’

    Modification discipline: raising current/speed limits multiplies the stress on every part downstream of the number — MOSFETs, wiring, brakes, frame, and the battery’s BMS. The e-bike classes are law in most states; a derestricted bike is, legally, often a moped without the brakes or the paperwork. This page documents how the limits work; it does not tune your bike.

    Water and controllers don’t mix despite ‘IP-rated’ marketing: connectors wick, and a controller that dies wet usually died at a connector first. Dry before power, always.

    The e-bike record runs on two tracks: a fire crisis written by regulators, and a control fight written by the manufacturers’ software.

    Did you know

    • The speed limit that decides your bike’s legal class is a configuration value. Whoever holds the means to write it — owner, dealer, or nobody — holds the bike’s legal identity. That is the whole controller fight in one sentence.
    • A $20 cable and a settings file made Bafang’s mid-drives the folk standard for DIY e-bikes — the company never advertised the programmability its UART port allowed; the community documented it anyway.
    • Most e-bike ‘range’ figures are computed, not measured: the controller counts watt-hours honestly, but the marketing number assumed a rider, a temperature, and a hill profile that are never in the room.
