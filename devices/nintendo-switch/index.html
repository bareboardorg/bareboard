    Nintendo Switch Family

    Nintendo’s hybrid handheld that sold 140+ million units — and shipped with controllers that drift.

    FCC ID

    FCC ID: BKEHAC001 (Grantee: BKE = Nintendo Co., Ltd.) for the original HAC-001 model.

    • Revealed before launch: 2.4/5 GHz Wi-Fi (802.11ac), Bluetooth, non-removable battery
    • Model numbers decoded: HAC-001 (console), HAC-002 (AC adapter), HAC-015/016 (Joy-Cons)
    • Available: Internal photos, test reports, RF exposure docs

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    What you can typically find:

    • Internal photos — board layout, chip placement, shielding, antenna design
    • Block diagrams — sometimes included, showing the RF chain architecture
    • Test reports — conducted and radiated emissions data, which can reveal operating frequencies and power levels
    • Label placement — where the FCC ID lives on the device

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (usually withheld under confidentiality requests), firmware, bill of materials, or debug interface documentation.

    What we reveal

    The FCC tells you what a device emits. We tell you what’s inside and how it works.

    • Chip-level identification — every major IC named, with datasheet links where public documentation exists
    • Board mapping — what connects to what, which test pads matter, where the debug interfaces hide
    • What the manufacturer won’t tell you — undocumented features, hidden radios, silent telemetry, repair blockers

    Key Chips

    • NVIDIA Tegra X1 — quad-core ARM Cortex-A57 + quad-core Cortex-A53, Maxwell GPU. The same chip family as the NVIDIA Shield TV.
    • Mariko revision (2019+) — die-shrunk Tegra X1 on 16nm, fixing the unpatchable boot ROM exploit (fusée gelée) of the original.

    Controversies & Legal

    Joy-Con drift

    The defining hardware defect of the Switch era. Joy-Con analog sticks develop “drift” — registering input without being touched — caused by wear on internal components and dust ingress. UK consumer group Which? estimated over 40% of original Joy-Cons were affected.

    US class actions (2019–2024): Filed in 2019, alleging Nintendo knowingly sold defective controllers. After five years, the cases were dismissed — not on the merits, but because Nintendo’s EULA contains a binding arbitration clause that plaintiffs had not opted out of.

    French settlement (2026): France’s DGCCRF found Nintendo of Europe committed “misleading commercial practices” from 2018–2023 by failing to inform consumers of the defect. Nintendo agreed to pay €35 million (~$40M) — the first major financial penalty over Joy-Con drift. Nintendo denied intentional wrongdoing.

    Nintendo has offered free Joy-Con repairs since 2019, including out-of-warranty units.

    Sources: ReadWrite, Tech Xplore (AFP), Nintendo Everything

    Right to repair

    Nintendo has historically opposed independent repair — lobbying against right-to-repair legislation, using proprietary screws, and restricting parts. Joy-Con drift made this a mainstream issue: a widespread defect on a $80 controller pair that users were told to send to Nintendo rather than fix themselves.

    Aggressive anti-piracy legal action

    Nintendo is the most litigious company in gaming regarding piracy and emulation. Notable actions include the 2024 lawsuit against the Yuzu Switch emulator developers (which ended in a settlement and Yuzu shutting down) and ongoing actions against ROM sites. Critics argue this chills legitimate homebrew and preservation work.

    Specific settlement terms were not independently verified for this page; Nintendo’s pattern of emulator litigation is well documented in gaming press.
