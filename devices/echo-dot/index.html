    Amazon Echo Dot (5th Gen)

    Amazon’s spherical smart speaker, rebuilt around the in-house AZ2 Neural Edge processor for on-device speech processing. The best-documented Echo Dot teardown target to date — including a debug header hiding under the rubber base.

    FCC ID

    FCC ID: QD5-DOTH-500 (Grantee: QD5 = Amazon.com Services LLC) for the 5th-generation Echo Dot.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    What you can typically find:

    • Internal photos — board layout, chip placement, shielding, antenna design
    • Block diagrams — sometimes included, showing the RF chain architecture
    • Test reports — conducted and radiated emissions data, which can reveal operating frequencies and power levels
    • Label placement — where the FCC ID lives on the device

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (usually withheld under confidentiality requests), firmware, bill of materials, or debug interface documentation.

    What we reveal

    The FCC tells you what a device emits. We tell you what’s inside and how it works.

    • Chip-level identification — every major IC named, with datasheet links where public documentation exists
    • Board mapping — what connects to what, which test pads matter, where the debug interfaces hide
    • What the manufacturer won’t tell you — undocumented features, hidden radios, silent telemetry, repair blockers

    Key Chips

    • Amazon AZ2 Neural Edge (marked MT8519BAAV) — the main application processor — Amazon’s second-generation on-device ML chip, also used in the Echo Show 15. No public datasheet; marking suggests a MediaTek-fabbed custom part.
    • MediaTek MT7653BSN — Wi-Fi/Bluetooth combo on a small daughterboard, with PCB antennas on both sides.
    • Micron NW942 — NAND flash for OS and data storage.
    • SK Hynix H54926AY — 0.5 GB DRAM.
    • Maxim MAX98396EWB — digital-input mono Class-DG audio amplifier driving the speaker (20 W into 8 Ω).
    • MediaTek MT6398BN — multi-rail DC-DC power management IC.

    Teardown Notes

    • The fabric-covered sphere opens from the bottom: peel back the non-slip rubber base to find four T5 Torx screws and a recessed nine-pin header that appears to be a debug or programming port.
    • Inside, the main PCB mounts to a cast metal housing — the metal doubles as a heatsink and RF shield. A small ribbon cable links the processor board to the button/microphone/LED board at the top.
    • The speaker connects to the main board through spring-loaded metal prongs touching metalized pads — no wires to desolder, a genuinely serviceable touch.
    • Twelve RGB LEDs ring the perimeter of the main PCB, producing the familiar light ring through the fabric.

    Simple Entry Points

    The debug header under the base

    You need: A 3.3 V USB-to-serial adapter (about $3), Dupont wires, and a T5 Torx driver.

    1. Remove the rubber base and the four T5 Torx screws to separate the enclosure.
    2. Locate the recessed nine-pin header on the bottom of the main PCB — documented by community teardowns as a likely debug/programming port.
    3. With the device powered off, connect GND first, then try each remaining pin as TX at 115200 baud while watching for boot output. Never connect VCC from your adapter — let the Echo Dot power itself.
    4. A serial console here would show the bootloader and kernel messages, which identify the firmware layout and available recovery options.
    $ ls /dev/ttyUSB*
/dev/ttyUSB0
$ screen /dev/ttyUSB0 115200
# (boot log appears here if the header carries a console)

    Pin functions on this header are not officially documented; identifying TX/RX/GND requires a multimeter and patience. This is normal reverse-engineering practice on hardware you own.
