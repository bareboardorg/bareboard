    DJI Drones

    The best-documented consumer aircraft ever sold — and the first product line on this site whose story is a live federal case file. DJI’s FCC filings are a masterclass in public hardware records; as of December 2025, the FCC will not issue new ones.

    FCC ID

    FCC ID pattern: SZ DJI Technology files under grantee code SS3. DJI’s filings are famously complete: full internal photos of every board, antenna layouts, and RF reports for the control link (OcuSync-family, in the 2.4/5.8 GHz bands), the Wi-Fi/Bluetooth radios, and the GNSS receiver. For any DJI model already authorized, its filing at fccid.io is the finest public teardown in the industry. What changed on December 22, 2025 is that no new filings can be granted — see the record.

    Every radio a device carries — Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, ultra-wideband, NFC — needs an FCC equipment authorization before the device can be sold in the United States. To get one, the manufacturer files photographs and test reports with the FCC, and those filings are public record. Check the label on your specific unit and look it up at fccid.io.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    • Internal photos — the flight-controller board stack, the vision-sensor boards, the gimbal’s three brushless motors, and the smart-battery’s own electronics
    • Antenna documentation — leg/antenna placement for the control link, which explains the range and the orientation sensitivity every pilot learns
    • Test reports — output power per band and the band-edge behavior of a link designed to hold a video feed at kilometers of range

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (withheld under confidentiality requests where the maker files them), firmware, and the full bill of materials.

    Exploded view — the assemblies in build order

    This is the centerpiece of the page: the device taken apart into its assemblies, in the order they go together, every part named in plain language with what it does. An exploded view is the drawing a manufacturer makes showing every part separated along the path it assembles — the single most useful document in repair, and the one most often kept behind a dealer login.

    The source

    Parts book: DJI does not publish one — but DJI is the rare maker that sells the parts anyway: arms, motors, gimbal assemblies, vision modules, and boards circulate through its service network and a large third-party market, and DJI’s own repair program publishes per-model repair pricing. The stack below is rebuilt from FCC internal photos, in flight-assembly order. DJI’s flyaway/coverage programs (DJI Care Refresh) are service contracts, not documentation — the distinction this page exists to teach.

    Coverage: PARTIAL — no public parts book or exploded diagram; FCC internal-photo basis (the richest of any platform in this wave). Parts availability is unusually good through service channels; documentation is not.

    Build order

    Step 1 — Airframe & propulsion

    Four arms, four brushless motors, four propellers. The airframe is a set of replaceable arms on most models — the crash economy depends on it.

    PartWhat this part does
    Brushless motors (4)Outrunner motors, one per arm. A motor that feels gritty or notchy after a crash has a bent shaft or damaged bearing — replace the arm/motor unit, don’t fly it ‘to test.’
    Propellers (quick-release)Matched CW/CCW pairs. A nicked prop is a vibration source that the flight controller will fight and the gimbal will show you. Props are consumables; treat them that way.
    Arms & landing gearDesigned break points. Carbon or plastic arms fold or snap to save the body — sacrificial structure, like a car’s crumple zone.
    ESCs (electronic speed controllers)The motor drivers — on the main board or per-arm boards depending on model. A burned ESC presents as one dead motor with a good motor swapped in.

    Step 2 — Flight controller & sensing

    The aircraft’s brain and its eyes. This stack is why a DJI flies like it’s on rails.

    PartWhat this part does
    Flight controller (FC) + IMUThe autopilot and its inertial measurement unit (gyroscopes + accelerometers, usually redundant). IMU calibration errors after a crash are the board telling you the crash mattered.
    GNSS moduleGPS + Galileo/BeiDou/GLONASS positioning. Slow satellite lock after storage is normal; no lock in the open is an antenna/module fault.
    Vision sensing systemDownward and omnidirectional cameras + infrared ToF (time-of-flight) sensors for obstacle avoidance and precision landing. These lenses are exposed: a scratched vision sensor is a blind spot the size of a wall.
    Main processing boardRuns the vision pipeline and the video encoder for the downlink — the hottest, most compute-dense board in the aircraft.

    Step 3 — Gimbal & camera

    A 3-axis brushless stabilizer carrying the camera — mechanically the most delicate assembly on the aircraft and the most crashed-into.

    PartWhat this part does
    Gimbal motors (3 axes)Yaw, pitch, and roll brushless motors that counter-rotate the camera against the aircraft’s motion thousands of times a minute. A gimbal that buzzes, droops, or overheats has a damaged motor or a bent axis.
    Camera + image sensor1-inch or Micro-Four-Thirds-class sensors on flagships; the camera board and the gimbal are serviced as an assembly in DJI’s network.
    Ribbon cablesThe gimbal’s flex ribbons are the classic crash fatality — invisible damage, intermittent video. Connector seating is the first check.
    Dampening plateRubber isolation balls between gimbal and frame. Aged/hardened dampers = jello in the footage.

    Step 4 — Smart battery & downlink

    The battery is a computer with cells attached; the downlink is the radio the FCC filing is really about.

    PartWhat this part does
    Intelligent Flight BatteryCells + a management board that counts cycles, balances cells, enforces storage charge, and can refuse flight. A swollen DJI battery is retired by the manual’s own rule (see the warnings in Faults) — and the board logs it.
    OcuSync-family downlinkThe proprietary control/video link (2.4/5.8 GHz, adaptive). Remote ID broadcast (the FAA’s drone license-plate signal) is part of this radio’s job on current models.
    Remote controllerIts own FCC-filed device: sticks (Hall-effect on newer models — the same drift physics as game controllers), its own battery, and the phone/tablet as display.

    What is missing, in the open: schematics, boardviews, and any published exploded diagram. DJI’s model is service-network repair with good parts logistics — genuinely better than most of this wave for getting a crashed drone fixed, and still a closed book at the board level. Both halves of that sentence are the finding.

    Key chips & components

    • Flight-controller & vision silicon — DJI designs its own boards around a mix of in-house and merchant silicon (image-signal and vision processing has used Ambarella-class parts historically; current models are identified per-board in FCC photos). No public datasheets for the integrated assemblies — collection targets logged.
    • The radio layer is the documented layer: the OcuSync-family link’s filed test reports (via fccid.io, grantee SS3) are the public record of output power and band behavior — more RF documentation than any phone on this site ships with.

    Faults — which part fails, and how you know

    Crash first, electronics second — the DJI fault tree is dominated by physics. DJI’s own manuals carry the battery rules quoted in Safety.

    • Gimbal overload / gimbal motor overloaded error — a bent axis, a ribbon fault, or debris in a motor gap. Check for the shipping gimbal clamp left on (the classic), then ribbon seating, then assume mechanical damage.
    • IMU / compass errors after a crash — recalibrate once. Errors that return are the aircraft reporting real sensor damage; DJI’s own guidance is service, not more calibration.
    • One motor stutters or won’t spin up — swap-test the propeller, inspect for hair/debris in the bell, then it’s motor vs. ESC: a known-good motor on the same arm separates them.
    • Battery ‘cell error’ / refusal to charge — the battery’s own board has flagged a cell imbalance or damage. That flag is the product working as designed; the pack is done. Do not attempt to ‘wake’ a flagged smart battery with a dumb charger.
    • Video feed breakup at short range — antenna damage in a leg (crash history), a failing gimbal ribbon (for camera feed specifically), or genuine RF congestion. The FCC-filed antenna map tells you which leg holds which antenna.
    • Flyaway — post-incident, the flight log is the black box: DJI keeps detailed onboard logs, and the log’s GPS/IMU trace usually names the cause (compass interference at takeoff is the recurring villain — rebar and car roofs are compass poison).

    Safety, before you open anything

    DJI prints this rule in its manuals and this page repeats it because it is the one that injures people: never use a swollen, leaking, or crash-damaged battery, and stop flying a pack that gets abnormally hot. A smart battery that has flagged itself is retired — the electronics are not being cautious on your behalf, they are reporting cell damage.

    Propellers at flight RPM are blades. Motors get tested on the bench with props off, always — an armed drone on a bench is the hobby’s most reliable source of stitches.

    Regulatory safety is also safety: Remote ID, registration, and airspace authorization (LAANC in the US) are the legal layer of not hurting anyone. The record below is about market access; the airspace rules bind regardless of how the case law lands.

    The full record — dated, sourced, nothing smoothed over

    No platform in this wave has a record like this one: a decade of escalating federal action, all of it in public documents, ending (for now) in a market freeze and a federal appeal.

    Did you know

    • The drones you already own are not banned: the Covered List blocks new authorizations. Existing models keep their grants, their parts channels, and — by an FCC waiver — their firmware updates through January 1, 2029.
    • DJI’s FCC filings (grantee SS3) are the deepest public hardware documentation of any consumer product line — the ban froze the product pipeline, not the public record; every authorized model’s internal photos remain downloadable.
    • The gimbal is three brushless motors flying in formation: the camera is stabilized mechanically, not digitally — which is why a bent gimbal axis cannot be fixed by a software update.

    Unverified — alleged, not proven

    “DJI drones covertly send your flight data to Beijing.” For the claim: the US government’s determinations are on the record above — the 2017 Army memo, the 2019 CISA alert, the 2025 interagency determination — and DJI’s software historically included analytics and third-party SDK components that drew legitimate researcher criticism (a 2020 Synacktiv analysis of DJI’s Android app documented aggressive data collection and an update mechanism that bypassed the Play store; DJI removed the flagged SDK and disputed the characterization). Against the claim as stated: multiple commissioned security audits (including FTI Consulting’s 2020–2021 examinations) found no evidence of covert exfiltration, especially with Local Data Mode enabled, and no public evidence has shown flight data reaching Chinese government hands. The regulator actions are documented fact; the covert-pipeline claim remains unproven in public evidence. This page reports both, labeled.
