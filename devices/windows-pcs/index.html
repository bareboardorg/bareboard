    Windows Laptops, Desktops & Surface

    Windows PCs are the anti-Apple lesson and the anti-lesson in one family. Business laptops from Lenovo, Dell, and HP ship with full public service manuals — exploded diagrams, part numbers, and screw-by-screw removal steps, free for anyone to download. Desktops are the one platform that was designed from the start to be opened, built to a shared standard anyone can buy parts for. And then there is Microsoft’s Surface: a Windows PC glued shut like a phone. This page teaches all three worlds — how to read them, how to diagnose them, and what the documented record actually says about each.

    FCC ID

    A laptop rarely has one FCC ID of its own — it carries the FCC IDs of its radio modules. The Federal Communications Commission (FCC — the U.S. radio regulator) certifies things that transmit: the Wi-Fi and Bluetooth card, and the mobile-broadband card if one is fitted. The processor, the board, and the rest of the machine do not transmit, so they are not what the filing is about.

    The label method. Look on the bottom cover, under the battery on older machines, or under a service door. You will find either the module’s own FCC ID printed there, or a “Contains FCC ID: …” line naming the transmitter module inside. Lenovo states the method plainly in its ThinkPad Regulatory Notice: “The FCC ID for the wireless LAN and Bluetooth module is shown on the enclosure of your computer” — and the same notice lists each preinstalled module separately, for example FCC ID: PD97265NGU for the Intel 7265NGW wireless LAN/Bluetooth module and FCC ID: TX2RTL8192EEBT for the Realtek RTL8192EEBT module (ThinkPad Regulatory Notice, Lenovo). The first three characters are the grantee code — the company that holds the certification: PD9 is Intel, TX2 is Realtek.

    A current example, verified. The Intel Wi-Fi 6E AX211 module (model AX211NGW) fitted to many current laptops carries FCC ID: PD9AX211NG. Its filing on fccid.io is a radio test report — it describes the “Wireless Module installed in Convertible PC,” lists 802.11ax dual-band Wi-Fi 6E plus Bluetooth 5.2, and nothing about the laptop’s main board (AX211 test report, fccid.io; a second AX211 grant, fccid.io). That is the point to remember: the filing covers the radio module, not the whole computer. Swap the Wi-Fi card and you have swapped the FCC ID that matters.

    Desktops usually have no FCC ID label at all unless a Wi-Fi card was installed — the same module rule applies. Surface devices carry their radio certifications the same way, module by module.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for a radio module includes the documents the manufacturer is required to submit: external photos, internal photos of the module, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual for the module. These are public record at fccid.io.

    What you can typically find:

    • Internal photos — of the radio card itself: its chips, shielding can, and antenna connectors
    • Block diagrams — sometimes included, showing the RF chain architecture
    • Test reports — conducted and radiated emissions data, which reveal operating bands, channels, and power levels
    • Label placement — where the FCC ID lives on the host machine

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (usually withheld under confidentiality requests), firmware, bill of materials, or debug interface documentation.

    The twist for Windows PCs: on most platforms this site covers, the FCC filing is the deepest public document you will ever get. For business Windows laptops it is not. The FCC filing tells you about a radio card the size of a postage stamp. The manufacturer’s service manual — the subject of the next section — documents the entire machine, part by part. Read the filing to identify the radios; read the service manual to learn the computer.

    Exploded view

    On most device pages here, this section has to say “partial.” Here it can be real. Lenovo publishes its ThinkPad Hardware Maintenance Manuals (HMMs) as free PDFs, and they contain exploded parts illustrations keyed to numbered part lists. The current manual used for this walk is the ThinkPad L14 Gen 7 and ThinkPad L16 Gen 3 Hardware Maintenance Manual (Lenovo). Lenovo’s own description of the section, verbatim: “By the exploded view and the part list, you can find the images, names, and types of all the CRU/FRUs of your computer.”

    Two terms first. A FRU (Field Replaceable Unit) is a part the manufacturer defines as a replaceable whole — you do not repair the fan’s motor, you replace the fan assembly, and the FRU list gives that assembly its own number in the diagram. A CRU (Customer Replaceable Unit) is the subset Lenovo designates for the customer to swap, split in the manual into self-service and optional-service CRUs. Every callout number below is the manual’s own number for the ThinkPad L14 Gen 7.

    One machine, in build order

    Read the exploded view the way the machine comes apart — outside in. This is also the order of the manual’s own replacement chapter.

    1. Base cover assembly (callout 19) — the bottom panel. On the L14 Gen 7 the manual’s first hardware step is: “Loosen the 10 captive screws and pry at the right hinge area.” Captive means the screws stay in the cover — they cannot fall into the machine or get lost on the bench. Everything else is behind this one panel.
    2. Built-in battery (callout 24) — the flat battery pack across the front of the chassis, held by screws (the manual’s battery step lists three M2 screws), not glued. It comes out second because every later step is safer with it disconnected and removed. The coin-cell battery (callout 25) — the small round cell that keeps the clock and settings — sits nearby.
    3. Storage — M.2 solid-state drive (callout 16, with bracket 15) — the gum-stick drive in its slot. One screw and it slides out. A second, shorter M.2 2242 slot (callouts 13–14, on selected models) can hold a smaller drive or the wireless WAN module (callouts 21–22).
    4. Memory module (callout 20) — the RAM (random-access memory — the machine’s short-term workspace) stick in its socket. It unclips and lifts out at an angle. A socketed stick you can upgrade in minutes: hold this picture in mind when the Surface section below talks about soldered memory.
    5. Thermal fan (callout 8) and heat sink (callout 7) — the fan that moves the air, and the metal heat sink, listed by the manual as “Heat sink with thermal grease,” that carries heat off the processor. Cooling is two numbered parts, each replaceable on its own.
    6. System board (callout 10) — the main board, listed as “System board with solid-state-drive thermal pad.” Everything above plugs into it. Around it in the same drawing: the USB I/O board (callout 11) and its cable (callout 9), the speaker assembly (callout 23), the trackpad (callout 30) and its cable (callout 29), and the smart card reader (callout 17, selected models).
    7. Keyboard (callout 2) — a self-service CRU: the owner-tier part, removable from the top after loosening its screws, sitting in the keyboard bezel assembly (callout 3) with the power button (callouts 5a/5b, with or without fingerprint reader).
    8. Display assembly / display unit (callout 1) — the whole lid, one FRU. The manual gives the lid its own second exploded view (LCD FRUs): LCD bezel (1), LCD panel (2), camera and microphone module (3), EDP cable (5) — the cable that carries the picture — wireless LAN antenna assembly (7), hinges (8), and LCD cover assembly (9). The antennas for your Wi-Fi live in the lid, which is why the radio cables run up through the hinges.

    That is what a real parts book looks like: one drawing, every assembly numbered, every number named, and a parts-lookup page behind it for the orderable part numbers. Lenovo keeps the same series of manuals for older machines in its Hardware Maintenance Manuals library, and the current ThinkPad T14 Gen 7 Hardware Maintenance Manual follows the same structure — safety chapter, diagnostics chapter, exploded view and part list, then removal steps.

    The manual’s own safety warnings, verbatim

    From the L14 Gen 7 / L16 Gen 3 HMM, quoted exactly, before any part is removed:

    “DANGER: Before removing any CRU/FRU, turn off the computer, unplug all power cords from electrical outlets, disable the built-in battery, and then disconnect any interconnecting cables.”

    “CAUTION: Use only the Lenovo-authorized battery specified for the computer. Any other battery could ignite or explode.”

    “If the battery is incorrectly replaced, there is danger of an explosion. The battery contains a small amount of harmful substances.”

    — ThinkPad L14 Gen 7 and ThinkPad L16 Gen 3 Hardware Maintenance Manual, Lenovo (linked above). Follow them literally: disable the built-in battery in the BIOS setup before the base cover comes off, exactly as the manual’s “Disable Fast Startup and the built-in battery” step instructs.

    Surface: partial — no public parts book

    Surface: partial — no public parts book. For the Surface line there is no equivalent of the HMM above for the classic models: no manufacturer exploded view with a numbered FRU list that an owner can download and work from. What exists publicly for those machines is the FCC module filings (photos of the radios), teardown photography by repair shops, and — for newer models only — the service guides and replacement components Microsoft began publishing years later (see Repair reality, below). The exploded-view standard this site holds every device to cannot be met for Surface from the manufacturer’s own public documents, so this section does not pretend otherwise and does not fabricate a diagram.

    Diagnosis & faults

    POST (Power-On Self-Test) is the check the machine runs on itself in the first seconds after you press the power button, before Windows starts. A machine that will not POST — no picture, no Windows logo — is not a Windows problem. Work the power in the order it flows; each step rules out a whole layer.

    No-POST, rail by rail

    1. Adapter voltage first. Confirm the charger is the right one for the machine and measure its output at the plug with a multimeter (a handheld meter for voltage and continuity): a USB-C charger should negotiate and deliver its rated voltage (commonly 20 V under load), a barrel-plug adapter should read its printed rating. No voltage at the plug = a dead adapter or cable. Stop there; nothing inside the laptop can fix it. Lenovo’s HMM makes the same check step one of its power diagnosis: verify the adapter, then the battery, each by substitution and by whether the machine starts on one source alone.
    2. Always-on rails: 3.3 V and 5 V. Even “off,” a plugged-in board makes standby power — 3.3 volts and 5 volts, present all the time the adapter or battery is connected. These rails feed the power button circuit and the controller that starts the machine. If the adapter is good but the always-on rails are absent on the board, the fault is in the board’s standby power section, before any start sequence begins.
    3. The power sequence. Pressing the button starts a strict order: standby rails up, then the embedded controller signals the chipset, then the main voltage rails rise one by one (memory power, then processor core power), then a “power good” signal releases the processor to run the POST. A board that dies at the same point every time is telling you which rail never came up. Measure in that order; the first missing rail is where the fault lives — everything after it is innocent.
    4. Strip to minimum. One memory stick, no storage drive, no Wi-Fi card, battery disconnected, adapter only. Add parts back one at a time. A shorted accessory card can hold the whole sequence down and imitate a dead board.

    Beep and blink codes: the machine talking without a screen

    When POST fails, many machines report the failure as sound or light, because the screen may be the thing that is dead. A beep code is a pattern of beeps from the board’s speaker; a blink code is a pattern of flashes from a status LED (light-emitting diode). The pattern is a number — count it, then look it up in that model’s service manual, because the meanings are defined per manufacturer and sometimes per model.

    A worked example from a current manual: Dell’s Latitude 7430 service manual documents its LCD power rail test reporting through the battery status LED — an error code of [2,7] (two flashes, pause, seven) means the display cable may not be connected properly, and [2,8] means a failure on the LCD power rail of the system board, so no power is supplied to the display (Latitude 7430 Service Manual — L-BIST, Dell). Same lesson as Lenovo’s symptom-to-FRU index in the HMM: the manufacturers who publish manuals also publish the code tables. Machines whose makers publish neither leave you guessing.

    Desktop power supplies: test by swapping, never by opening

    A desktop PSU (power supply unit) converts wall power into the 12 V, 5 V, and 3.3 V rails the board uses. Test one three safe ways: (1) swap in a known-good PSU of equal or greater wattage — if the machine starts, the old PSU was the fault; (2) use a plug-in PSU tester, a cheap block that loads the outputs and reads each rail; (3) measure rails at a spare connector with a multimeter while the PSU is installed and running. All three keep the PSU’s case closed.

    Never open a power supply. This is our own safety instruction, stated plainly: the large capacitors inside a PSU can hold a dangerous charge long after the cord is unplugged, and that charge can kill you. There is no user-serviceable part inside. A failed PSU is replaced as a unit — on a standard desktop that is four screws and a set of plugs, which is exactly why the design is safe to service.

    The three worlds

    “Windows PC” is three different repair worlds sharing one operating system. Which world your machine lives in was decided by its product line, not by Windows.

    The business lines are open for an unglamorous reason: their buyers are companies with fleets of thousands of machines and in-house or contracted technicians, and a machine that cannot be serviced is a machine a fleet buyer will not purchase twice. The manual is a sales feature. Remember that whenever a consumer maker claims a glued design was “necessary”: the same industry, in the same years, shipped the opposite to customers who could demand it.

    Key Chips & boards

    The service manuals name assemblies. The boards themselves carry another layer of identification, printed in white silkscreen (the ink lettering on the board) — learn to read it, because board numbers, not laptop model names, are how you find schematics, parts, and other technicians’ notes.

    • ODM board numbers. Most laptops are designed and built not by the brand on the lid but by an ODM (Original Design Manufacturer) — a company that designs and manufactures the machine for the brand. The big three are Compal, Quanta, and Wistron. Their board numbers are silkscreened on the board itself: Compal boards carry LA- numbers (read the LA- number off your board and search by it — that string, not the retail model name, is the board’s true name in repair documentation and parts listings). Quanta and Wistron boards likewise carry their own printed board and revision numbers. Identification practice, stated as practice: photograph every silkscreened number before you order anything; boards are revised mid-production, and the revision letter matters.
    • Intel Wi-Fi 6E AX211 (AX211NGW) — the radio module from the FCC section: FCC test report (fccid.io). A replaceable M.2 card on machines that keep it socketed — and the whole FCC identity of the laptop’s wireless in one part.
    • onsemi NCP302150 — an integrated DrMOS power stage: DrMOS combines the driver chip and the MOSFET switches (the transistors that chop power) in one package. Laptop and desktop boards feed the processor and graphics through rows of such phases in a VRM (voltage regulator module — the circuitry that converts the main rail down to the ~1 V a processor core runs on); each phase is one DrMOS part and its coil. A shorted DrMOS is the classic dead-board fault the rail-by-rail diagnosis above hunts. Full part listing and context: the Datasheet Index, which documents this part as used eight-at-a-time feeding a GPU core.
    • Winbond W25Q16JW — a small serial flash chip (16-megabit, 1.8 V) of the type that holds the BIOS/UEFI firmware (the board’s own start-up program) on PC boards. When a machine “bricks” after a failed firmware update, this class of chip — read and reprogrammed externally with a clip and a programmer — is often the way back. Datasheet hosted in our Datasheet Index.
    • Chipsets and processors. On modern Windows laptops the processor and its companion chipset are soldered (that is the honest limit of the business-laptop world too — a dead processor means a system-board FRU, not a chip swap). The repairable layer is everything around them: power stages, flash, radio cards, and every assembly in the exploded view. For the wider chip census see the Chip Database.

    Board schematics and boardview files for specific ODM boards are not published by the brands; where no public documentation exists for a part named here, it is logged in our collection targets rather than linked to an unverified copy.

    Repair reality

    Business laptops: the manual libraries are real and public. Lenovo’s HMM library runs from current machines (L14 Gen 7 / L16 Gen 3) back through its end-of-life manual archive. Dell publishes model service manuals the same way — the Latitude 7430 Service Manual used for the blink codes above is one ordinary example, safety chapter and “before working inside your computer” included. HP’s EliteBook line has its Maintenance and Service Guides (for example the EliteBook 840 G11). None of this needs an account, a contract, or permission.

    Surface: the scores tell the story, in order. iFixit scores repairability from 0 (unrepairable) to 10 (easiest to repair):

    • 2013 — original Surface Pro: 1 out of 10. iFixit found the display assembly held by heavy adhesive, the battery glued down behind the motherboard, and over 90 screws inside (iFixit, Surface Pro teardown).
    • 2017 — original Surface Laptop: 0 out of 10. iFixit’s verdict, quoted in its teardown: “This laptop is not meant to be opened or repaired; you can’t get inside without inflicting a lot of damage” — the processor, RAM, and storage were soldered, and the battery was, in their words, difficult and dangerous to replace (iFixit, Surface Laptop teardown).
    • 2022 — Surface Pro 9: 7 out of 10. A markedly more repairable machine: storage accessible through a rear hatch, a battery screwed in rather than glued, and Microsoft publicly offering repair manuals and spare parts — iFixit’s tablet scorecard records all three as the reasons (iFixit, tablet repairability scores; the Pro 9’s provisional 7/10 was reported at teardown time by Notebookcheck).

    The turn. In December 2021, Microsoft and iFixit announced a partnership: Microsoft-designed Surface service tools — a display debonding tool, a display bonding frame, and a battery cover — manufactured and sold by iFixit, initially to iFixit Pro independent repairers and Microsoft Authorized Service Providers rather than to the general public (Engadget, December 2021). Microsoft later began selling Surface replacement components directly — batteries, displays, keyboards, SSDs and more for Surface Laptop models through the Microsoft Store — and publishes self-repair information listing replaceable components and service guides per model (Microsoft Support, self-repair information). All of that is real progress, and all of it dates from the 2020s — a decade after the glued originals, and after the agreement in the next section.

    Desktops: repair reality is the control group. When a desktop PSU, fan, drive, or board fails, the repair is a part swap with hand tools and the replacement can come from any maker, because ATX standardized the interfaces. No score is needed; the platform was never locked. If you want the deep bench guidance, start at Repair Guides and the Raspberry Pi hub for the single-board end of the same open tradition.

    Controversies & Legal

    The two Microsofts

    One company runs both stories on this page. Microsoft’s software business lives on the open PC platform — an ecosystem of Lenovo, Dell, HP, and home-built machines whose openness (public manuals, standard parts, owner servicing) is a large part of why Windows won. Microsoft’s own hardware line, Surface, spent its first decade as the least repairable Windows machines ever scored: 1/10, then 0/10, with glued batteries setting the lifespan of otherwise working computers. The company that profited from everyone else’s open platform sold a closed one. That contrast — not any single teardown — is the controversy, and it is documented in the scores above.

    October 2021: the As You Sow agreement

    In October 2021, after shareholder representative As You Sow filed a resolution pressing Microsoft on repair, Microsoft agreed — and As You Sow withdrew the resolution — to: complete a third-party study of the environmental and social impacts of increasing consumer access to repair, including for Surface and Xbox; expand the availability of certain parts and repair documentation beyond its Authorized Service Provider network; and initiate new mechanisms to enable local repair options for consumers, with expanded options promised by the end of 2022. As You Sow described it as the first known shareholder resolution relating to the right-to-repair movement (As You Sow, October 7, 2021). The December 2021 iFixit tool partnership and the later parts sales in Repair reality are what followed.

    Windows machines under the repair laws

    A Windows laptop or desktop is an ordinary consumer electronic product under the state right-to-repair laws: the same statutes that cover phones and game consoles cover the PC in your bag, including their requirements on parts, documentation, and tools. The state-by-state picture is at repair rights — electronics states, and the federal ground (warranty law and the DMCA repair exemptions) at repair rights — federal. The business-laptop makers were, in effect, complying with the documentation half of these laws decades before the laws existed — for their fleet customers. The laws exist to extend the same treatment to everyone else.

    The Full Record

    The documented record of this platform — recalls of trust as much as of hardware: preinstalled software that broke security, drivers that leaked or exposed the machine, a screen failure the maker first could not fix, and the repair-hostility record, each entry dated and sourced. Documented findings are stated as fact with their source. Anything not proven is labeled, or left out.

    DateEntry
    August 2014 – early 2015Lenovo ships Superfish adware on consumer laptops. Lenovo preinstalled VisualDiscovery, developed by Superfish, Inc., on consumer laptops sold in the United States. The FTC’s complaint charged that the software acted as a “man-in-the-middle” between the user’s browser and websites — including encrypted pages — injecting advertising and creating serious security vulnerabilities; it was installed on hundreds of thousands of laptops. Lenovo stopped shipping it in early 2015. This was the consumer line; the ThinkPad business line of the Exploded view section was not the subject of the complaint. (JD Supra summary of the FTC settlement; PCWorld)

Lenovo settled with the FTC and 32 state attorneys general: $3.5 million paid to the states, a ban on misrepresenting preloaded ad-injecting software, affirmative consumer consent required before installing such software again, and a comprehensive software security program for preloaded software, subject to third-party audits for 20 years. (JD Supra; PCWorld)
    May 11, 2017A keylogger is found in HP’s audio driver — a debug trace shipped by mistake. Swiss security firm modzero reported that MicTray64.exe, part of the Conexant HD audio driver package on HP EliteBook, ProBook, Elite, and ZBook machines, recorded all keystrokes — via a low-level keyboard hook — to a world-readable log at C:\Users\Public\MicTray.log and broadcast them through a debug interface; tracked as CVE-2017-8360. modzero’s advisory timeline shows discovery on April 28, 2017 and public release on May 11 after vendor contact attempts. HP’s statement, in full fairness, said the code was supplier software written to test audio functionality before launch that “should not have been included in the final shipped version,” that HP had no access to customer data as a result, and that fixes would be available via HP.com — a debugging feature left in production, not a deliberate spying program, on the documented record. (Threatpost; The Register; NVD, CVE-2017-8360)
    May 11, 2018“Flickergate”: Microsoft replaces flickering Surface Pro 4s. Owners documented Surface Pro 4 displays that flickered until the machines were unusable, a failure users campaigned on at Flickergate.com. Microsoft stated it had determined “a small percentage of Surface Pro 4 devices are exhibiting a screen flicker that cannot be addressed with a firmware or driver update,” and offered free replacement of eligible devices for up to three years from the original purchase date, with refunds for customers who had already paid for an out-of-warranty repair of the issue. Scope, stated exactly: eligible Surface Pro 4 devices with the flicker, after installing the latest updates — not a general recall of the line. (CRN; Digital Trends)
    May 4, 2021Dell’s DBUtil driver: five flaws under one CVE, present since 2009. SentinelLabs disclosed five high-severity flaws in Dell’s firmware-update driver dbutil_2_3.sys — two memory-corruption and two input-validation flaws allowing local escalation to kernel-mode privileges, plus a denial-of-service flaw — tracked together as CVE-2021-21551 (CVSS 8.8). The driver had been in use since at least 2009 and came preinstalled on most Dell Windows machines; SentinelLabs reported it to Dell on December 1, 2020, Dell released a security update (advisory DSA-2021-088), and SentinelLabs stated it had found no evidence of in-the-wild abuse at disclosure. (SentinelLabs)
    2013 – 2022The Surface repair-hostility record, and its reversal. 1/10 (Surface Pro, 2013), 0/10 (Surface Laptop, 2017), the October 2021 As You Sow agreement, the December 2021 iFixit tool partnership, and 7/10 with public manuals and parts (Surface Pro 9, 2022) — the full arc, with sources, is in Repair reality and Controversies & Legal above, and is not repeated here.

    Unverified — not recorded as fact: claims that repeat about these events beyond what the sources above support — for example, that the HP MicTray logging was deliberate surveillance (modzero itself framed it as debugging turned spyware-like, and HP documented it as a test feature shipped by mistake), or that the Dell driver flaws were exploited in the wild (SentinelLabs explicitly reported no evidence of abuse at disclosure). Where a claim has no published evidence behind it, this page says so rather than repeating it.

    Did you know

    • The open desktop is older than Windows 95’s successors — and still current. The ATX standard that lets any maker’s board, case, and power supply fit any other maker’s was released by Intel in July 1995, and as of 2026 it still remains the standard for personal computers (Wikipedia, ATX; Lenovo glossary).
    • The first Surface Pro had over 90 screws — and still scored 1 out of 10. iFixit is pro-screw; the score was lost to glue: the display was anchored with, in their words, the most adhesive they had ever seen on a small device, and the battery was glued down behind the motherboard (iFixit teardown).
    • Zero is a score only one laptop has earned. Reporting the later Surface Laptop 3, iFixit noted the original Surface Laptop’s 0 out of 10 in June 2017 — “This device is not meant to be opened or repaired” — and that no other laptop they had scored had received a zero, not even recent MacBook Pros (iFixit News).
    • A vulnerable Dell driver sat on machines for about 12 years before anyone published it. SentinelLabs found Dell’s dbutil_2_3.sys firmware-update driver had been in use since at least 2009; its five flaws (CVE-2021-21551) were only reported to Dell in December 2020 and disclosed in May 2021 (SentinelLabs).
    • The HP “keylogger” dated itself to Christmas. modzero’s researchers dated the MicTray keylogging code on HP computers to “at least Christmas 2015” from the files’ own metadata — a debug feature that sat in shipped audio drivers for well over a year before it was found (Threatpost).
    • Your laptop’s FCC ID belongs to a card the size of a postage stamp. The certification on the bottom label is the Wi-Fi/Bluetooth module’s, not the computer’s — one Intel AX211 module carries the same FCC ID, PD9AX211NG, into every brand of laptop that fits it (AX211 FCC test report).
