    Xbox Family

    Four generations of Microsoft consoles — from a PC in a black box in 2001 to the Series X|S — and the most expensive hardware failure in gaming history.

    FCC ID

    FCC IDs vary by model and revision. Microsoft’s grantee code — the first three characters of every FCC ID it files, identifying the company the grant was issued to — is C3K. Check the label on your specific console and look it up at fccid.io — the filings include internal photos that are invaluable for hardware research.

    Verified on fccid.io from the grants themselves (grantee: Microsoft Corporation, One Microsoft Way, Redmond, WA):

    • FCC ID: C3K1525 — the WLAN module (the Wi-Fi radio board) fitted in the original Xbox One. Grants dated June 19, 2013 and September 18, 2013 list the equipment notes as “WLAN MODULE” and the modular type as “Limited Single Modular” — in plain terms, this filing covers the radio board inside the console, not the whole console.
    • FCC ID: C3K1914 — a Microsoft filing granted June 20, 2020, listed in the grant as a “Wireless Computer Device” covering 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands. It is one of the filings from the Series X|S launch generation; the grant text itself does not name a retail product, so we do not either.

    Other model-to-FCC-ID mappings are being verified and will be listed here. The label on your unit is the authority for your unit.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    What you can typically find:

    • Internal photos — board layout, chip placement, shielding, antenna design
    • Block diagrams — sometimes included, showing the RF chain architecture
    • Test reports — conducted and radiated emissions data, which can reveal operating frequencies and power levels
    • Label placement — where the FCC ID lives on the device

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (usually withheld under confidentiality requests), firmware, bill of materials, or debug interface documentation.

    Exploded view

    Coverage: Partial — no public parts book. Microsoft publishes no console service manual and no illustrated parts book (a manufacturer document that draws every assembly apart and numbers every part) for Xbox consoles. There is no official exploded diagram to reproduce, and we do not invent one. What follows is a text exploded view — the assemblies in build order, outside in — built from the public record that does exist: the FCC filings above (whose exhibits include internal photos) and independent teardown documentation of the Series X, principally iFixit’s Xbox Series X teardown. Where a fact comes from a teardown rather than a Microsoft document, it is a teardown finding.

    Safety first. Microsoft’s own Xbox Series X|S product guide warns, verbatim: “Always select and use an appropriate AC power supply and/or power cord for your device. Failure to take the following precautions can result in serious personal injury or death from electric shock or fire or in damage to your device.” (Microsoft Xbox Series X|S Product and Regulatory Guide.) In plain language: the power supply is inside these consoles, and a power supply can hold a stored electric charge in its capacitors (parts that store electricity) even after you unplug the console. Unplug the console and leave it unplugged before opening it, and treat the power supply unit itself as a sealed box to swap whole, not a board to probe.

    1. Shell (outer case and base). The plastic and metal skin of the tower. It protects the inside and shapes the airflow. On the Series X, teardown finding: the first screws are bright green T8 Torx screws, hidden under stickers and a trap door in the base.
    2. Fan and heatsink. The fan is the part that moves air; the heatsink is the metal block that soaks heat away from the hot chips. Series X teardown findings: one large 130 mm fan at the top, exhausting upward, and a copper vapor-chamber heatsink (a flat sealed chamber that spreads heat evenly) bolted over the main processor, with an aluminium centre chassis between the boards that also carries heat away. Together they are most of the volume inside the case — this console is mostly a cooling system with a computer inside it.
    3. Power supply unit (PSU). The box that turns wall power into the low-voltage power the boards run on. Series X teardown findings: an internal unit whose main rail supplies up to 21.25 amps at 12 volts (255 watts), plus a 60-watt peripheral output — 315 watts in total — and which uses polymer capacitors, a longer-lived type, to filter its 12-volt output.
    4. Optical drive (Series X only). The disc drive that reads game discs and 4K Blu-ray discs. The Series S has no disc drive. Series X teardown finding: the drive is the same model used in the Xbox One S and Xbox One X — and its circuit board is paired to the mainboard (see Repair reality).
    5. Mainboard with APU. The main circuit board, and on it the APU (accelerated processing unit — the single chip that combines the CPU, which runs the game code, and the GPU, which draws the picture). Almost everything else plugs into or solders onto this board, including the HDMI port (the socket your TV cable plugs into) at its edge.
    6. Storage. The drive that holds the operating system and your games. On Series X|S this is an NVMe SSD (a small, very fast solid-state drive with no moving parts) in a standard M.2 2230 socket — a short card held by one screw, sitting under thermal compound (heat-transfer paste) on the board sandwich, per the teardown. It is mechanically removable. Whether a replacement will actually work is a separate question, answered in Repair reality.

    Read this view next to Diagnosis & faults: the view shows you what each part is, not which part has failed.

    Diagnosis & faults

    Xbox consoles diagnose themselves first in software. Microsoft’s own recovery environment is the Xbox Startup Troubleshooter — a recovery menu built into the console that loads before the main system software, so it works even when the console will not boot to its home screen.

    Getting to it (from Microsoft’s startup-error guidance): power the console off and unplug the power cord; wait 30 seconds and plug it back in; press and hold the Pair button and the Eject button, then press the Xbox button on the console; keep holding Pair and Eject for 10–15 seconds, through two power-up tones. On the Series S, which has no Eject button, hold Pair alone. The console powers up straight into the Startup Troubleshooter. Microsoft’s pages: Xbox startup error support and Perform an offline system update.

    Error-code driven diagnosis. When startup fails, the console shows an error code (a short code such as E101 or E102). The code is the diagnosis: Microsoft’s startup-error guidance routes each code to a fix order — restart, then reset from the Startup Troubleshooter choosing “Keep games and apps” (which resets the operating system and deletes possibly corrupted data without deleting your games or apps), then an offline system update, then a full factory restore, then service. Codes in the E100–E102 family are, in Microsoft’s framing, problems installing the latest console operating-system update.

    The Offline System Update (OSU). If the console cannot download or install its update normally, Microsoft publishes an offline route: on a Windows PC, download the Offline System Update file — OSU1 for Series X|S and most Xbox One models — from the Xbox Support page above, put the extracted $SystemUpdate folder on a USB flash drive of at least 6 GB formatted as NTFS (a Windows file-system format), plug the drive into the console, and choose “Offline system update” in the Startup Troubleshooter. The original 2013 Xbox One is the exception: depending on the system version it is stuck on, it may need an older file (OSU2 or OSU3) first. Take OSU files only from Microsoft’s support page — the file changes with each system release.

    The hardware failures independents actually see (repair-community documented — these are the fault patterns independent repair shops report on their benches, not a Microsoft list):

    • HDMI port damage — the console powers on but the TV shows no signal, or the picture only works with the cable held at an angle. The port is soldered to the mainboard and its pins bend when a console is moved with the cable plugged in. Shops also report failure of the small HDMI retimer chip (the chip that cleans up the video signal on its way to the port) when the port itself looks undamaged. Sources: iAssist Series X|S repair page, iFixForU Series X repair cost guide.
    • Fan and cooling failure — a normally quiet console turns loud, or shuts down mid-game: dust packed into the top exhaust and heatsink, dried-out thermal compound, or a failing fan bearing. Fixed by a full clean and thermal service, per the same repair-community sources.
    • Power supply failure — a completely dead console, or one that dies after a power surge. Because the PSU is internal on Series X|S, One S and One X, there is no external brick to swap-test; the unit inside has to be tested or replaced.

    APU = accelerated processing unit, one chip combining the CPU and GPU. The pattern across five rows is the story of the industry: bought-in PC parts (2001), custom chips built for Microsoft alone (2005), then one combined AMD chip per console (2013 onward).

    Key Chips

    • NVIDIA NV2A (original Xbox, 2001) — the graphics chip, a variant of NVIDIA’s GeForce 3 line running at 233 MHz, built only for this console. No public datasheet exists — see the note below.
    • ATI Xenos (Xbox 360, 2005) — the graphics chip, running at 500 MHz with 10 MB of its own fast embedded memory (eDRAM) on the same package. An early user of unified shaders (one pool of processors that can do every kind of graphics work). No public datasheet exists.
    • AMD Jaguar APU (Xbox One, 2013) — eight low-power x86-64 CPU cores and the graphics in one semi-custom AMD chip. The industry converged on PC architecture. No public datasheet for the console version.
    • Scorpio Engine (Xbox One X, 2017) — a larger semi-custom AMD chip in the same pattern, built for 4K gaming. No public datasheet.
    • AMD Zen 2 + RDNA 2 APU (Series X|S, 2020) — eight Zen 2 CPU cores and an RDNA 2 graphics unit in one custom AMD chip (52 graphics compute units in Series X, 20 in Series S). AMD publishes architecture documentation for Zen 2 and RDNA 2 as used in its PC parts, linked here; the console chip itself has no public datasheet — our datasheet index lists “PlayStation 5 / Xbox Series X SoCs — no public datasheets,” and the same is true here.
    • Series X|S internal storage — a custom Western Digital PC SN530 NVMe SSD (product brief linked; the brief covers the SN530 family, including the short M.2 2230 form factor the console uses), 1 TB in Series X and 512 GB in Series S, in a standard socket. See the chip database for the wider silicon picture.

    Custom console silicon is where public documentation ends. NV2A, Xenos, the Jaguar and Scorpio console chips, and the Series X|S console SoCs have no public datasheets to link; that gap is recorded, not papered over.

    Repair reality

    What Microsoft sells you: controller parts (2023)

    In 2023 Microsoft began selling genuine replacement parts for Xbox controllers through the Microsoft Store — for the Xbox Wireless Controller and the Xbox Elite Wireless Controller Series 2. The store’s own Xbox Wireless Controller repair parts collection lists, among other items, replacement top cases, replacement buttons, a replacement input PCBA (PCBA = printed circuit board assembly, the populated circuit board) and a replacement PCBA and motor assembly. Microsoft published its own repair guides and step-by-step videos alongside the parts, and reporting at launch put prices from about $24 for button sets to about $60 for a circuit board and motor assembly. Source: Engadget, “Microsoft starts selling replacement parts for Xbox gamepads” (August 1, 2023).

    That program covers controllers. No equivalent Microsoft parts program for the consoles themselves appears in the sources above — there is no Microsoft Store shelf of Series X fans, power supplies or mainboards for owners.

    The storage question: socketed, but paired

    Teardown finding (iFixit): the Series X internal SSD is not soldered down — it is a standard-format M.2 2230 NVMe card that lifts out of its socket, which led iFixit to note that “it’s nice to see Microsoft using a replaceable drive here, even if software barriers prevent you from doing the replacing,” and to warn that SSD repair would likely be “problematic at best.” Repair-community documentation since then (a repair-rights wiki summary of teardown and bench tests, including a published test in which a working SSD swapped between two Series X consoles would not boot in the receiving console) reports that the drive is cryptographically paired (locked by encryption keys) to its own console’s processor, so a blank or donor drive is not accepted. State of the record: the socket is real and publicly documented; the pairing is repair-community documented, not something Microsoft has published a specification for, and Microsoft sells no pre-paired replacement SSD and no pairing tool. A dead internal SSD is therefore, in practice, a Microsoft service event, not a parts swap. Sources: iFixit Series X teardown, Consumer Rights Wiki — Xbox Series SSD repair limitation.

    The optical drive has the same shape of problem, as a teardown finding: iFixit’s tests indicate the drive’s circuit board is paired to the mainboard, and the documented workaround is to desolder that board from the failed drive and transplant it into a replacement drive — bench soldering work, not a swap.

    What remains maker-only

    • Board-level HDMI repair — replacing the soldered HDMI port, or the retimer chip behind it, is microsoldering (soldering under magnification) that shops do without any Microsoft parts channel.
    • Power supply repair — component-level PSU work is done by independents at their own risk; Microsoft publishes no PSU schematic or parts list.
    • Anything paired — storage and optical-drive electronics cannot be re-paired outside Microsoft’s own service system, on the record above.

    Controversies & Legal

    The Red Ring of Death (2005–2007)

    The defining hardware failure of the Xbox 360. Consoles failed in large numbers with a general hardware failure signalled by three flashing red lights around the power button — the “Red Ring of Death.” On July 5, 2007, Microsoft announced it was extending the warranty for that specific failure to three years, reimbursing owners who had previously paid for repairs for it, and taking a pre-tax charge of $1.05–$1.15 billion to pay for it all. It remains the reference case for what a console defect costs when a manufacturer has to own it in public.

    Sources: Computerworld, Game Developer

    The repair turn: As You Sow (2021)

    In October 2021, Microsoft reached an agreement with As You Sow, a shareholder advocacy group that had filed a shareholder resolution (a formal proposal by investors) pressing Microsoft on repair. As You Sow withdrew the resolution, and Microsoft agreed to complete a third-party study of the environmental and social impacts of increasing consumer access to repair — expressly including Surface devices and Xbox consoles — to expand the availability of certain parts and repair documentation beyond its Authorized Service Provider network, and to initiate new mechanisms for local repair options. As You Sow described it as the first known shareholder resolution on the right-to-repair movement. The 2023 controller-parts program above arrived after that agreement. Source: As You Sow, “Microsoft Agrees to Expand Consumers’ Repair Options” (October 7, 2021).

    Where your own repair rights stand today is state by state and device by device: see right-to-repair law for electronics, by state.

    Did you know

    • There are two Master Chiefs inside a Series X. iFixit’s teardown found Microsoft had hidden a Master Chief (the Halo character) depiction inside the console — and then found a second one while peeling the boards apart: “We’re delighted to have two Master Chiefs living inside our Halo machine.” Source: iFixit Series X teardown.
    • The way in is bright green — and hidden. The Series X’s first case screws are bright green T8 Torx screws, a colour no user was meant to miss, fitted under stickers and a trap door in the base. Teardown finding, same source.
    • The Series X disc drive is a hand-me-down. Its optical drive is the same model Microsoft shipped in the Xbox One S and Xbox One X — carried across a full console generation. Teardown finding, same source.
    • The Series X power supply was built to last longer than most. Its 315-watt internal PSU filters its 12-volt output with polymer capacitors, which iFixit notes are “pricier but longer-lasting” than the electrolytic capacitors most power supplies use. Teardown finding, same source.
    • A disc-scratching argument about a games console made it to the US Supreme Court. Microsoft Corp. v. Baker (2017) is taught as a class-action procedure case — the console that started it is almost a footnote in its own lawsuit. Source: Wikipedia, “Microsoft Corp. v. Baker”.
