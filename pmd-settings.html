The Settings, in Plain Language

Your chair’s behavior — how fast it goes, how hard it launches, whether it filters out your tremor — is a list of numbers stored inside it. A dealer tool reads and writes that list. You were never shown it. Here are the core drive and seating settings, setting by setting.

Today, changing these takes the dealer’s programmer. Knowing what each one means is the first half of taking it back: you cannot ask for a setting you cannot name, and you cannot tell when a swap has silently taken one away.

Provenance: the parameter list and ranges are from Curtiss-Wright’s own R-net programming documentation (On-Board Programming manual SK78571/3 and the R-NET Technical Manual), rewritten in plain language.

SettingRangeWhat it actually doesWhat goes wrong
Top speed (forward speed, max/min)0–100%How fast the chair goes at the top speed setting, and at the lowest. The settings in between are spread evenly between the two.After a factory reset, top speed can come back clamped too slow for a crosswalk — or too fast for a hallway.
Acceleration (forward accel, max/min)0–100%How hard the chair launches when you push the stick. Higher = harder launch.Too high: the launch snaps your head back. Too low: the chair feels sluggish and cannot climb a door threshold from a stop.
Deceleration (forward decel, max/min)0–100%How hard the chair brakes when you let the stick go. Higher = shorter stop.Too high: letting go pitches you forward. Too low: the chair coasts into walls — or traffic.
Turn speed / turn accel / turn decel0–100%How fast the chair turns and how sharply it ramps into and out of a turn.Too high on a mid-wheel-drive chair: violent spinning and caster strikes.
Tremor dampening (joystick filtering)0–100%A filter that smooths out high-frequency hand tremor and spasm so the chair follows your intended push, not your shaking.Critical on a module swap: if a swapped module comes back at 0%, a rider with tremor or spasticity gets a chair that bucks with every shake — uncontrollable. This is the setting a wiped configuration takes with it. And the manual’s own warning cuts the other way: heavy dampening also increases stopping distance — it is a balance, not a maximum.
Torque (low-speed power)0–100%Extra power at low speed, for climbing ramps, door thresholds, and thick carpet.Too low: the chair stalls on a one-inch threshold. It is a working setting, not a comfort setting — a reset that loses it strands you at a curb cut.
Sleep timer0–30 minHow long the chair sits idle before it powers itself down.At 0 (off), the battery drains overnight. Too short, and a switch or sip-and-puff user gets locked out mid-rest.
Seating actuator accel / decel0–100%How fast power tilt, recline, elevate, and legrests start and stop moving.Must stay at 100% on systems with automatic current-sensing endstops — see the warning below.

The one setting that can break hardware

Some power seating systems find the end of an actuator’s travel by sensing motor current (an automatic endstop) instead of a physical switch. On those systems, the seating actuator acceleration and deceleration must be 100%. Set lower, the endstop detection does not work and the motor drives past its mechanical limit — destroying the actuator or bending the seat frame. (Curtiss-Wright R-NET Technical Manual, “Endstop Autodetect”: “Automatic endstop can only be used with actuator Acceleration and Deceleration values of 100%.”)

This is the honest core of the dealer’s safety argument — real for a handful of settings. It is not an argument for locking all of them, including reading your own.
