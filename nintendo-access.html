Access and Control: Nintendo

The Nintendo Switch has two recovery modes, and they share a name in most people’s heads. One is Nintendo’s — a menu for fixing a console that will not start. The other is NVIDIA’s — a factory mode built into the processor itself, which on launch-era hardware hands the owner the deepest access any modern console has ever given up: a complete backup and restore of the machine. This page keeps them separate, and maps the rest of Nintendo’s doors besides.

This is access documentation: what the doors are, which consoles have them, and what an owner can do with them — above all, make the backup that turns a dead console from a total loss into a repair. There are no keys here and no software downloads. The Switch hub page covers the hardware and the drift lawsuits; this page covers the doors.

Provenance: Nintendo’s support pages document Maintenance Mode and nothing deeper — Nintendo has never published a word about recovery mode, the rail pinout, or test points. Everything beyond Maintenance Mode is attributed to the community documentation it comes from (the NH Switch Guide, the ConsoleMods wiki, repair and preservation communities) and labelled as community knowledge, never as Nintendo fact.

Door one: Maintenance Mode — Nintendo’s, on every Switch

Maintenance Mode is Nintendo’s built-in recovery menu. It exists on every Switch — original, Lite, OLED, patched or not — and Nintendo’s own support pages walk owners through it when a console freezes, will not start properly, or is stuck after an update.

What it unlocks, in one line: update the system, or reset the console to factory condition without deleting save data — the official way to un-brick a sick Switch while keeping what matters.

Nintendo’s steps:

  1. Turn the console completely off. If it will not respond, hold the POWER button for about twelve seconds to force it off.
  2. Hold both volume buttons — Volume Up and Volume Down — and press POWER once.
  3. Keep holding both volume buttons until the Maintenance Mode screen appears, then let go.

The menu offers a system update, Initialize Console Without Deleting Save Data, and a full initialize that erases everything. The middle option is the one to know: it resets the system software while keeping save data, screenshots and user information. It is the last official step in Nintendo’s own troubleshooting for a console that keeps crashing — everything before it is updates and restarts.

Know this menu exists before you need it, because the day the console will not boot is the wrong day to learn it exists.

Door two: RCM — NVIDIA’s, in the chip

The Switch’s processor is NVIDIA’s Tegra X1. Like every Tegra, it contains a recovery mode — shortened to RCM — built into its bootROM. The bootROM is the very first program the processor runs, stored in read-only memory inside the chip itself (ROM = memory whose contents are fixed forever). Recovery mode is a factory and repair feature: instead of starting the console’s system, the chip stops, and waits for a computer to send it a small program — a payload — over the USB-C cable. Nintendo did not add this mode and cannot remove it; it is part of the silicon it bought.

On its own, RCM accepts only NVIDIA-authorised payloads. What changed everything is a flaw in that check, disclosed in April 2018 by the fail0verflow team and researcher Kate Temkin and catalogued as CVE-2018-6242 — the community calls it fusée gelée. On chips made before Nintendo and NVIDIA corrected it, the flaw lets an owner’s payload run. Because the flaw lives in read-only silicon, no system update can close it on those units — and no update can open it on units made after the correction.

How an owner enters RCM

The trigger is electrical, and it runs through the right Joy-Con rail. One contact in that rail is a signal line the processor samples as it starts: connect it to ground at power-on, and the chip enters recovery mode instead of booting.

  1. Turn the console fully off.
  2. Slide a jig — a small moulded tool that bridges the correct rail contacts, and nothing else — into the right Joy-Con rail. The contact that must be grounded is pin 10.
  3. Hold Volume Up and press POWER. If it worked, the screen stays completely black: no logo, no backlight glow of a normal boot. That black screen is RCM — the console is now waiting for a payload over USB-C.
  4. Connect the console to a computer or phone and send a payload with one of the community launcher tools (on Windows, the community tool is called TegraRcmGUI). Remove the jig; it is only needed for the moment of power-on.

The community guide’s warning, verbatim, because people keep learning it the expensive way: “USING A PAPERCLIP OR TIN FOIL CAN/WILL DAMAGE YOUR CONSOLE, DO NOT DO THIS!” — and on making a jig: “Make sure your jig NEVER touches pin 4. Pin 4 provides 5 volts of power to the Joy-Con, and can permanently damage the rail or console if shorted.” (NH Switch Guide, a community source.) A proper jig costs a few dollars. A rail costs a board repair.

Two confusions to avoid. RCM is not Maintenance Mode — different mode, different entry, different maker; the volume-buttons-both menu above is Nintendo’s, and RCM needs the rail signal as well as Volume Up. And a console can also fall into RCM on its own if its internal storage is missing or too corrupted to boot — which is why a completely black, unresponsive Switch is not always a dead one.

Which Switches have the door

  • Launch-era units (model HAC-001, made from March 2017 until Nintendo’s production change around mid-2018): open. These carry the uncorrected Tegra chip. The community maintains serial-number checkers that sort units into unpatched, possibly patched, and patched — the serial is on the sticker on the bottom of the console. If you are buying a used Switch for repair research, this is the number that matters.
  • Later HAC-001 units (“patched”): the wall. Nintendo quietly shipped chips with the flaw corrected during 2018. These consoles still enter RCM — the mode is in every Tegra — but they refuse owner payloads. Entering RCM on one proves nothing except that the mode exists.
  • The 2019 refresh (HAC-001(-01)), Switch Lite (HDH-001) and Switch OLED (HEG-001): closed. All use the revised chip (the community and Nintendo’s filings call it by its codename, Mariko) with the corrected bootROM. Every one of them, always.

There is no setting, update or accessory that moves a console between these groups. The group was decided on the production line, in the silicon. Community projects have since built soldered-in hardware for the closed models; that is a different trade — board surgery, not a door — and it is outside what this page documents.

What RCM unlocks

Send the community bootloader Hekate as the payload and the black screen becomes a workbench. Each capability, in one plain line:

  • Full storage backup. Hekate copies the console’s entire internal storage to the microSD card — the two small boot partitions (called BOOT0 and BOOT1) and the full raw image of everything else. This is a bit-for-bit snapshot of the machine’s memory.
  • Full storage restore. The same tool writes a backup back. A console whose storage failed, corrupted, or was wiped can be returned to the exact state of its backup — system, games and save data together.
  • Storage inspection from a PC. Hekate can present the internal storage to a connected computer as a USB drive, so its contents can be examined and copied with ordinary tools.
  • Running owner software. From the bootloader, the console can start community system software and homebrew — programs written by owners, running on their own hardware — or other operating systems entirely, such as Linux.
  • Diagnostics. The bootloader reports on the storage chip’s health and identity before any system software is involved — the first thing to check on a console that will not boot.

The backup is the one that matters for this site’s mission, so be blunt about the discipline: take the backup before anything goes wrong. The Switch’s storage contents are encrypted with keys unique to each individual console. A backup from another Switch is useless to yours, and a console whose storage dies with no backup of its own generally cannot be brought back — the keys died with it. Owners of launch-era units hold the only complete insurance policy Nintendo never sold.

The honest risks, stated with the capabilities and not after them. A payload runs with total control of the machine, so a careless restore can leave a console worse off — the backup is also the protection against that, which is why it comes first. System updates permanently burn microscopic eFuses inside the chip (one-way fuses that record the newest system version, to prevent downgrades), so restoring an old backup needs the bootloader’s help to boot at all. And Nintendo bans consoles it detects running modified software from its online services — a modified console should be treated as an offline machine. None of this is hidden by the community; it is in its guides, and it belongs in ours.

The storage board: the part Nintendo got right

Open an original Switch and the internal storage is not soldered to the main board. The eMMC — the storage chip (embedded MultiMediaCard: flash storage and its controller in one package) — lives on its own small daughterboard, a little circuit board that plugs into the main board under the metal shield and lifts off with a plastic pry tool once the shield is removed. No soldering to remove the module; the memory chip is soldered only to its own small board. The OLED model keeps the separate-board design (at 64 GB instead of 32 GB); on the Switch Lite the chip is soldered to the main board, and this door closes.

Why it matters, in one line: a failed storage module on an original or OLED Switch can be replaced with a blank one, and the owner’s own backup restored onto it — a documented community repair that turns the most fatal failure on the console into a board swap and a restore. Photo-documented upgrade guides in the repair community (the GBAtemp community’s write-ups among them) walk the same path to fit larger modules.

The pairing catch is data, not permission: the module’s contents are encrypted with your console’s unique keys, so a module pulled from another Switch reads as noise in yours, and your module only ever works again in your console or as the source of a restore. The board is swappable. The identity inside it is not transferable. That is the correct way to build this — compare the paired drive boards on the PlayStation page, where the check is enforced by the motherboard rather than by mathematics.

The Joy-Con rail, and what is not documented

Each side rail is a ten-contact connector carrying power and data between the console and an attached Joy-Con. Sliding a Joy-Con onto the rail pairs it automatically; attached, the controller talks to the console over the rail’s wired serial link and charges from it, and detached, it switches to Bluetooth. The rail’s right side carries the RCM trigger line (pin 10) described above. Nintendo publishes no pinout for it — the contact-by-contact documentation that exists is community work, in the NH Switch Guide and the ConsoleMods wiki, and it is labelled there as reverse engineering, which is what it is.

For the rest of the board, the honest sentence is short: Nintendo publishes no schematics, no test-point map, and no UART documentation for the Switch. A UART — a simple serial connection that prints diagnostic text — is the standard first door on most hardware (see the PS4 on the PlayStation page, where the community has documented one). On the Switch, no equivalently documented, owner-usable diagnostic port is part of the public record; board-level diagnosis in the repair community proceeds from the rail, from RCM on launch units, and from component-level testing. Where documentation is thin, this page says thin — it does not repeat forum lore as fact.

Earlier Nintendo, at summary level

  • GameCube (2001). Expansion ports underneath (serial and high-speed ports, used by the broadband and modem adaptors). Nintendo offered owners no software access; the machine is simple enough that repair is direct hardware work — no pairing, no firmware locks of the modern kind. Community documentation is deep.
  • Nintendo DS (2004). No owner-facing access surface from Nintendo at all; the community’s historical access ran through the cartridge slot. Repairs are part-for-part.
  • Wii (2006). The SD card slot became the community’s door, and full backups of the internal storage are standard practice in Wii repair — with the same rule as the Switch: the backup has to exist before the failure. Nintendo’s own documentation covers none of it.
  • Nintendo 3DS (2011). Has a documented recovery key combination — hold L, R, A and Up on the D-pad while turning the power on — that boots straight to a system-update screen for a console that cannot start normally. Deeper access is extensively documented by the community.
  • Wii U (2012). Internal-storage backups are community-documented, and the lesson of the machine is the backup rule again: a Wii U with corrupted storage and no owner-made backup generally has no owner recovery path.
  • Switch 2 (2025). No owner-access route is publicly documented at the time of writing: no recovery-mode equivalent, no published board documentation. Nintendo’s settings-level recovery is the whole surface so far. If that changes, this page changes.

The law (United States)

Section 1201 of the DMCA makes bypassing a digital lock on a copyrighted work — console firmware counts — illegal even for a lawful purpose, unless an exemption covers it. Exemptions are granted every three years and written at 37 CFR § 201.40. The current repair exemption, § 201.40(b)(15) — adopted 2021, renewed 2024, in force through October 2027 — allows circumventing a lock when necessary to diagnose, maintain or repair a consumer device. For consoles it narrows to one sentence, quoted exactly:

“For video game consoles, ‘repair’ is limited to repair or replacement of a console’s optical drive and requires restoring any technological protection measures that were circumvented or disabled.”

The Switch has no optical drive. Neither does the Switch Lite, the OLED model, or any handheld Nintendo has ever made. So this exemption does not cover Switch access at all — the one console with the deepest owner access is squarely outside the one console repair exemption, because the exemption was written around disc drives. The same gap closes over every cartridge and download-only console. That is the state of the law as written, and it is why the rulemaking record matters: broader console exemptions have been proposed and not adopted, and the separate anti-trafficking rules (§ 1201(a)(2) and (b)), which ban distributing circumvention tools, cannot be exempted by the Librarian at all.

What does apply: copyright law has never made it illegal to open your own hardware, replace its parts, or read a connector’s pins — the rail pinout, the storage board swap, and Maintenance Mode on this page involve no circumvention question at all. On warranty, the US Federal Trade Commission warned Nintendo, Sony and others in 2018 that “warranty void if removed” conditions violate the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act unless waived or unless the owner’s work caused the failure; opening a console does not by itself void a US warranty, though damage your work causes is yours, and Nintendo’s online-services bans for modified consoles are account decisions outside warranty law entirely.

This section is a summary of public law for education, not legal advice. BareBoard is not a law firm.
