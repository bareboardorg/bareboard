Getting a Repair Paid For

Your chair broke. The supplier’s queue is measured in weeks, and somewhere in that queue is an approval step. For repairs, Medicare’s own rules say most of that queue should not exist. This page is those rules, in plain language, with the document names — so you can cite them, not just believe them.

Provenance: compiled from CMS’s own publications — the Medicare Benefit Policy Manual (Publication 100-02, Chapter 15, §110.2), the Standard Documentation Requirements Policy Article A55426 as published by the DME MACs (the regional contractors that process Medicare equipment claims; this page cites Noridian’s publication), CMS’s prior-authorization documentation for power wheelchairs, and 42 CFR §414.210. This is documentation, not legal advice: if your claim is denied, the appeal steps at the end are the road, and your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) walks it with people for free.

First question: do you own the chair, or is Medicare still renting it?

Everything below splits on this. A capped rental is Medicare’s rent-to-own category: Medicare pays monthly, and after 13 months of payments the supplier must transfer ownership to you.

  • Standard power wheelchairs (billing codes K0813–K0831 and K0898) must be rented — capped rental is the only route. During those 13 months, the chair is the supplier’s property on loan to you.
  • Complex rehabilitative power wheelchairs — the Group 3 chairs most full-time users ride (codes K0835–K0843 and K0848–K0864) — can be rented or purchased, and the supplier must offer you the choice when the chair is first delivered. Most are purchased. A purchased chair is yours from day one.

If your chair is still in its rental period: there is no separate Medicare payment for repairs at all — the DME MACs state it flatly: no payment for repair of rented items under any circumstances during a capped rental period. That is not a gap in your coverage. It means the repair is already paid for inside the rent: the supplier must service, repair, or replace the chair as part of the rental. A supplier who bills you for a rental-period repair, or parks your broken rental for a month, is failing an obligation — say so, in those words.

If you own the chair — purchased outright, or past month 13 of a rental — read on. The rest of this page is yours.

Repairs to a chair you own are covered

Medicare’s definition of a repair (Benefit Policy Manual, Ch. 15, §110.2) covers work — parts and labour — needed to make equipment serviceable again after damage or wear. For equipment you own, the Standard Documentation Requirements (Policy Article A55426) say it in one line: repairs are covered when necessary to make the item serviceable.

The limits, so nobody can inflate them later:

  • Routine maintenance is not a repair. Testing, cleaning, regulating and checking are excluded. A covered repair fixes something that is broken or worn out.
  • Warranty work is the maker’s bill, not Medicare’s — parts and labour covered by a manufacturer’s or supplier’s warranty are not separately payable. (Know which clock your failed part is on: Parts, Not Assemblies prints the makers’ warranty terms next to Medicare’s.)
  • There is a cost ceiling: if a repair would cost more than replacing the chair for the remaining period of need, Medicare will not pay the excess.
  • Your share is the standard Part B split: Medicare pays 80% of the approved amount for the repair; you owe 20% coinsurance (your percentage share) after the annual Part B deductible. A supplier quoting you 100% of a covered repair is not describing Medicare.

The paperwork myth: a repair does not need a new prescription

This is where weeks are manufactured. The rule, from Policy Article A55426: a new treating practitioner’s order is not needed for repairs. What a repair claim needs instead is documentation of two things: continued medical need (you still need the chair) and that the repair itself is reasonable and necessary (the chair is broken and this fixes it). And if Medicare paid for the chair in the first place, the chair’s medical necessity has already been established — it does not get re-proven because a motor died.

Do not confuse this with prior authorization — Medicare’s pre-approval step. Prior authorization is real, but it belongs to delivery of a new chair: CMS requires it before delivery for specific new power wheelchairs (its own documentation checklist covers codes K0856 and K0861, the Group 3 single- and multiple-power-option chairs). It is a purchase gate. A supplier who sends a repair into the same queue — new evaluation, new face-to-face, new everything — is adding steps Medicare’s repair rules do not contain. Name the article: A55426.

The loaner rule nobody mentions

While your own chair is in the shop, Medicare pays for a temporary replacement: one month’s rental of a loaner chair, billed under code K0462 (the DME MACs publish this under Repairs, in so many words). One month will not cover a two-month repair — that gap is real, and it is the supplier’s delay, not a rule. But the loaner itself is a covered benefit with a code. Ask for it by the code.

Why repairs are the whole game: the five-year clock

Medicare will not replace your chair inside its five-year reasonable useful lifetime except for loss, theft, or irreparable damage (42 CFR §414.210(f) — the hub’s law section has the wider picture). Chair electronics die at a median of 32.2 months; batteries at 22.3 months. Do the arithmetic and the system’s design is plain: your chair is expected to break, more than once, in a window where replacement is barred — and repairs are the only bridge Medicare built across that window. The rules above are not fine print. They are the bridge. Use them like it.

When a supplier says no

  1. Ask which rule. “Which Medicare rule requires a new evaluation for a repair? The Standard Documentation Requirements, Article A55426, say a new order is not needed for repairs.” A rule that cannot be named usually does not exist.
  2. Get the denial in writing. Verbal refusals evaporate. A written denial carries the reason — and the reason can be appealed.
  3. Use the free help. Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) gives free, local Medicare counselling, including on equipment claims and appeals. 1-800-MEDICARE can also open a complaint against a supplier.
  4. Write the code down. Same as every page in this section: the fault code, the date, what was said, by whom. A repair you can document is a repair you can argue about — and win.
