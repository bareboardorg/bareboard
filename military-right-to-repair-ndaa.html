    Why the military cannot fix its own gear

    The United States military buys some of the most expensive machines on Earth — fighter jets, ships, trucks, generators — and then, in many cases, is not allowed to fix them. The repair manuals, the diagnostic software, and the right to make a spare part often stay with the company that sold the machine. In 2026, both houses of Congress voted to change that. As of late November 2026, the change looks likely to be stripped out of the final defense bill — for the second year in a row.

    What “right to repair” means for the military

    A right to repair is the right of the owner of a machine to fix it: to have the manual, the parts, the tools, and the software access needed to diagnose and repair it, instead of being forced to go back to the seller. For a phone or a tractor, that is a consumer question. For the military, it is a readiness question: a jet that waits months for a contractor to fly in is a jet that does not fly.

    The specific right at stake in this fight is technical data rights — the government’s legal right to use the drawings, manuals, and software that describe a weapons system. When the Pentagon buys a system without those rights, the contractor keeps them, and every repair that needs that information has to go through the contractor, on the contractor’s schedule, at the contractor’s price. The Project On Government Oversight (POGO), a nonpartisan watchdog, notes that maintenance and repair can make up as much as 70% of a weapons program’s total lifetime cost.

    What Congress voted for

    The annual defense policy bill is the National Defense Authorization Act, usually shortened to NDAA. It is the law that sets defense policy and spending for the fiscal year. Fiscal year 2027 (FY27) runs from October 1, 2026 to September 30, 2027.

    Both chambers of Congress put military right-to-repair provisions into their versions of the FY27 NDAA:

    • House, Section 861 — an amendment by Representatives Maggie Goodlander and Pat Harrigan giving the Department of Defense default government-purpose rights to the technical data and software it needs to maintain its own equipment. Government-purpose rights mean the government may use the data for its own purposes, including repair, without paying again for each use.
    • Senate, Section 804 — language by Senators Elizabeth Warren and Tim Sheehy, approved by the Senate Armed Services Committee 27–0. The same idea: the Pentagon gets the data rights it needs to repair what it owns.

    Both provisions passed with bipartisan support. Neither is law yet: the House and Senate versions have to be reconciled into one final bill in a conference committee, and then passed and signed.

    Why it is likely to be dropped

    On November 18, 2026, House Armed Services Committee Chair Mike Rogers (Republican, Alabama) said at a Hudson Institute event that the provision will not survive the final bill. Reporting by Military.com on November 24, 2026 put it plainly: “The top Republican on the House Armed Services Committee has alluded to the ‘right to repair’ provision likely being left out of the final version of the Fiscal Year 2027 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA), even as it garnered support from both congressional chambers.”

    Rogers gave two reasons. First, he said repairs can be dealt with contractually — that is, the Pentagon can negotiate repair rights into individual contracts instead of having them by law. Second, he said the Department of Defense gave him zero examples over a year of being unable to get what it needed. He also said, “I’m not going to start telling people I’m going to take your [intellectual property].”

    Critics of the decision, including POGO, blame the power of defense contractors: the companies that profit from repair and sustainment contracts have lobbied hard against the provisions. A POGO-led letter urging Congress to keep the provisions was published on November 19, 2026.

    The “zero examples” claim, against the record

    The claim that there are no examples of the military being unable to repair its own equipment does not match the public record. Examples reported by POGO, by the Government Accountability Office (GAO — Congress’s own audit agency), and in a letter from a Marine Corps logistics officer to the Federal Trade Commission include:

    • The F-35 fighter. POGO reports the F-35 Joint Strike Fighter program — expected to cost about $1.6 trillion in sustainment alone — faces delays, inadequate repair equipment, and costly maintenance problems, in part because the Department of Defense does not have the access to repair information and materials it needs. Maintainers have needed contractor help for corrosion repairs because they lack certain technical data, with too few contractor personnel to respond promptly.
    • Marine Corps engines in Japan. Marines stationed in Japan waited months for repairs on engines that had to be shipped back to the United States, because repair by Marines would have violated the repair-support contracts. The same pattern applied to other costly repairable parts shipped from Okinawa.
    • $900 a page for manuals. The Air Force paid $900 per page for upgrades to contractor-owned maintenance manuals.
    • A contractor flown in for a simple repair. The Navy once had to spend thousands of dollars to fly in a contractor to make simple repairs.
    • A generator in Korea. A mechanic on exercise in Korea was prohibited from maintaining a generator because the warranty would be voided — the unit had to choose between voiding a warranty and losing the equipment. Marines who did fix warrantied equipment were reprimanded.
    • A ship’s crane motor. The GAO documented a Navy littoral combat ship with a broken crane motor: a qualified repair contractor would not finish the job without the original manufacturer present. The Navy waited two and a half weeks, and shipyard officials considered that quick.

    Congressional reporting has also named the T-38 trainer fleet among the programs affected. And a Department of Defense survey of more than 200 sustainment programs found half of the respondents reporting data-rights deficiencies that obstructed competition or in-house support.

    None of this proves that every repair problem is a data-rights problem. But it does show that the “zero examples” claim is not the whole record — the examples are public, documented, and specific.

    FY26 versus FY27: do not mix them up

    This is the second year of the same fight, and much of the search traffic mixes the two years up. Here is the distinction:

    • FY26 NDAA: similar right-to-repair provisions appeared in both chambers’ proposals for the fiscal 2026 defense law, and were stripped from the final version in December 2025 after industry pushback. What survived was a requirement for improved tracking of technical-data problems — a system to catalog the reasons repairs stall, not the means to fix them.
    • FY27 NDAA: the provisions came back — House Section 861 and Senate Section 804, described above — and as of November 24, 2026 they look likely to be stripped again in conference. The final bill had not yet passed at the time of writing.

    If you read a story about right-to-repair provisions being “stripped from the NDAA,” check the date: a December 2025 story is FY26; a November 2026 story is FY27.

    What happens next

    The final FY27 NDAA has to pass before the lame-duck session of Congress ends, with government funding running to a December 11, 2026 deadline. Three outcomes are possible: the provisions survive in some form; they are dropped again; or they are replaced with a weaker compromise, such as better tracking or a study. Senators Warren and Sheehy have also pushed a standalone bill, the Warrior Right to Repair Act, which would require contractors to provide the technical data and materials the military needs — that route does not depend on the NDAA conference.

    Why this is on a repair-education site

    This is the same pattern this site documents everywhere else, at the largest possible scale: the owner of a machine — here, the taxpayer, through the military — is told it may not fix what it owns, because the instructions are someone else’s property. A wheelchair owner waiting on a dealer’s programmer and a maintainer waiting on a contractor’s manual are waiting on the same idea. The difference is only the stakes: when a chair waits, a person is stranded; when a jet waits, a squadron is grounded.
