Repairing a graphics card (GPU)

A graphics card — the board that draws everything on your screen, built around a GPU (graphics processing unit) chip — is two different repair problems in one box. At the service level it is one of the most repairable parts in a PC: fans, paste, pads, and connectors are all owner-serviceable with screwdrivers. At the board level it is repairable too, but the manufacturer keeps the circuit diagrams and the factory test software locked away, so the work is slower than it should be. This page covers both levels, the documented failure record of the current power connector, how to spot a counterfeit used card, and — stated plainly, with the record — exactly what is and is not published.

Safety first

Read this before you open anything.

The power supply can kill you; the card cannot. A PSU (power supply unit — the box that converts wall power for the PC) stores electrical charge in its capacitors even after it is unplugged, and that charge can be lethal. Work on the graphics card only, with the PC switched off and unplugged. Never open the PSU itself — no repair on this page needs it.

Mind static. ESD (electrostatic discharge — a static spark, often too small to feel) can kill a memory chip silently. Wear a grounding wrist strap, or touch a grounded metal object often, and work on a plain hard surface rather than carpet.

Burnt plastic means stop. If a power connector smells burnt or shows brown, softened, or melted plastic, stop using the card. That failure mode has a government recall attached to it (see the connector record below); it is a fire hazard, not a quirk.

The oven trick is a myth, and it is harmful. Baking a dead card in a kitchen oven does not repair it. A home oven cannot run the controlled temperature profile that solder needs, the heat warps the board and cooks plastic connectors and fan bearings, and flux fumes contaminate an oven that later cooks food. When a baked card “revives,” what usually happened is that heat temporarily closed a cracked solder joint — the crack is still there, and it fails again. Professionals rework boards with hot-air stations and controlled profiles. Skip the oven.

Step one: prove it is the card

Most “dead GPU” reports turn out to be something else. Work this order before you blame the card — each step rules out a whole family of causes, cheapest first.

  1. Reseat everything. Power off, unplug. Pull the card and push it back into its PCIe slot (the long connector slot on the motherboard) until the latch clicks. Reconnect every power cable until each plug is fully home. On the 16-pin connector this matters most: NVIDIA’s own guidance is to plug the power cable into the card first, firmly and evenly, before installing the card into the machine. A visible gap between plug and socket means it is not seated.
  2. Swap the display path. Try a different cable, a different port on the card, and a different monitor or TV input. If one port is dead and the others work, you have a port problem (Level 1), not a dead card.
  3. Rule out the slot and the motherboard. Try the card in a second PCIe slot or in another PC. Or put a known-good card in this PC. Whichever way the fault moves tells you which part owns it.
  4. Rule out the power supply. A failing PSU imitates a failing GPU almost perfectly: black screens and crashes under load. Test with a known-good PSU of at least the wattage the card maker specifies. Test by swapping, never by opening the PSU (see the safety box).
  5. Rule out software. Boot into safe mode, or boot the PC from a live USB operating system. If the picture is clean there, the card is fine and the driver is not: remove the graphics driver completely with a driver-cleaning tool in safe mode, then install a fresh copy from NVIDIA or AMD.
  6. Watch heat and fans. Free monitoring tools — GPU-Z (from TechPowerUp) is the standard one — show the card’s temperatures and fan speeds. Fans that never spin, or temperatures climbing steeply at light load, point at the cooling hardware (Level 1 fixes), not at dead silicon.

If the card still fails after all six, the fault is on the card itself. Start at Level 1 anyway — it is cheap — and treat Level 2 as the step after.

Level 1: service anyone can do

Tools for everything in this section: small Phillips screwdrivers, a soft brush, compressed air, isopropyl alcohol at 90% or stronger, lint-free wipes, new thermal paste, replacement thermal pads, and a cheap digital caliper (a measuring tool with a sliding jaw and a digital readout) for the pads. Work powered off, keep every screw with the part it came from, and take a photo before each step — the photo is your reassembly manual.

Cleaning

Dust mats itself into the heatsink fins (the stack of thin metal plates that carry heat away) and chokes the airflow; overheating that cures with a cleaning is common. Remove the card, hold each fan still with a finger — never let compressed air spin a fan freely, which can overspeed it — and blow the dust out from the back of the heatsink toward the front. Brush what stays. The gold contacts on the PCIe edge connector can be wiped gently with a lint-free cloth dampened with isopropyl alcohol.

Fan replacement

The fans are wear items and they are replaceable — as assemblies matched to your exact card model, not as universal parts. Remove the fan shroud (the plastic cover) screws, unplug the small fan connector (2 to 4 pins) from the board, and read the part number printed on the sticker in the center of the fan hub. Order that part number, refit, and route the cable so it cannot touch the blades. A fan that grinds, wobbles, clicks, or refuses to start is a fan to replace, not a card to bury.

Thermal paste replacement

Thermal paste is the heat-conducting compound between the GPU die (the silicon chip itself) and the cooler. Over years it dries out and pumps out from between the surfaces, and the die temperature climbs while everything else looks normal. The repair, in order:

  1. Remove the cooler: undo the screws around the die first — they are usually spring-loaded and arranged in a square — then the remaining shroud and backplate screws, keeping each set separate.
  2. Separate the cooler from the board with a gentle twist. Old thermal pads (next section) will resist; never pry against the die.
  3. Clean the die and the cooler’s contact plate with isopropyl alcohol and a lint-free wipe until both are mirror-clean. Keep every thermal pad in place and note where each one sat — you need them for the pad step below.
  4. Apply a pea-sized dot of new paste in the center of the die (or a thin, even spread; either is fine — coverage is what matters).
  5. Refit the cooler, tightening the die screws a little at a time in an X pattern so pressure stays even, then the rest of the screws.
  6. Test under load and compare temperatures with before.

Do not substitute liquid-metal compounds unless you fully understand them: they conduct electricity, they can short the small components around the die, and they corrode aluminum coolers.

Thermal pads: measure, then replace

Thermal pads are the soft, squishy squares that carry heat from the memory chips and the power circuitry into the cooler. The memory chips — VRAM (video RAM; on modern cards these are GDDR chips, arranged around the die) — have no other cooling path, so the pads are not optional. The classic self-inflicted injury in GPU service is fitting pads of the wrong thickness: too thick, and the cooler cannot sit flat on the die, so the core overheats and the board flexes; too thin, and the pad never touches, so the memory overheats. Measure first:

  1. As you lift the cooler, keep each pad with its location. Thickness often differs from spot to spot on the same card.
  2. Measure each removed pad with the caliper at an edge that sat outside the contact patch. Pads compress in service, so the squashed middle reads thinner than the pad really was.
  3. Replace like-for-like. Pads are sold in 0.5 mm steps, commonly 0.5 to 3.0 mm. When you must choose between two sizes, a softer pad in the thicker size conforms better than a hard pad.
  4. If a pad crumbled and cannot be measured, measure the gap instead: with the cooler resting in place, use the caliper’s depth rod or a feeler gauge (a set of thin metal blades of known thickness) to find the distance between the component’s top and the cooler’s raised landing above it. That distance is the pad thickness the card was built with.

Output ports

Work every port before condemning one: swap cables, swap the monitor input, and look into the port with a light. Pocket lint and dust pack into HDMI and DisplayPort sockets and can be teased out with a wooden toothpick and isopropyl alcohol, powered off. A port with bent pins or a cracked housing is a soldered component: replacing it is hot-air soldering work (Level 2 territory), and many repair shops will replace a single port for far less than a card costs.

The 16-pin connector: the documented record

The RTX 40-series and later high-end cards take power through one 16-pin plug — 12VHPWR: twelve power pins and four sense pins, rated for up to 600 watts through a connector smaller than the plugs it replaced. Its failure record is not folklore; it is documented, and you should know it before you trust or blame one.

What NVIDIA said. After owners reported melted plugs on RTX 4090 cards in the autumn of 2022, NVIDIA published its findings on November 18, 2022: it was aware of “about 50 cases globally,” and “a common issue is that connectors are not fully plugged into the graphics card.” It recommended plugging the power cable into the card before installing the card, and promised an expedited replacement process regardless of which cable or card was used. That is the manufacturer’s own statement, and the prevention advice in it is worth following — it is step 1 of the diagnosis order above.

What independent testing found. GamersNexus, an independent test lab and publication, ran a failure analysis and could reproduce melting with a partly inserted plug, made worse by debris inside the connector and by sideways load on the cable; they also criticized how little margin for error the design leaves the user. Treat their work as what it is — careful secondary analysis, not the manufacturer’s word and not a regulator’s finding.

The design was revised. The standards body (PCI-SIG) revised the socket in its PCIe CEM 5.1 specification: the revised connector, called 12V-2x6, shortens the sense pins so a partly seated plug should receive no power at all. Melted connectors have still been reported on newer cards. No manufacturer and no regulator has published an overall failure rate for the connector, so anyone who quotes you one is inventing it.

The recall. On February 8, 2024, the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC — the federal product-safety regulator) announced recall 24-112: CableMod’s angled 12VHPWR adapters, versions 1.0 and 1.1 in 90-degree and 180-degree forms, about 25,300 units. The firm had received 272 reports of the adapters becoming loose, overheating, and melting into the GPU, with at least $74,500 in property-damage claims in the United States. The CPSC classed them as fire and burn hazards and instructed consumers to stop using them and contact CableMod for removal instructions and a refund. If you own one of these adapters, that instruction still stands.

Prevention, in order: push the plug fully home — no visible gap, latch engaged — seating it into the card before the card goes into the case; do not bend the cable sharply right at the plug (give it a gentle run of cable first; cable makers commonly specify about 35 mm before any bend); use the cable that came with your power supply or the card’s own adapter, not third-party angled adapters; and re-check the plug after moving the PC.

If yours already melted: the connector on the card is a soldered part, and replacing it is routine work for a board-level repair shop — you do not automatically need a new card. One U.S. repair shop reported fixing on the order of a hundred melted-connector RTX 4090s a month (a technician-reported figure, carried by Tom’s Hardware); whatever the exact number, a melted plug is a repair, not an obituary.

Level 2: board-level repair — what it actually involves

This level uses a multimeter (a handheld meter that measures voltage, resistance, and continuity) and hot-air soldering equipment. It is learnable, and it is also exactly what a good independent shop does — so even if you never pick up the tools, this section tells you what you are paying for and what questions to ask.

Start with eyes and nose. A burnt smell, a discolored patch on the board, or a power component with a crater blown in it localizes the failure before any meter comes out.

Finding a short on the 12-volt rail. The card is fed 12 volts from the slot and from the external plug. That power goes to the VRM (voltage regulator module — the circuitry that converts 12 volts down to roughly the 1 volt the GPU die runs on). The VRM works in parallel slices called phases; each phase pairs a driver with power switches (MOSFETs — the switching transistors), often combined in one DrMOS chip. Our datasheet library carries the NCP302150 DrMOS (datasheet, hosted copy) as a worked example: eight of them feed the GPU core on one RTX 4080 model. With the card out of the PC and unpowered, measure resistance between the 12-volt input and ground: near-zero means something on that rail has failed short. The standard isolation method is to feed the shorted rail about 1 volt from a current-limited bench power supply and find what warms up — by fingertip, by a thermal camera, or by watching isopropyl alcohol evaporate off the hot part. The hot part is usually the failed phase’s DrMOS. Replace it with a hot-air station, re-measure, and only then power the card.

Memory faults. The pattern to recognize: on-screen artifacts (wrong pixels, repeating patterns, garbage textures) that appear under load, worsen as memory speed rises, and ease when you deliberately underclock the memory in software — a free test in tools such as MSI Afterburner — point at the GDDR memory chips, or their solder joints and power, rather than at the die. Confirming which chip is the slow part of this repair (see the locked drawer, below). Replacing a memory chip is hot-air BGA work (BGA — ball grid array: the chip connects through a grid of tiny solder balls underneath it), done on a rework station with a controlled temperature profile.

Corrupted BIOS. The card’s BIOS (its built-in start-up firmware, held on a small flash chip on the board — on the RTX 4080 example above, a Winbond W25Q16JW serial flash: datasheet, hosted copy) can be corrupted by an interrupted flash or a failing chip, leaving a card that will not start. If your card has a dual-BIOS switch (a tiny switch near the top edge, fitted to some models), flip it to the backup and you are done. Otherwise: boot the PC using the motherboard’s integrated graphics or a second card for picture, back up whatever BIOS is on the card (GPU-Z can save it), and re-flash the exact stock BIOS for your card model with the public flashing tool (NVFlash). Exact model matters — a file meant for a different card can brick yours — and the stock file works because it carries the manufacturer’s valid signature (see the locked drawer).

Ports and small parts. Soldered output ports, fan headers, and the 16-pin power connector itself are all replaceable at this level, one component at a time — the opposite of the replace-the-whole-card answer you will get from the manufacturer.

The locked drawer: what NVIDIA does not publish

Everything above is doable today. It is slower and more expensive than it should be, for one documented reason: the manufacturer keeps the repair documentation and the factory diagnostics to itself.

As of November 2026, NVIDIA publishes drivers, installation guides, and specification sheets for its cards. It does not publish, for any GeForce card: a schematic (the circuit diagram of the board), a boardview file (the component map a technician loads to find every part and test point), a service manual, or a spare-parts list — and it sells no parts, not even fans or power connectors, to owners or to independent repair shops. The data-center line is no more open: as our datasheet index records, the public datasheet for the H100 is a two-page specification table, and the detailed documentation sits behind non-disclosure agreements.

The factory test software is in the same drawer. NVIDIA’s own diagnostics — the platform repair technicians know as MODS, and its memory tester as MATS — can isolate a memory fault to a single chip in minutes. They circulate only inside NVIDIA’s authorized service channel and are neither sold nor licensed to the public. That is the honest reason the memory diagnosis in Level 2 is slow: technicians work by comparison, by heating and cooling suspect chips, and by experience, doing in hours what the factory tool does in minutes.

Leaked copies of the diagnostics, and leaked boardview and schematic files, exist in the wild. BareBoard does not link to or host leaked or confidential material — that is a standing rule of this site (what we post, and why we may) — so this page names the gap instead of filling it with stolen goods. An unverifiable tool from an unknown source is also, on its own merits, not something to run against a customer’s hardware.

Firmware is signed. NVIDIA’s GPUs check a cryptographic signature on their firmware, and the open-source driver project’s documentation records that, since the Maxwell generation, the signed firmware images a card demands can only come from NVIDIA. The practical results for owners: modified firmware is effectively locked out, and the BIOS recovery in Level 2 works precisely because the manufacturer’s stock file carries a valid signature.

None of this makes repair impossible. It makes board repair slower, and it is why independent shops keep bins of donor boards — dead cards bought for their parts. When a shop quotes you real labor to find one bad memory chip, the locked drawer is what you are paying for.

Buying used: the counterfeit record

The used market works, and most used cards are honest. But the fraud patterns are documented well enough that you should know the three main ones before you buy.

Painted memory (2023). Investigators Iskandar Souza and Paulo Gomes of TecLab documented ex-mining cards — cards run hard around the clock in cryptocurrency mining rigs — being sold as new after their GDDR memory chips were painted to hide the yellowing that sustained heat leaves on used chips. Scraping and heating the coating in their tests revealed older, mismatched markings underneath. Tells: screws showing tool wear on a “new” card, broken or missing seals, and memory chips whose printing looks oddly uniform or wrong for the model.

Relabeled cards. In 2025 a repair technician documented cards sold as RTX 4090s that were actually modified RTX 3080 and 3090 boards — dies re-marked to read as the newer chip, in one case fitted with fake memory. Separately, repair shops have received “4090s” whose GPU die and memory chips had been harvested off the board entirely and sold on, leaving a card that can never work. And workshops openly rebuild ex-mining cards into configurations the factory never sold — RTX 3080s rebuilt with 20 GB of memory, for instance — which is legitimate when disclosed and fraud when the card is sold as a factory product.

How to check a used card on arrival:

  1. Buy through a channel with returns, and test the card immediately, inside the return window.
  2. Run GPU-Z (free, from TechPowerUp). Since version 2.12 it flags a known class of counterfeits — old chips whose BIOS lies about what they are — with a [FAKE] tag. It does not catch everything: it will not catch a harvested board or every re-marked die. So also compare the die name, memory type, and memory size it reports against the specification for your exact model.
  3. Run a sustained load test and watch for artifacts, crashes, and abnormal temperatures.
  4. If the card produces no picture at all, stop and return it. “It just needs a driver” is not an explanation for a card with no output — harvested boards exist, and the return window is shorter than the argument.

The supply-chain record: variants and smuggling cases

Some of the confusion in the GPU market is policy-made, and it is on the public record. Two threads matter to a buyer or repairer.

Export rules created the variants. In October 2022 the U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS — the Commerce Department office that writes export rules) restricted exports of advanced GPUs to China. NVIDIA answered with cut-down China-market variants, the A800 and H800, with their chip-to-chip interconnect speed reduced to fit under the rule. On October 17, 2023, BIS rewrote the test — measuring performance density instead of interconnect speed — which brought the A800 and H800, and the RTX 4090, inside the controls; NVIDIA’s own securities filing that day listed the covered products. The same pattern later repeated with the H20. For a repair reader the practical point is this: a China-market variant is not a counterfeit. It is a different product, with different firmware and a different warranty reality, and it should be bought and priced as one.

Smuggling is a court record, not a rumor. In United States v. Greg Lui (U.S. District Court for the Central District of California, No. 2:26-cr-00618), a federal indictment filed September 29, 2026 alleges that Lui’s company, Earthmade Computer, moved more than $300 million worth of servers containing export-controlled GPUs — the indictment names A100 and H100, RTX 4090, and RTX 5090 products — through Malaysia and Singapore to China on false paperwork. The Justice Department announced his arrest on October 1, 2026. The charges are conspiracy to violate the Export Control Reform Act, outbound smuggling, and conspiracy to commit money laundering. An indictment is an allegation, not a verdict: the defendant is presumed innocent, and nothing in that case has been proven in court. The case is recorded here because it is the documented end of the same supply chain that produces the gray-market cards described above — not because any individual card you meet came from it.

The backdoor question: what is and is not on the record

Status: UNVERIFIED. Claims that NVIDIA GPUs contain a built-in backdoor or remote kill switch are widely repeated and are not supported by published evidence. Here is the whole of the actual record, both sides stated.

Documented: On July 31, 2025, the Cyberspace Administration of China (China’s internet regulator) summoned NVIDIA and asked it to explain alleged backdoor security risks in its H20 chips and to submit supporting documents. That summons is a real regulatory act, reported at the time by Reuters and others; it is an allegation by a regulator, not a technical finding.

Documented: Separately, a U.S. legislative proposal (the Chip Security Act) would require location-verification technology in export-controlled chips — that is, it proposes adding a tracking mechanism by law, openly, in the future. A proposal to add a mechanism is evidence that the mechanism is not already there.

Documented: NVIDIA’s answer is in writing. On August 5, 2025 its chief security officer published: “There are no back doors in NVIDIA chips. No kill switches. No spyware,” and “NVIDIA GPUs do not and should not have kill switches and backdoors.”

Not documented: As of this writing, no regulator, court filing, or independent laboratory has published physical or technical evidence of a backdoor or kill switch in an NVIDIA GPU. Claims that one exists — from any direction — remain unverified, and this page will say so until evidence, rather than statements, appears.

Your legal ground

Repairing your own card — opening it, renewing paste and pads, replacing fans, and board-level work — stands on the same federal ground as everything else on this site: the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act limits a manufacturer’s ability to void your warranty for servicing your own property, and the DMCA §1201 repair exemptions cover the circumvention needed to diagnose and repair lawfully acquired devices. The full citations, state laws, and the escalation playbook for when a manufacturer says no are on The Shield: right-to-repair law; if a warranty claim is refused because you opened the card, the template letters are in the repair demand letter page.
