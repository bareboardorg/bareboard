    Bench Notes

    Short observations from the workbench. No fluff, no filler — just things worth knowing.

    115200 is almost always the answer

    If you’re hunting for a serial console and getting garbage, try 115200 first. It’s the default baud rate on the overwhelming majority of embedded Linux devices, routers, and IoT gadgets. The runner-ups are 57600 and 9600, but 115200 hits more often than everything else combined.

    TX idles high — use it

    Not sure which pad is TX? Power the board and probe with a multimeter. UART TX idles at logic HIGH (3.3V or 1.8V). The pin sitting at a steady rail voltage while the board boots is almost certainly TX. RX idles high too but won’t show boot chatter on a scope.

    Don’t connect VCC on your serial adapter

    The board powers itself. Your USB-to-serial adapter powers itself. Connecting VCC between them joins two power supplies that were never meant to meet. You only need three wires: TX, RX, GND. Leave VCC unconnected.

    The FCC ID is a cheat code

    Every wireless device sold in the US has an FCC ID printed on it. Look it up at fccid.io and you’ll find internal photos, test reports, block diagrams, and sometimes full schematics — all legally public. It’s the single best free intelligence source in hardware hacking.

    Your boards are tougher than you think (but ground yourself anyway)

    Modern chips have decent ESD protection. You’re unlikely to kill a board by touching it. But “unlikely” isn’t “never” — a $5 grounding strap is cheaper than a dead SoC. Ground yourself, especially in dry environments.

    Read the datasheet before you probe

    Five minutes with the pinout diagram saves an hour of guessing. The datasheet tells you which pins are power, which are ground, and which ones will release magic smoke if you short them. Our datasheet index is a good place to start.
