    Raspberry Pi Family

    The single-board computer that put a Linux box in every hacker’s drawer. Over 60 million units sold — and a supply chain saga that tested the community’s loyalty.

    FCC ID

    FCC IDs by model (grantee code 2ABCB = Raspberry Pi). Check the label on your board and look it up at fccid.io:

    • FCC ID: 2ABCB-RPI5 — Raspberry Pi 5
    • FCC ID: 2ABCB-RPI4B — Raspberry Pi 4 Model B
    • FCC ID: 2ABCB-RPI3BP — Raspberry Pi 3 Model B / B+
    • FCC ID: 2ABCB-RPI0W — Raspberry Pi Zero / Zero W / Zero 2 W
    • FCC ID: 2ABCB-RPI400 — Raspberry Pi 400
    • FCC ID: 2ABCB-PICOW — Raspberry Pi Pico W

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    What you can typically find:

    • Internal photos — board layout, chip placement, shielding, antenna design
    • Block diagrams — sometimes included, showing the RF chain architecture
    • Test reports — conducted and radiated emissions data, which can reveal operating frequencies and power levels
    • Label placement — where the FCC ID lives on the device

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (usually withheld under confidentiality requests), firmware, bill of materials, or debug interface documentation.

    What we reveal

    The FCC tells you what a device emits. We tell you what’s inside and how it works.

    • Chip-level identification — every major IC named, with datasheet links where public documentation exists
    • Board mapping — what connects to what, which test pads matter, where the debug interfaces hide
    • What the manufacturer won’t tell you — undocumented features, hidden radios, silent telemetry, repair blockers

    Key Chips

    • Broadcom BCM2835/2837/2711/2712 — the application processors. Broadcom does not publish full public datasheets; documentation comes via Raspberry Pi’s own releases.
    • RP2040 / RP2350 — Raspberry Pi’s in-house microcontrollers. Fully documented — RP2040 datasheet (PDF) is a model of open hardware documentation.
    • Wireless: Cypress/Infineon CYW43455 (Pi 3B+, 4), CYW43439 (Pi Zero 2W) for Wi-Fi/Bluetooth.

    FCC IDs vary by model — see the list above. Check the label on your board and fccid.io for your specific revision.

    Controversies & Legal

    The 2021 price increase

    In October 2021, Raspberry Pi announced its first-ever price increase: the 2GB Raspberry Pi 4 went from $35 to $45, citing semiconductor supply chain costs. CEO Eben Upton stressed the change was temporary and that “in the entire history of Raspberry Pi, we have never increased the price of a product.” The 1GB variant was reintroduced at $35 as an alternative.

    Sources: TechSpot, Engadget

    Supply chain shortages (2021–2023)

    Global chip shortages hit Raspberry Pi hard. Production was capped at ~7 million units in 2021 (flat vs. 2020), with severe shortages of the Pi Zero and 2GB Pi 4. By 2022, scalpers were selling Pi 4 boards at 3× retail on Amazon. Approved resellers imposed quantity limits and anti-scalping measures. Supply did not meaningfully recover until 2023.

    Broadcom dependency

    Every full-size Raspberry Pi depends on a Broadcom SoC — a company famous for NDA-gated documentation and limited public datasheets. The community has long noted the tension between Raspberry Pi’s open-source ethos and its reliance on closed Broadcom silicon. The in-house RP2040/RP2350 microcontrollers, with fully public datasheets, represent Raspberry Pi moving toward greater openness.

    Commercial prioritization

    During shortages, Raspberry Pi prioritized industrial/commercial customers (Compute Module) over hobbyist retail, drawing criticism from the maker community that had built the brand. The company argued that commercial contracts kept the lights on during the crisis.
