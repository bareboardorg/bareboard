Q-Logic, Translated

Thirty-nine Quantum Rehab chairs in this site’s census run Q-Logic electronics — and until now they had no fault lesson, because nobody had translated Q-Logic into plain language. This is that translation: what the screen shows, what it means, and what you check first.

Q-Logic is not one box. It is a system: a hand control (the joystick unit in front of you, with the screen), a power module (the box in the base that drives the motors — Quantum’s manuals also call it the powerbase module), and bus cables (the cables the parts use to talk to each other). When something fails, the hand control is where the chair tells you.

Provenance: compiled from the manufacturers’ own published documents — Quantum Rehab’s Q-Logic 3 Basic Operation Instructions (INFMANU4809), its Q-Logic 2 Basic Operation Instructions (INFMANU4499), the published Q-Logic 3 Fault List, and the Q6 Edge 2.0 owner’s manual held in this site’s library — and checked against them. Codes that could not be confirmed in published material are not listed. Documentation, not bench measurement: if your chair disagrees with this page, the chair wins — write down what yours actually does.

Before any check in these tables that touches a plug, cable, motor, brake, actuator, or battery terminal: put the chair on level ground, switch it OFF, and disconnect the main battery harness or trip the main circuit breaker. Do not do the check while seated in the chair. Connector pins can remain live after switch-off. Keep a disconnected cable from touching skin, jewelry, tools, or metal, and cover or restrain it so the pins cannot short. At a battery terminal, work on one terminal at a time with an insulated tool. If you cannot disconnect power, inspect visually only and stop.

The manufacturer’s own warning, carried word for word from the Q6 Edge 2.0 owner’s manual: “Do not use the power chair while the drive motors are disengaged! Do not disengage the drive motors when the power chair is on an incline, as the unit could roll on its own! Only engage the freewheel mode when on a level surface.”

And its mandatory instruction on the same levers: “You must turn off the power before disengaging the drive motors; otherwise, you may get an error code on the controller. To clear this code, turn off the controller and place the power chair in drive mode. Then, turn on the controller. The error message should be cleared. If it is not, contact your Quantum Rehab Provider.” Keep that sequence. Half the “faults” on this page are that sequence done out of order.

What Q-Logic is, and which chairs have it

Quantum Rehab is the complex-rehab division of Pride Mobility Products. Its chairs are sold through providers, not off a shelf. Q-Logic is the electronics family Quantum fits to them: Quantum’s own parts books label the assemblies “Q-LOGIC, CURTIS” — the system is built by Curtis Instruments, the mobility-electronics maker whose own controller manuals carry its enAble name (see Curtis’s Model 1212 manual for the smaller enAble controllers). Quantum sells the chair and the support. Curtis built the brain.

Quantum’s own “What Can I Do with Q-Logic 3?” page lists Q-Logic 3 on the Edge 3 Stretto, Edge 3, 4Front, Q6 Edge 2.0, Q6 Edge 2.0 X, Q6 Edge HD, Q6 Edge Z and Q1450. The library holds the paper trail for the Q6 line: the Q6 Edge 2.0 owner’s manual, the Q6 Edge 3 Stretto parts book, the Q6 Edge 3 Illustrated Parts Breakdown, and the earlier 2012 Q6 Edge parts book from the Q-Logic 2 era — its joystick pages list Q-Logic 2 and Q-Logic 3 hand controls side by side, which is why both generations live on the same chairs.

Three words you need before the tables:

  • Motor 1 and Motor 2 are the two drive motors. Q-Logic does not promise that Motor 1 is the left one on your chair — identify a motor by its plug at the power module, not only by side.
  • Brake 1 and Brake 2 are the electric parking brakes, one inside each motor. A brake code is very often a freewheel lever (the lever on each motor that disconnects it so the chair can be pushed) left in the push position.
  • An encoder is the sensor that reports how far a motor has turned. An actuator is a powered seat motor (tilt, recline, elevate, legrests). An inhibit is a deliberate “do not drive” signal — the charger being plugged in is the common one.

Q-Logic 3 — read the words on the colour screen

The Q-Logic 3 hand control has a colour LCD screen (LCD = the flat display). It shows three kinds of message, in Quantum’s own definitions: Information (does not affect how the chair runs), Warning (may affect it), and Error (does affect it). Error codes appear on the screen by number, with a short text. The chair also warns you before a fault: pop-up screens appear when the battery falls to 50%, 30% and 15%, and if the electronics get too hot the chair slows itself down first (thermal rollback — a protective speed cut, not a breakdown).

One honest wrinkle: Quantum’s guides do not all number two joystick codes the same way — the Basic Operation Instructions print them as 34 and 35, the Fault List and the Q-Logic 3 Programming Guide as 35 and 36. The words on the screen are the same. Trust the words first, the number second, and write down both.

That is the owner’s table — the codes Quantum publishes a fix for. It is not the whole list. The full Fault List runs past #215, and most of it never reaches your screen with an explanation. That gap is the next section.

Q-Logic 2 — numbers on the LCD

Q-Logic 2 is the previous generation, fitted to earlier Q6 Edge chairs and many Quantum and Jazzy rehab chairs of its era. Its hand control also has an LCD screen, and it uses the same three message kinds — information, warning, error — with codes shown by number. Quantum’s Q-Logic 2 Basic Operation Instructions publish only the codes an owner can easily put right. They are, word for word in meaning:

Q-Logic 2 also has thermal rollback: if the motors or controller pass a safe temperature, the controller cuts output to 20% of full operation until things cool, then resumes on its own. A Q-Logic 2 chair that goes slow on a hot climb and recovers is doing its job.

Behind this short owner table sits a much longer shared list. The published Q-Logic 1 and 2 fault-code list runs from software faults at #1 through bus, joystick, battery, motor and brake faults past #200 — including entries whose remedy is a parameter change, and which mark that parameter, in the list’s own words, as “an OEM parameter.” You can read that list. You cannot act on most of it. That is the gate, in one document.

Q-Logic 1 and the simpler Curtis chairs — thin coverage, said plainly

Coverage here is thin, and this page will not pretend otherwise. For the original Q-Logic (the system the parts books call Q-Logic 1, on pre-2012 chairs such as the early Q6 Edge — see the 2012 parts book), no owner-level Basic Operation guide with an error table was found in this site’s library or in Quantum’s or Pride’s publicly posted manuals during this build. What is public is the shared Q-Logic 1 and 2 fault-code list above: its joystick codes (#35–#39), battery codes (#46, #47, #100–#105), motor codes (#49–#54, #74–#75) and brake codes (#81–#90) match the Q-Logic 2 owner table closely enough to use the same first checks — cable, connector, freewheel lever, charge. Its software faults (#1–#34) all carry the same published remedy, “update software,” which is a programmer job, not an owner job.

The simpler Curtis-built controls on Quantum and Jazzy chairs — the NE and NE+ hand controls listed in the Stretto parts book alongside Q-Logic — are a separate, non-expandable family. No publicly posted Quantum owner guide with a full NE/NE+ error table was held in the library or found on Quantum’s site for this page, so their codes are not translated here rather than guessed. If you have one of those chairs, the code, a photo of the display, and the chair’s serial number are what to take to a provider — and the missing guide is named in this page’s source notes as a collection target.

What the programmer sees that you don’t

Say it plainly: your screen shows you a number and one line. The programming device shows the full sentence, the history, and the settings behind it — and the programming device is not sold to you.

The evidence is in Quantum’s own owner guide. The Q-Logic 3 error table in the Basic Operation Instructions heads its detailed columns “Code Text (programming device)” and “Description Help text (programming device)”. The help text exists. It is labelled, in the owner’s manual, as belonging to the other tool.

That tool, for Q-Logic 3, is ECON: ECON-W on a Windows computer and ECON-i on an iPhone or iPad, both by Curtis Instruments, connecting by the chair’s built-in Bluetooth. Quantum UK’s ECON page describes it as a tool that allows “clinicians and service technicians” to customise and configure Q-Logic 3 chairs, with access to diagnostics and data logs. The ECON-i app’s own listing states its authorised users: original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) and approved dealers/providers, therapists, and wheelchair service technicians. The owner of the chair is not on the list. There is also a handheld programmer (HHP) — Quantum’s own programming-parameters article names the required equipment as “Handheld programmer (HHP), Econ-W or Econ-I” — and Quantum Canada’s Interactive Assist gives a provider a live remote connection to your chair’s electronics, including its error-code history, from their programming station. You cannot open that history yourself.

Two things are worth knowing inside the gate. First, that same Quantum article documents a real owner protection: Q-Logic 3 stores its programming files (XPA files — the chair’s whole setup as files) in the chair itself, with revisions kept, so a lost dealer copy is not the only copy. Ask, in those words, for your XPA files to be exported and handed to you before any module is swapped. Second, the generic Curtis programmers named on The Keys — the 1313 hand-held and 1314 PC Programming Station, each sold in User, Service, Dealer and OEM versions — are the wider Curtis tier system Q-Logic grew from. Each version works at its own level and every level below it. The version is the permission.

And the codes follow the same rule. The full Q-Logic 3 Fault List contains entries whose only published help text is an instruction to connect a programming device — for example #16 and #17 (brake shorted or programmed incorrectly: “Connect programming device. Check Brake Voltage Setting”) and #40 and #45 (encoder or input configuration that only a programming device can change). A code you can read but cannot clear is not a diagnosis you own. It is a ticket you are handed.

Owner checks before any module is condemned

Work this order. It is the order the codes themselves point to, and every step is in The Cheap Fixes.

  1. Freewheel levers (Procedure 1). Both levers firmly to DRIVE, power off and back on. Brake codes #3, #4, #25, #26 on Q-Logic 3 and the #81–#90 group on Q-Logic 2 start here.
  2. Charger and inhibit (Procedure 2). Charger unplugged from the controller, socket pins clean and straight. That clears Q-Logic 2 #107, explains Q-Logic 3 #75, and removes a whole family of “will not drive” calls.
  3. Battery pair (Procedure 3). Charge overnight, check both main plugs, terminal bolts and the main circuit breaker on the rear frame (push it back in if it has tripped, after a minute’s rest — the owner’s manual procedure). Load-test each battery. Q-Logic lives on a 24-volt pair: one dying battery drags the other down, and Quantum’s rule is to replace both at the same time, never mix old and new. Undervoltage, overvoltage, main-relay and half the “random” codes live here.
  4. Bus and motor cables (Procedure 4). With power disconnected: hand control cable, 9-pin D-sub, motor and brake plugs at the power module, encoder cables, seat module cables — unplug, inspect for bent pins, corrosion and pinches, reconnect firmly, one at a time. Communication codes (#204, Q-Logic 2 bus faults) and motor-not-connected codes are cable codes until the cable has been proved good.
  5. Joystick at power-up. Nothing resting on the stick, hands off, then switch on. That is the whole fix for the not-centered codes on both generations.

If the code survives all five, you have not failed — you have done the diagnosis. Write the code, the exact screen words, and the date down, and photograph the screen. The owner’s manual itself tells you what a provider will ask for: “model number, serial number, nature of the problem, and the error code if available.” Hand them all four. A chair that “powers up, but does not move” with code #3 recorded is a brake-lever conversation. The same chair with no code recorded is a module quote.

Before you call anyone

Read the screen out loud. Write the number and the words. Try the owner checks in order. Then call with the code in your hand — and ask for two things before any part is ordered: the fault history read out before it is cleared, and your XPA programming files exported to you. Both are button-presses in the dealer’s tool. Neither costs them a part.
