    The 16-pin connector that keeps melting

    Four years after RTX 4090 owners first reported melted power plugs, the same failure is back — and it is no longer only a flagship problem. In November 2026 alone, more than ten melted GeForce 16-pin connectors were reported, spanning the RTX 5090 down to the RTX 5070, and even an RTX 4090. This page is the failure analysis: what melts, why it melts, how to inspect your own connector in thirty seconds, and when a melted card or power supply can still be saved. It complements our GPU Repair hub, which carries the full documented record back to 2022 and the CPSC recall — this page is the November 2026 evidence and the inspection procedure.

    What the connector is

    High-end NVIDIA cards since the RTX 40 series take power through one 16-pin plug. The original version is called 12VHPWR: twelve power pins carrying 12 volts, plus four small sense pins that tell the card what the cable can deliver. It is rated for up to 600 watts through a plug smaller than the 6- and 8-pin plugs it replaced. The revised version, 12V-2x6, arrived with the ATX 3.1 power supply standard in September 2023. The revision shortened the sense pins, so a plug that is not fully seated should receive no power at all — a fail-safe against the exact failure that melted the first plugs. The November 2026 cases show the failure mode is not gone.

    What melts, and why

    What melts is the plastic housing of the plug and socket, and sometimes the metal pins inside it. The cause is heat from electrical resistance at a bad contact. The chain works like this:

    1. A pin does not make full contact. The plug is not fully seated, a retaining clip is broken, debris is in the socket, or the cable pulls sideways on the plug.
    2. Less contact means more resistance. The same current forced through a smaller contact area heats that spot.
    3. Heat softens the plastic. The housing deforms, the contact gets worse, the heat rises further. The loop feeds itself until the plastic melts and, in the worst cases, chars.

    The key point is that this is a contact failure, not a wattage failure. A 5070-class card draws far less power than a 5090, but if only one or two pins are carrying the load, those pins overheat anyway. That is why the November cases include lower-power cards, and why “only 5090s at 600 watts” was never the full story.

    The November 7, 2026 case makes the mechanism concrete. A Galax RTX 4080 SG owner found melted plastic and carbon deposits on the 12VHPWR housing after freezes and black screens in Cyberpunk 2077. The retaining clips were broken on both the cable plug and a Cooler Master MWE Gold V2 1050W ATX 3.1 power supply. With nothing holding the plug firmly home, vibration shifted it, the contact area shrank, and the connector heated locally until it melted. The card had been warning the owner first — freezes and black screens under load are the symptoms of a connector going bad, and they should be treated as a stop-and-inspect signal, not a driver problem.

    The November 2026 case list

    VideoCardz documented more than ten melted GeForce power connector and cable cases shared in November 2026 alone, drawn from Reddit, Weibo, Taiwanese forums, Facebook, and the Thermal Grizzly community. The spread:

    • RTX 5090 and 5090 D — melted GPU sockets and melted adapters.
    • RTX 5080, 5070 Ti, and 5070 — melted connectors on cards well below flagship power draw.
    • RTX 4090 — the original melting card, still appearing in the case list four years on.
    • Damage on both ends — cases include melted GPU sockets, melted adapters, and damaged power-supply-side connectors. A melted PSU-side connector means the power supply itself is part of the failure, not just the cable.

    No manufacturer and no regulator has published an overall failure rate for the connector, so anyone who quotes you one is inventing it. What the case list does prove is that the failure is ongoing, that it spans the whole RTX 50 stack, and that the 12V-2x6 revision did not end it.

    The thirty-second inspection

    Do this with the PC switched off and unplugged. Do it when you first install a card, after moving the PC, and any time the card crashes or black-screens under load.

    1. Look for a gap. The plug must sit fully home in the socket, with no visible gap between the plug body and the socket. A gap means it is not seated — push it in firmly and evenly until it is flush.
    2. Listen for the latch click. The plug has a retaining latch (a small clip that snaps over a tab on the socket). When the plug seats, the latch clicks. No click, no seat. If the clip is broken — on the plug or on the socket — the plug can walk out with vibration, exactly as in the November 7 case. A broken clip is a failed connector: replace the cable, and if the socket’s clip is broken, have the socket replaced.
    3. Check the bend. Do not bend the cable sharply right at the plug. Give it a gentle run of cable first; cable makers commonly specify about 35 mm before any bend. A sharp bend at the plug pulls the pins sideways and unseats them.
    4. Look and smell. Brown discoloration, softened or shiny plastic, a burnt smell, or black carbon deposits mean the connector has already overheated. Stop using the card. This is a fire hazard, not a quirk.
    5. Seat the cable before the card. NVIDIA’s own guidance, from its November 2022 statement, is to plug the power cable into the card first, firmly and evenly, before installing the card into the machine. It is easier to seat the plug properly when the card is in your hands than when it is wedged in a case.
    6. Use the right cable. Use the cable that came with your power supply, or the card’s own adapter. Do not use third-party angled adapters: the CableMod angled 12VHPWR adapters were recalled by the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission on February 8, 2024 (recall 24-112, about 25,300 units, 272 reports of loosening, overheating, and melting). If you own one, the CPSC instruction still stands: stop using it.

    When a card or power supply is salvageable

    A melted connector is a repair, not an obituary — but only if the damage stopped at the connector.

    • Card socket melted, board otherwise fine: the 16-pin socket is a soldered part, and replacing it is routine work for a board-level repair shop. One U.S. repair shop reported fixing on the order of a hundred melted-connector RTX 4090s a month. Do not power the card again until the socket is replaced and the board is checked for heat damage to nearby parts.
    • Only the cable plug melted: replace the cable with the power supply maker’s own cable for that exact model. Then inspect the card’s socket closely — a melted plug usually leaves heat damage in the socket too.
    • PSU-side connector damaged: replace the power supply, not just the cable. A damaged PSU-side connector means the power supply’s own socket has been overheated, and it cannot be trusted to hold contact again. Never open the power supply itself — it stores lethal charge even unplugged.
    • Not salvageable: if the heat spread into the board — charred circuit board, melted solder on nearby components, or a burnt smell from the board itself rather than just the connector — the repair gets expensive fast, and a shop should quote it before you commit. A card that still black-screens after a socket replacement has damage beyond the connector.
