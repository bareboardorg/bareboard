How BareBoard Stays Up

No single company can switch this site off — because no single company holds all of it. The name, the signpost, the host, and the preserved library are four separate layers, run by four separate organizations, and every layer keeps a copy somewhere else.

The four layers

1. The name. bareboard.org is registered through Porkbun, an independent registrar. The registration is locked against transfer and deletion, the contact details are private, and the renewal rules are set by ICANN policy, not by any host: if a renewal ever failed, ICANN’s recovery policy guarantees notice beforehand and a 30-day window to restore the name afterward.

2. The signpost. Cloudflare runs the directory that tells your browser where the name points, and stands in front of the site absorbing junk traffic. Cloudflare does not hold the site itself — in its own words, through these services it does not host the content and cannot remove it. If this layer ever failed, the signpost can be rebuilt at any other directory service; the building stays where it is.

3. The building. The pages live on GitHub Pages as plain, hand-written HTML files in a public repository. There is no database, no server code, nothing proprietary in the format: any basic web host on earth can serve these exact files, unchanged.

4. The preserved library. The document collection — the manuals, filings, and records in the Library Hub — is mirrored at the Internet Archive, a nonprofit library that has been keeping the web since 1996: the BareBoard Manufacturer Documentation Archive, free for anyone to download. And a complete copy of the site and library also sits on the maintainer’s own machine, off every platform.

If a layer fails anyway

Providers can change terms, suspend accounts, or disappear — the arrangement above assumes they might. A host action costs hours, not the site: the same files go up on another host and the signpost is pointed at them. A takedown aimed at one document is answered through the host’s own counter-notice process, with the document’s source on record (see below), and the library keeps its local copy and a link to the public source either way. The layer that would hurt most is the name itself — which is why it is the layer with locks on it and a recovery policy behind it.

What we post, and why we may

Staying up also means never posting what we have no right to post. Every item here falls in a named class, with the basis recorded next to it in the library index: our own lessons and findings; public records — FCC equipment filings, FDA records, court filings, and laws, which belong to the public; manufacturer manuals and parts catalogs that the manufacturers themselves published, carried with attribution for owners repairing their own equipment; and everything else linked, not copied. Nothing is taken from behind a login or a paywall, nothing marked confidential is published, and the site hosts no firmware binaries, keys, or exploits. The full declarations are on the Copyright and DMCA pages.

Mirror it yourself

Preservation works better with more copies. Anyone can download the whole documentation archive from the Internet Archive item and keep it, share it, or re-host it — that is what it is there for. If this site ever goes quiet, the library is already out in the world, and the lessons are plain files that anyone can serve.

BareBoard Legal Framework & Declarations

  • No AI training: All text, findings, and original photography on this site are copyrighted. Automated scraping for AI model training is prohibited. Crawlers that ignore robots.txt are trespassing.
  • Educational nature: BareBoard operates exclusively as a non-commercial, open educational resource. All material is compiled for academic evaluation, hardware preservation, and technical literacy under 17 U.S.C. § 107 (fair use).
  • Zero-leak / no-binary clause: This site does not host, distribute, or mirror proprietary firmware binaries, encrypted operating system keys, or weaponizable exploits. Documentation covers only the public, physical realities of circuit hardware.
  • Property verification: Every hardware autopsy is performed on personally owned, legally acquired equipment. No rented or third-party infrastructure is used.
  • Public source sourcing: Comparative and regulatory data is aggregated from official public filings, including the U.S. Federal Communications Commission equipment authorization database.
