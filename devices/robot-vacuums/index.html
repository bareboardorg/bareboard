    Robot Vacuums

    A floor computer that maps your home to clean it. Two sensor religions (camera and laser), one brush-and-fan drivetrain, and a record that runs from leaked living-room photos to the bankruptcy of the company that invented the category.

    FCC ID

    FCC IDs vary by brand and model — iRobot, Roborock, Ecovacs, and Shark file under their own grantee codes for Wi-Fi/Bluetooth (and, on dock-equipped models, sometimes a second radio in the dock). The internal photos show what the marketing hides: the navigation sensor stack and how much of the robot is dustbin.

    Every radio a device carries — Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, ultra-wideband, NFC — needs an FCC equipment authorization before the device can be sold in the United States. To get one, the manufacturer files photographs and test reports with the FCC, and those filings are public record. Check the label on your specific unit and look it up at fccid.io.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    • Internal photos — the main board, the fan motor, the sensor turret (LiDAR models) or camera window (vSLAM models), and the battery pack’s true size
    • Test reports — radio duty cycles that explain why a vacuum is a polite network citizen and a mapping dock is not
    • The manual — filed publicly, including the maintenance schedule the makers themselves print (brushes, filters, wheels) — the closest thing to a service document the category publishes

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (withheld under confidentiality requests where the maker files them), firmware, and the full bill of materials.

    Exploded view — the assemblies in build order

    This is the centerpiece of the page: the device taken apart into its assemblies, in the order they go together, every part named in plain language with what it does. An exploded view is the drawing a manufacturer makes showing every part separated along the path it assembles — the single most useful document in repair, and the one most often kept behind a dealer login.

    The source

    Parts book: none published — but this category is a partial exception on parts: iRobot and Roborock both sell owner-replaceable modules (wheel modules, brush cages, batteries, even main boards on some Roomba generations slide out as units). No exploded diagram is published; the stack below is rebuilt from FCC internal photos and the makers’ own module-replacement guides, which are public.

    Coverage: PARTIAL — no public parts book or exploded diagram; FCC internal-photo basis + manufacturers’ published module-replacement guides. Uniquely for this wave: several assemblies below are genuinely sold as owner-swappable modules, and the table says which.

    Build order

    Step 1 — Chassis, bumper & cliff sensors

    The robot’s body is a sensor platform that happens to hold a vacuum.

    PartWhat this part does
    Bumper + infrared windowThe front bumper is a switch (bump sensing) behind an IR-transparent strip (wall-following and dock homing receivers). A scratched IR window presents as navigation failure.
    Cliff sensors (4–6, downward IR pairs)Infrared emitter/detector pairs that see the floor’s reflection vanish at a stair edge. Dust on these is the #1 cause of a robot that refuses to run ‘in the dark’ it imagines.
    Top deck: LiDAR turret or camera windowLiDAR models spin a laser rangefinder in a turret (a small motor + the LDS unit); camera (vSLAM) models look forward/up through a window. This part decides the robot’s whole navigation character.

    Step 2 — Drive & cleaning train

    Two drive wheels, a caster, brushes, and a fan. Every part here is a wear item and most are owner-replaceable modules on the major brands.

    PartWhat this part does
    Drive wheel modules (L/R)Spring-loaded motor + gearbox + treaded wheel, sold as slide-in modules on Roomba. A robot driving in circles has usually lost one.
    Main brush(es)Bristle or rubber extractors. Hair wrap is the design condition, not abuse; the rubber dual-roller designs exist to shed it.
    Side brushThe spinning whisk that sweeps edges into the main brush’s path. A consumable.
    Suction fan (vacuum motor)A high-speed blower. It is why the dustbin’s seal matters: leaks upstream of the fan are suction lost forever.
    Dustbin + filterThe bin and its HEPA-style filter. A clogged filter is the most common ‘weak suction’ fault and the cheapest fix in the category.
    Mop module (combo models)A pad + water tank that clips under or behind the bin; on dock models the dock washes it.

    Step 3 — Electronics & battery

    One main board, one battery, and the radios that make the map leave the robot.

    PartWhat this part does
    Main board + SoCNavigation compute (camera models run real vision pipelines), motor drivers, and the radio. Board-level parts are not sold; boards sometimes are, as modules, on older Roombas.
    Battery packLi-ion, typically 1800–5200 mAh, screwed under a bottom plate — owner-replaceable on most major brands with a screwdriver, a fact worth naming because phones forgot it.
    Charging contacts & dock (base)Two metal pads mate with the dock. Auto-empty docks add a vacuum motor and a bag — a second appliance with its own consumables and its own failure modes.

    What is missing, in the open: schematics and any published exploded diagram. What exists — module sales and replacement guides — makes this category the least sealed in the smart home, and it still isn’t documented to the standard this site holds for a wheelchair. Both facts belong on the page.

    Pick a robot by how it sees; everything else follows.

    Key chips & components

    • Navigation SoCs — camera models run ARM application processors doing visual SLAM; specific parts are identified per model in FCC internal photos. Public datasheets for the exact SKUs are uncommon — collection targets logged per family.
    • Motor drivers & fan controllers — commodity power parts of the classes indexed in this site’s Datasheet Index (the same driver families that move the site’s stepper and DC projects).

    Faults — which part fails, and how you know

    Symptom first — this category’s faults are dominated by dirt, hair, and one famous wheel.

    • Refuses to start, flashes a cliff/dark error in daylight — dust film on the cliff sensors. Wipe the sensor windows before anything else; this is the category’s most misdiagnosed fault.
    • Drives in circles / one wheel’s tread gone — a drive wheel module: hair in the axle, a worn tread, or a dead gearmotor. On Roomba these are owner-swappable modules by design.
    • Weak suction, loud fan — clogged filter or a dustbin seal leak. The fan is fine; its air is escaping. Filter first, always.
    • Won’t dock / won’t charge — oxidized charging contacts (robot and dock), or the dock’s IR homing window blocked. Clean contacts with a melamine pad per the maker’s own guide.
    • Map wrong, rooms merged, robot ‘lost’ — a dirty LiDAR window or camera lens, or a moved charging dock (the map’s origin). Software resets fix maps; they do not fix dirty optics.
    • Error tones after a brush change — the brush cage isn’t latched or the end caps weren’t swapped onto the new brush. The robot is reporting a mechanical truth.

    Safety, before you open anything

    The battery lives under a screwed plate and is owner-replaceable on most major brands — replace it with the maker’s pack or a reputable equivalent, and recycle the old one. A swollen pack in a device that drives itself around your house is not a ‘monitor it’ situation.

    Auto-empty docks are mains appliances with their own motors and bags; unplug before clearing a dock clog. And a robot vacuum’s map of your home is sensitive data — know which account it lives in and what the maker’s retention policy says before you sell or scrap the robot (factory-reset it, like a phone).

    The full record — dated, sourced, nothing smoothed over

    The category’s record is a privacy record and a business record: what the mapping saw, and what happened to the company that mapped first.

    DateEventSource
    2019–2020iRobot development robots (specially modified test units in paid beta testers’ homes, cameras labeled and participants consented) capture images of home interiors, including a woman on a toilet. The images flow to data-labeling contractors for machine-learning training.MIT Technology Review investigation, Dec. 19, 2022
    2022-12-19MIT Technology Review publishes the screenshots — obtained from gig workers who labeled them — proving floor-robot camera data leaves the home and passes through human labelers. iRobot confirms the images came from test units, not customer robots, and says the labeling violated its data agreements.MIT Technology Review, Dec. 19, 2022; iRobot statement
    2022-08-05Amazon announces a $1.7 billion acquisition of iRobot — the mapping data of millions of homes valued as part of a platform deal, drawing immediate regulator and privacy-advocate scrutiny.Amazon/iRobot announcements
    2024-01-29Amazon and iRobot terminate the acquisition after the European Commission moves to block it; iRobot cuts roughly 31% of its workforce and its CEO departs. The FTC’s parallel review ends with the deal.iRobot/Amazon joint statement, Jan. 29, 2024
    2025-12-14iRobot files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy under a plan to sell the company to Picea Robotics — its own longtime contract manufacturer. The category’s inventor ends as a brand inside its supply chain.iRobot Chapter 11 filing, S.D.N.Y., Dec. 2025

    Did you know

    • A flagship robot’s most famous warranty is about pet waste: iRobot’s “P.O.O.P.” (Pet Owner Official Promise) guarantees a replacement if a j7 runs through pet waste in the first year — because obstacle avoidance is a data problem and everyone in the industry knows it.
    • The battery is held by screws and sold to owners: robot vacuums quietly kept the replaceable-battery norm that phones abandoned.
    • LiDAR models navigate perfectly in total darkness; camera models can be defeated by a dark room. The ‘smarter’ sensor is the one that needs your lights on.
