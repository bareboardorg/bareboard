    Texas right-to-repair law: what it does, and what it does not

    Texas has a right-to-repair law now. It took effect September 1, 2026, and it is the largest state consumer-electronics repair law yet — and the first led by a Republican legislature. If you live in Texas, it changes what a manufacturer must give you when your electronics break. But read the fine print: the law has a one-year grace period, it can require you to get remote permission or an internet connection, and — said plainly, because it matters most to this site’s readers — it does not cover wheelchairs or medical devices at all.

    What the law is

    HB 2963, the Texas consumer-electronics right-to-repair law, applies to consumer electronic products costing $50 or more, first sold after September 1, 2026. Manufacturers must make available, on fair and reasonable terms, the parts, tools, and documentation (manuals, schematics, diagnostics) needed to repair the product — to owners and to independent repair shops, not only to authorized dealers.

    Texas joins six other states with active consumer-electronics repair laws: New York, Minnesota, California, Colorado, Washington, Oregon, and Connecticut (effective July 1, 2026). Kansas has enacted its own law, applying only to equipment made for sale in Kansas from July 1, 2027.

    The loopholes: where the law does not bite yet

    The live debate about the Texas law is whether its exceptions swallow it. Three matter:

    • The one-year grace period. Manufacturers get up to a year after a product’s first sale before they have to provide the parts, tools, and documentation. A product that breaks in its first year — when failures are most common — may have no repair material available at all, legally.
    • Remote authorization. The law allows a manufacturer to require remote authorization or an internet connection before a repair can proceed. That is the software-lock problem in legal form: the repair still depends on the manufacturer’s server saying yes. If the server is down, or the product line is discontinued, the authorization may never come.
    • The product window. The law reaches products first sold after September 1, 2026. Everything already in people’s homes — the phone, laptop, or television bought in August — is outside it.

    What it does not cover: wheelchairs and medical devices

    This is the part that must be said plainly, because it is the part most likely to be misunderstood. HB 2963 is a general electronics law. Like the consumer-electronics repair laws in other states, it expressly excludes medical devices. A powered wheelchair is a medical device. So:

    • A Texas wheelchair owner gains nothing from HB 2963 for their chair. The dealer-only programmers, the locked firmware, and the parts restrictions on power mobility devices are untouched by this law.
    • Texas has no wheelchair right-to-repair law. Eight states do — Colorado, California, Maryland, Nevada, Oregon, Rhode Island, Tennessee, and Washington — but Texas is not one of them. The full list, with the statutes and their enforcers, is on our Shield page, and the Texas carve-out is noted in the law section of our PMD section.
    • What does protect a Texas wheelchair owner today is federal law: the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, and the DMCA §1201 repair exemptions — including the medical-device exemption that is under threat right now (see our DMCA 1201 explainer).

    The same exclusion applies to the other state electronics laws: they are written for phones, laptops, and appliances, not for medical devices. Do not let a headline about “right to repair in Texas” tell you your chair is covered. It is not.

    What to do with it

    • For electronics: if a product first sold after September 1, 2026 breaks and the maker refuses parts, tools, or documentation, ask in writing, cite HB 2963, and keep the refusal. If the maker claims the one-year grace period, note the date the product was first sold and the date the grace period ends.
    • For wheelchairs: use the federal ground and the demand-letter templates on our repair demand letter page, and the escalation ladder on The Shield.
