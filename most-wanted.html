The BBO Top 10 Most Wanted

The bounty board. These are the devices we most want on the teardown bench — ranked not by fame, but by how badly they need examining and how hard they are to find. When a device is acquired, it graduates off the board and the next one moves up.

The ranking

50%Rarity. How hard is it to find? Used-market scarcity, active listing counts, discontinued with no new stock. The harder to source, the higher the bounty.
30%Darkness. How little public research exists? No teardown, no FCC internal photos, no public documentation. Zero prior examination outranks everything except rarity itself.
20%Reach. Installed base × still in use. A rare device nobody owned matters less than a rare device millions once owned.

Score = 0.5 × Rarity + 0.3 × Darkness + 0.2 × Reach, each scored 0–10. Recomputed whenever the device universe updates or a bounty is claimed.

The board

  1. Humane AI Pin

    Sought for: Failed 2024 startup device, now disappearing from the market. Snapdragon-based wearable with almost no public board-level documentation.

    Why it matters: documents an entire product category's rise and fall at the silicon level.

    Priority: HIGH PRIORITY

  2. Rabbit R1

    Sought for: MediaTek-powered AI handheld. Little verified teardown data exists on its internals, power design, or component choices.

    Why it matters: independent examination of heavily marketed AI hardware.

    Priority: HIGH PRIORITY

  3. Apple Vision Pro

    Sought for: Dual-chip (M2 + R1) spatial computer. Apple publishes nothing about the board; independent analysis is scarce and the display stack is unique.

    Why it matters: the most complex consumer board of its generation, undocumented.

    Priority: HIGH PRIORITY

  4. Nintendo Switch 2

    Sought for: New generation hardware with unknown silicon revisions, board layout, and repairability characteristics.

    Why it matters: baseline documentation before revisions and cost-downs change the design.

    Priority: HIGH PRIORITY

  5. Meta Quest Pro

    Sought for: Discontinued headset with pancake-lens optics and Snapdragon XR2+ board. Units are vanishing; the display assembly deserves recording.

    Why it matters: preservation of a short-lived premium design.

    Priority: ELEVATED

  6. DJI Avata 2

    Sought for: FPV drone with OcuSync video pipeline and tight firmware lockdown.

    Why it matters: independent examination of video transmission hardware the manufacturer keeps closed.

    Priority: ELEVATED

  7. Amazon Astro

    Sought for: Home robot, rare in the wild, with an unknown sensor and compute stack.

    Why it matters: almost no public hardware analysis of a shipping home robot.

    Priority: ELEVATED

  8. Oura Ring 4

    Sought for: Extremely compact wearable; no public teardown of the current generation.

    Why it matters: miniaturization techniques worth studying and recording.

    Priority: ELEVATED

  9. Peloton Bike+

    Sought for: Subscription-linked exercise hardware.

    Why it matters: documents the “connected hardware” category where software decisions can limit hardware you own.

    Priority: STANDARD

  10. Steam Deck OLED

    Sought for: Valve handheld with some existing coverage.

    Why it matters: the HDR OLED panel supply chain and revised board layout deserve a complete record.

    Priority: STANDARD

BareBoard Legal Framework & Declarations

  • No AI training: All text, findings, and original photography on this site are copyrighted. Automated scraping for AI model training is prohibited. Crawlers that ignore robots.txt are trespassing.
  • Educational nature: BareBoard operates exclusively as a non-commercial, open educational resource. All material is compiled for academic evaluation, hardware preservation, and technical literacy under 17 U.S.C. § 107 (fair use).
  • Zero-leak / no-binary clause: This site does not host, distribute, or mirror proprietary firmware binaries, encrypted operating system keys, or weaponizable exploits. Documentation covers only the public, physical realities of circuit hardware.
  • Property verification: Every hardware autopsy is performed on personally owned, legally acquired equipment. No rented or third-party infrastructure is used.
  • Public source sourcing: Comparative and regulatory data is aggregated from official public filings, including the U.S. Federal Communications Commission equipment authorization database.
