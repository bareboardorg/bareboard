    Chromebooks

    The school-fleet computer — and the best first full teardown in computing. A million identical boards, firmware documentation Google actually publishes, and a write-protect switch you are allowed to flip.

    FCC ID

    FCC IDs vary by manufacturer and model — a Chromebook is built by Acer, HP, Lenovo, ASUS, Samsung, or Dell to Google’s platform designs, and each OEM files under its own grantee code. The filings matter here for the usual reason (internal photos of boards you will genuinely open) and one unusual one: the same reference design appears under many brands, so one filing can document several retail models.

    Every radio a device carries — Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, ultra-wideband, NFC — needs an FCC equipment authorization before the device can be sold in the United States. To get one, the manufacturer files photographs and test reports with the FCC, and those filings are public record. Check the label on your specific unit and look it up at fccid.io.

    What the FCC filing revealed

    The FCC filing for this device includes the documents manufacturers are required to submit: external photos, internal photos, test setup photos, RF test reports, and the user manual. These are public record at fccid.io.

    • Internal photos — small single boards: SoC, soldered or socketed storage (eMMC on most, NVMe on high-end models), one or two RAM configurations, and the battery spanning the chassis
    • Board names in the manual — every Chromebook board has a code name (the Chromium project names boards, not marketing); the filed manual and the project’s public docs use it
    • Test reports — Wi-Fi 6/6E radio configurations by region

    What the FCC filing does not include: schematics (withheld under confidentiality requests where the maker files them), firmware, and the full bill of materials.

    Exploded view — the assemblies in build order

    This is the centerpiece of the page: the device taken apart into its assemblies, in the order they go together, every part named in plain language with what it does. An exploded view is the drawing a manufacturer makes showing every part separated along the path it assembles — the single most useful document in repair, and the one most often kept behind a dealer login.

    The source

    Parts book: no single book — something better and worse. Google’s Chromium project publishes board-level documentation openly (board names, firmware design, recovery and write-protect mechanics), and several OEMs publish real service manuals for education models because school districts demanded them. But there is no universal Chromebook parts catalog: screens, keyboards, and batteries are model-specific and sourced by part number off the failed part itself. The exploded view below is the generic education-fleet board layout the FCC photos and Chromium docs agree on.

    Coverage: PARTIAL — no public universal parts book; firmware and board documentation is unusually public via the Chromium project, and that openness is cited per-step. Assembly coverage from FCC internal photos + published OEM service guides for education models.

    Build order

    Step 1 — Base, keyboard & battery

    A fleet Chromebook is a shallow plastic or aluminum base. The battery is usually the biggest part in it.

    PartWhat this part does
    Battery packA 2–3 cell lithium pack, screwed (fleet models) or glued (consumer models) to the base. The first part schools replace, and the first to swell.
    Keyboard deck / palmrestOn most fleet models the keyboard is part of the top cover — one spilled drink can mean a whole deck. Some education models make it separately replaceable; check before ordering.
    Speakers & I/O boardUSB-C charging + data on both sides on many models; a small daughterboard carries the side ports on some designs.

    Step 2 — Mainboard

    One board does everything. Its code name — printed on the board and used throughout the public documentation — is the key to every firmware and parts search you will ever run on the machine.

    PartWhat this part does
    SoC (Intel N-series / Core, MediaTek Kompanio, or Snapdragon)The processor. Fleet models favor low-power Intel (N100-class) or ARM (Kompanio); storage and RAM are soldered on most.
    eMMC / NVMe storageSoldered eMMC on budget models (32–64 GB is common and is a wear item); socketed NVMe appears on high-end models.
    Security chip (H1 / Titan C2)Google’s discrete security chip. It enforces verified boot and holds the firmware write-protect state — there is no write-protect screw on modern models; protection is electronic, released through the chip’s debug interface (CCD) or by disconnecting the battery on some designs.
    Wi-Fi moduleUsually a socketed M.2 card (Intel AX201-class) — one of the few genuinely swappable parts.
    Firmware flash (SPI ROM)The chip that holds coreboot — the open-source firmware most Chromebooks boot. Readable and writable with a clip programmer once write-protect is released.

    Step 3 — Display assembly

    The lid: panel, webcam, antennas, hinges.

    PartWhat this part does
    LCD panel (30-pin eDP)The panel connector is an industry-standard 30-pin embedded DisplayPort — panels cross between brands more often than any other Chromebook part. The panel’s own part number is the order key.
    Webcam & microphonesA small board at the top bezel; privacy shutters appear on newer fleet models.
    Wi-Fi antennasRouted into the lid for height — which is why a hinge-area pinched cable presents as a Wi-Fi fault.
    HingesThe fleet killer. Hinge mounts tear out of plastic lids; the fix is the lid or the deck, not the hinge.

    What is missing, in the open: schematics. Board documentation, firmware source (coreboot is open source), and recovery images are public; the schematic PDFs are not. That still makes this the most honestly documented mass-market computer line in existence — the floor every other platform on this page is measured against.

    Chromebooks are reference designs wearing brand badges. The families that matter for parts and firmware:

    FamilyTypical chipsHallmarksNotes

    ARM fleetMediaTek Kompanio 500/800 seriesFanless, long battery, soldered everythingLenovo/Duet-style tablets and clamshells.

    Key chips & components

    • Intel N-series / Celeron SoCs — Intel publishes datasheets for its embedded lines through its public documentation library; the N100-class is documented at family level. Datasheet status: indexed for related Intel parts in this site’s Datasheet Index; model-specific briefs are collection targets.
    • MediaTek Kompanio — product briefs public, full datasheets not. Collection target logged.
    • Google H1 / Titan C2 security chip — documented by the Chromium project’s public CCD (Case Closed Debugging) documentation; the chip is the write-protect authority on modern boards.
    • coreboot + depthcharge — the open-source firmware stack most Chromebooks ship; source and board documentation are public in the Chromium project — the only mass-market computer whose boot firmware you can read.

    Faults — which part fails, and how you know

    Fleet machines fail fleet ways. Symptom first:

    • Won’t update / ‘ChromeOS is missing or damaged’ — first check the model’s AUE date (the software end-of-life), then recovery from a USB image (Esc + Refresh + Power), then storage: a worn eMMC presents exactly like corruption.
    • Dead, no charge light — the USB-C charger and cable first (fleet chargers die constantly), then a battery-disconnect reset: on many models, unplugging the internal battery for 30 seconds clears a latched power state.
    • Keyboard rows or columns dead — liquid history. On deck-integrated models the honest fix is the top cover assembly; price it before promising a “keyboard repair.”
    • Screen black, machine clearly boots (sounds, caps light) — backlight or a pinched eDP cable at the hinge; an external monitor separates panel faults from board faults in one minute.
    • Swollen base, trackpad clicking itself — the battery. Stop, replace, recycle. Fleet swelling is the single most common Chromebook hardware failure.

    Safety, before you open anything

    The battery is screwed or glued under the palmrest you are leaning on. Punctures happen when a swollen pack is pried against the deck. Discharge first, no metal levers on the pack, and a swelling battery ends the repair until it is out.

    USB-C charging means the port carries 20 V and the board carries a charge controller that fails shorted on liquid damage. A board that smells burnt near the port is a board to diagnose unpowered, with a meter — not by plugging in chargers until one works.

    The full record — dated, sourced, nothing smoothed over

    The Chromebook record is the e-waste argument in product form — and the industry’s clearest proof that update policy, not hardware, is what kills computers.

    DateEventSource
    2010-12Google ships the Cr-48 pilot laptop: the first Chromebook, given away to testers. Verified boot and automatic updates are the platform’s founding ideas.Google/Chromium project record
    2016–2019Early models reach their Auto Update Expiration dates and stop receiving updates while fully functional — schools, the biggest buyers, are hit first and hardest.Google AUE policy list; US PIRG reporting
    2023-04US PIRG’s “Chromebook Churn” report calculates that doubling Chromebook lifespans would cut emissions and save US schools $1.8 billion — naming expiring software support, glued batteries, and parts scarcity as the mechanisms.US PIRG Education Fund, “Chromebook Churn,” April 2023
    2023-09-14Google announces 10 years of automatic updates for Chromebooks — for platforms released from 2021 onward, counted from platform release — the longest update commitment of any consumer OS at the time.Google announcement, Sept. 14, 2023
    2022-02Google launches a Chromebook repair program for schools with Acer, HP, and Lenovo, publishing repair guides for common models so district IT can fix fleets in-house.Google for Education, Feb. 2022

    Did you know

    • Your Chromebook’s real name is printed on its board: the Chromium board code name unlocks the firmware docs, the recovery image, and the parts cross-references — the marketing name unlocks none of them.
    • Most Chromebooks boot open-source firmware (coreboot). The most locked-down consumer OS runs on the most open boot stack in the industry.
    • The write-protect “screw” mostly doesn’t exist anymore: protection moved into the security chip, and the documented way past it is a debug protocol (CCD), not a screwdriver.
