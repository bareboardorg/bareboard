Community reverse engineering

Protocol Reverse Engineering

Closed protocols the community has pried open. What was reverse engineered, what’s still unknown, and the open-source projects that document it. Only public, community-driven work — no leaked confidential material.

Take control for under $10

You don’t need a $300 debugger. The cheapest way into a device’s firmware is a Raspberry Pi Pico (about $4), a USB-to-serial breakout board (about $2–3 — look for CH340 or CP2102), and a micro USB cable you already own. That’s the whole shopping list.

Step 1: listen before you touch

Many devices — routers, smart plugs, cameras — have a serial console (UART) that narrates everything the device does as it boots. Think of it as the device’s diary. It’s often exposed on small test pads you can reach without fully opening the case. Plug in your USB-to-serial adapter, then:

$ ls /dev/ttyUSB*
/dev/ttyUSB0
$ screen /dev/ttyUSB0 115200

/dev/ttyUSB0 is your adapter showing up. 115200 is the speed (baud rate) most devices use — if you see gibberish, try 9600 or 57600. screen just opens a window onto the serial line. Power the device on and watch:

U-Boot 2021.04 (Jan 12 2022 - 09:14:22)
DRAM:  128 MiB
Loading kernel from NAND...
Starting kernel ...
[    0.000000] Linux version 5.4.0 (builder@factory)
[    1.240000] console [ttyS0] enabled
root@smartplug:/#

That root@smartplug:/# prompt is a root shell on the device itself. From here the firmware can be copied off directly — no disassembly required.

Step 2: turn a $4 Pico into a debug probe

For chips with SWD (the debug port on ARM microcontrollers — two tiny pads usually labeled SWDIO and SWCLK), flash Picoprobe firmware onto a Raspberry Pi Pico. It becomes a proper debug probe:

$ picotool load picoprobe.uf2
Loading picoprobe.uf2 into RP2040 device...
The device was rebooted into application mode.
$ openocd -f interface/picoprobe.cfg -f target/rp2040.cfg
Open On-Chip Debugger 0.12.0
Info : RP2040 Flash Bank Command
Info : Listening on port 3333 for gdb connections

OpenOCD is free, open-source software that can now read and write the target chip’s flash through the Pico — the same job as a $100+ commercial probe.

Step 3: back up, then take over (ESP32 example)

ESP-based smart-home devices are the friendliest starting point: the manufacturer’s own flashing tool is free. Always save a copy of the original firmware first — that’s your way back if anything goes wrong:

$ esptool.py --port /dev/ttyUSB0 read_flash 0x0 0x400000 backup.bin
esptool.py v4.7
Serial port /dev/ttyUSB0
Connecting...
Chip is ESP32-D0WD-V3 (revision v3.0)
4194304 bytes read successfully.
$ esptool.py --port /dev/ttyUSB0 write_flash 0x0 tasmota.bin
Writing at 0x0003e800... (100 %)
Wrote 598016 bytes at 0x00000000 in 5.6 seconds.
Hard resetting via RTS pin...

Tasmota replaces the vendor’s cloud-dependent firmware with local control — no account, no app, no internet required. Prefer config files over clicking? ESPHome does the same job. Either way, the device now answers to you — not a server that might shut down next year (see the obsolescence archive for why that matters).

Simple entry points: everyday ports that open the door

Sometimes the case never needs to open at all. Many devices respond to the ports and buttons you already use every day — if you know the right signal to send. The methods below use ordinary connectors: a Joy-Con rail, a charging port, an Ethernet jack, a headphone socket, a USB cable. No soldering, no clips, and usually no extra hardware beyond a cable or a paperclip.

A note on scope: everything here is for hardware you own, for educational and repair purposes — understanding how your own devices boot, recovering a device that will not start, or installing community firmware that you choose. None of these methods bypass copy protection or digital rights management, and nothing here is intended to enable that. If a method does not apply to a device you own, treat it as a case study in how embedded hardware is designed.

Nintendo Switch — RCM through the Joy-Con rail

The Switch’s processor has a built-in recovery mode called RCM. To reach it, pin 10 inside the right Joy-Con rail must be connected to ground while the console powers on — this tells the console to behave as though the Home button is being held. A small jig that slides into the rail bridges pin 10 to a ground pin (pins 1, 2, or 7). Community guides recommend a proper jig (~$5, or 3D-printed) and warn that paperclips or tinfoil can damage the rail’s pads.

You need: an RCM jig; a USB-C cable; a computer running free payload software.

Steps: power the console fully off, slide the jig into the right Joy-Con rail, hold Volume Up and tap Power. A black screen means RCM is active — the screen stays dark because the console is waiting for instructions over USB.

$ # connect the Switch by USB-C, then send a bootloader payload\n$ python3 fusee-launcher.py hekate.bin\nTegra X1 detected in RCM mode (0955:7321)\nUploading payload... complete.\n$ # the Switch wakes into the Hekate bootloader

The Switch entry point is a case study in how recovery modes work at the hardware level: a boot ROM — code burned into the processor that cannot be changed — listens on USB for instructions whenever a specific pin is grounded at power-on. Researchers use this to study the Tegra X1’s boot process and to run alternative operating systems such as Linux on hardware they own. Note: payload delivery only works on unpatched launch-era consoles; later units can still enter RCM but will not accept payloads.

Samsung phones — a resistor in the charging port

Classic Samsung phones with micro-USB ports check the resistance between the port’s ID pin (pin 4) and ground (pin 5) at power-on. A 301 kΩ resistor across those two pins tells the phone to start directly in Download Mode — the firmware installation mode — with no buttons pressed. A few cents of resistor soldered into a spare micro-USB plug is the entire tool.

You need: a spare micro-USB plug, one 301 kΩ resistor (three 100 kΩ in series works the same), basic soldering.

Steps: solder the resistor between pins 4 and 5 of the plug, power the phone off, insert the jig. The phone enters Download Mode on its own — then swap in a normal USB cable and communicate with it over USB.

$ # the phone now shows the Download Mode screen\n$ lsusb | grep -i samsung\nBus 001 Device 017: ID 04e8:685d Samsung Electronics Co., Ltd\n$ # the bootloader is communicating over USB

TP-Link routers — the reset button is a recovery button

Most TP-Link routers include a built-in recovery routine: if the reset button is held while the router powers on, the bootloader pauses and tries to download a firmware file over the network (TFTP) from a fixed address — 192.168.0.66. All you need is an Ethernet cable, a paperclip for the recessed button, and free TFTP software on your computer.

You need: Ethernet cable, paperclip, free TFTP server software (tftpd64 on Windows; tftpd-hpa or dnsmasq on Linux).

Steps: set your computer’s wired address to 192.168.0.66, place the correctly named firmware file for your exact model (for example ArcherC7v2_tp_recovery.bin — check your model’s documentation for the right name) in the TFTP folder, connect to a LAN port, hold reset, power on, and release after about five seconds. The router fetches the file and flashes it by itself.

$ sudo ip addr add 192.168.0.66/24 dev eth0\n$ sudo dnsmasq --enable-tftp --tftp-root=/tftpboot --port=0\n$ cp ArcherC7v2_tp_recovery.bin /tftpboot/\n$ # hold reset, power on the router, watch the server log:\ndnsmasq-tftp: sent /tftpboot/ArcherC7v2_tp_recovery.bin to 192.168.0.1\n$ # the router flashes the file and reboots on its own

3.5mm headphone jack — a serial port in disguise

On some Android phones and media players, the 3.5 mm headphone jack doubles as a serial console. Community documentation describes the arrangement: the sleeve carries power while the tip and ring carry the transmit and receive lines. A simple adapter cable plus a USB-to-serial converter turns the headphone jack into the same boot-log window described in the playbook’s first technique — with zero disassembly.

You need: a 4-pole 3.5 mm plug wired to a USB-to-serial breakout, and the pinout for your specific device — the postmarketOS serial debugging wiki and device forums track which models support this.

Steps: connect the cable, open a terminal at 115200 baud, and power the device on.

$ screen /dev/ttyUSB0 115200\n[BL] Bootloader starting...\n[BL] serial console active\nAndroid boot log follows...

This is device-specific — it only works where the manufacturer wired the jack this way — so verify against your model’s documentation first.

ESP8266/ESP32 devices — one button, one cable

Espressif chips enter flash mode when GPIO0 is held low during power-on. Manufacturers wire this to an onboard button (often labeled BOOT or FLASH). Hold the button, connect the USB cable, release the button — the chip is now waiting for firmware over the same cable it charges from. No probe, no clips, nothing beyond the charging cable.

You need: the device’s own USB cable. That is all.

Steps: hold the button while plugging in USB, then talk to the chip with the free esptool utility. Save a backup of the original firmware first — it is your only way back.

$ # hold BOOT, plug in USB, release BOOT\n$ esptool.py --port /dev/ttyUSB0 chip_id\nDetecting chip type... ESP32\nChip ID: 0x240ac0\n$ esptool.py --port /dev/ttyUSB0 read_flash 0x0 0x400000 backup.bin\n4194304 bytes read successfully.\n$ # original firmware safely backed up, using only the charging cable

Every way in: the firmware access playbook

If a device runs code, that code is stored on a chip inside it — and there is always a method to read it. The techniques below are ordered from simplest to most involved. Each one names a specific device and the exact steps for it. Total equipment cost for the full set is under $25; most techniques need under $10.

1. Serial console — Sonoff Basic smart switch

The Sonoff Basic (an ESP8266-based smart switch) exposes a 4-pin serial header on its board: 3V3, RX, TX, GND. Connecting a USB-to-serial adapter here gives you the device’s boot log and, on many firmware versions, a command shell — all without removing any components.

You need: USB-to-serial breakout (~$2–3, CH340 or CP2102), 4 jumper wires, micro USB cable.

Use it when: the device has a visible header or test pads. This is always the first method to attempt: it is non-destructive and frequently sufficient on its own.

$ ls /dev/ttyUSB*
/dev/ttyUSB0
$ screen /dev/ttyUSB0 115200
ESP8266EX boot ROM
SPI flash detected: Winbond 25Q32 (4 MB)
WiFi connecting to HomeNet...
Tasmota 14.1.0 ready

2. Serial console on devices without a header — Amazon Echo Dot (3rd gen)

Many devices hide the serial connection on unlabelled test pads instead of a header. The pads can be identified with a multimeter: the ground pad shows continuity with the USB shield, and the transmit pad idles at 3.3 V while the device is powered. Community documentation and the device’s public FCC filing photographs show pad locations for the Echo Dot (3rd generation).

You need: USB-to-serial breakout (~$2–3), multimeter (~$8, useful for all future work), fine probe tips or pogo pins.

Use it when: there is no obvious header. The identification method — ground by continuity, transmit by idle voltage — works on nearly any device.

$ # multimeter: black probe on USB shield, red probe on each pad
$ # pad 4 reads 0 ohms --> GND
$ # pad 7 reads 3.3V idle --> TX (device transmit)
$ screen /dev/ttyUSB0 115200
U-Boot 2017.11
DRAM:  256 MiB
Starting kernel ...

3. SWD debug port — Raspberry Pi Pico as the probe

ARM microcontrollers include a Serial Wire Debug (SWD) port: two pins (SWDIO and SWCLK, plus ground) through which the chip’s entire flash memory can be read and written. A Raspberry Pi Pico flashed with the free Picoprobe firmware functions as a complete debug probe.

You need: Raspberry Pi Pico (~$4), 3 jumper wires, free OpenOCD software.

Use it when: the serial console is absent or locked. Wiring: Pico GP2 to target SWDIO, Pico GP3 to target SWCLK, GND to GND.

$ picotool load picoprobe.uf2
Loading picoprobe.uf2 into RP2040 device...
The device was rebooted into application mode.
$ openocd -f interface/picoprobe.cfg -f target/rp2040.cfg \
    -c "init; dump_image firmware.bin 0x10000000 0x200000; exit"
Open On-Chip Debugger 0.12.0
dumped 2097152 bytes in 3.42s

4. JTAG on ESP32 — smart bulbs and plugs

Espressif documents the ESP32’s JTAG debug pins: GPIO 12 (TDI), GPIO 13 (TCK), GPIO 14 (TMS), GPIO 15 (TDO). Many ESP32-based smart bulbs and plugs route these to test pads on the board edge. The same $4 Pico running Picoprobe works as the adapter.

You need: Raspberry Pi Pico (~$4) with Picoprobe, jumper wires, free OpenOCD with ESP32 support.

Use it when: working with ESP32-based IoT devices where the serial pins are inaccessible but test pads are visible.

$ openocd -f interface/picoprobe.cfg -f target/esp32.cfg
Open On-Chip Debugger 0.12.0
Info : esp32: Debug controller was reset.
Info : esp32: Core 0 was reset.
$ # flash can now be read and written through the debug port

5. SPI flash clip — Wyze Cam (Winbond 25Q128)

The Wyze Cam stores its firmware on a Winbond 25Q128 SPI flash chip in an 8-pin SOIC package. A SOIC-8 test clip clamps directly over the chip — no soldering required — and a USB programmer reads the full 16 MB.

You need: SOIC-8 test clip (~$3), CH341A USB programmer (~$3).

Use it when: serial and debug ports are unavailable but the flash chip is visible and reachable on the board.

$ flashrom -p ch341a_spi -r wyze-backup.bin
flashrom v1.3 on Linux
Found Winbond flash chip "W25Q128.V" (16384 kB, SPI).
Reading flash... done.
$ ls -la wyze-backup.bin
-rw-r--r-- 1 you you 16777216 Oct  7 08:30 wyze-backup.bin

6. Replace the firmware — Sonoff Basic with Tasmota

Once serial access is established (technique 1), the original firmware can be replaced. On the Sonoff Basic, holding the onboard button while applying power places the ESP8266 in flash mode (GPIO0 held low). The free esptool utility then writes new firmware.

You need: the USB-to-serial setup from technique 1. No additional hardware.

Use it when: the goal is local control without the vendor’s cloud service. Always save a backup of the original firmware first — it is the only path back.

$ # hold the Sonoff button, plug in USB, release the button
$ esptool.py --port /dev/ttyUSB0 read_flash 0x0 0x100000 backup.bin
Detected ESP8266EX chip
1048576 bytes read successfully.
$ esptool.py --port /dev/ttyUSB0 write_flash 0x0 tasmota.bin
Wrote 598016 bytes at 0x00000000 in 5.6 seconds.
Hard resetting via RTS pin...
$ # the switch now runs Tasmota: local web UI, no cloud account

7. eMMC direct read — phones and tablets

Phones and tablets store data on an eMMC chip — effectively a soldered-in memory card. An eMMC adapter presents it to a computer as an ordinary drive, allowing a complete image to be copied even when the device no longer boots.

You need: eMMC-to-SD adapter (~$5–8), fine tweezers or pogo pins for the contact pads.

Use it when: the device is non-functional but the storage chip is intact.

$ dmesg | tail -2
[  120.4] mmc0: new high speed MMC card at address 0001
[  120.5] mmcblk0: mmc0:0001 SA08G 7.25 GiB
$ sudo dd if=/dev/mmcblk0 of=emmc-backup.img bs=4M status=progress
7570718720 bytes (7.6 GB) copied

8. Over-the-air interception — no hardware required

Devices download firmware updates from the internet, and those files frequently sit on public servers behind predictable URLs. Observing what the device — or its companion app — requests reveals the direct download address.

You need: a computer. Nothing else.

Use it when: any network-connected device needs analysis. Attempt this before any hardware method: it costs nothing.

$ curl -s 'http://update.vendor.com/api/check?model=X100&ver=1.2.3'
{"latest": "1.4.0", "url": "http://update.vendor.com/fw/X100_1.4.0.bin"}
$ curl -O http://update.vendor.com/fw/X100_1.4.0.bin
$ file X100_1.4.0.bin
X100_1.4.0.bin: Squashfs filesystem, little endian

9. Companion app traffic analysis — proxy method

Vendor phone apps communicate with the same update servers as the hardware. Routing the phone’s traffic through a proxy on a computer reveals firmware URLs, API endpoints, and authentication flows.

You need: free mitmproxy software; phone and computer on the same Wi-Fi network.

Use it when: the device itself is uncooperative but its app checks for updates.

$ mitmproxy --mode transparent --showhost
# install the mitmproxy certificate on the phone, then open the vendor app
192.168.1.5:51234: GET https://api.vendor.com/v2/firmware/latest
            << 200 OK 312b
            {"version":"2.1.0","url":"https://cdn.vendor.com/fw/app_2.1.0.zip"}

10. Bootloader modes — DFU, fastboot, download mode

Nearly every phone and tablet has a documented key combination that starts it in a special bootloader mode — Apple’s DFU, Android fastboot, Samsung Download mode — in which the bootloader communicates over USB and accepts low-level commands outside the normal operating system’s restrictions.

You need: a USB cable and the published key combination for the device.

Use it when: the operating system will not boot, or low-level access is required that the running system would not permit.

$ # iPhone: hold Power + Home 10 seconds, release Power, keep holding Home
$ lsusb | grep -i apple
Bus 001 Device 042: ID 05ac:1227 Apple, Inc. iPhone (DFU Mode)
$ # the bootloader is now communicating over USB

11. Bus traffic capture — logic analyzer

Chips communicate over SPI and I2C buses on exposed circuit traces. A logic analyzer records these signals, and free software decodes them into readable data. Observing the boot ROM fetch firmware from flash captures the firmware without any modification to the device.

You need: 24 MHz logic analyzer clone (~$8–10), free PulseView/sigrok software, mini grabber clips.

Use it when: understanding the boot sequence is the goal, or when data in transit (keys, configuration) must be observed.

$ sigrok-cli --driver fx2lafw --config samplerate=24M \
    --channels MOSI=0,MISO=1,CLK=2,CS=3 -O binary > boot.sr
$ # open boot.sr in PulseView and apply the SPI decoder:
$ # MOSI shows flash read commands; MISO returns the firmware bytes

12. CH341A USB programmer — the $3 instrument

A single inexpensive USB adapter handles both SPI flash chips and I2C EEPROMs — the two most common firmware storage types in IoT devices, routers, and PC BIOS chips. Combined with the clip from technique 5, it reads firmware from a powered-off board in minutes.

You need: CH341A programmer (~$3).

Use it when: routinely — it is worth acquiring before it is needed. It complements techniques 5 and 11.

$ # SPI flash (8-pin chip):
$ flashrom -p ch341a_spi -r spi-backup.bin
Found Winbond flash chip "W25Q64.V" (8192 kB, SPI).
Reading flash... done.
$ # I2C EEPROM (small settings chip) with the same adapter:
$ flashrom -p ch341a_i2c -r eeprom-backup.bin
Reading flash... done.

13. Voltage glitching — when the chip refuses

Some chips verify a password or signature before granting access. A precisely timed dip in supply voltage — or a brief disturbance of the clock signal — can cause the chip to skip exactly that verification step. This is an advanced method, included here for completeness.

You need: a Pico or Arduino to generate the pulse (~$4), a MOSFET to dip the power rail (~$1), and patience: success typically requires hundreds of attempts.

Use it when: all software and debug interfaces are locked, and the device must be accessed without removing chips.

$ python3 glitch.py --target bootloader --attempts 1000
[#0046] offset 4380ns width 120ns ... no response
[#0047] offset 4400ns width 120ns ... no response
[#0048] offset 4420ns width 120ns ... response received
verification bypassed after 48 attempts

14. Chip removal — the final option

When no other method succeeds, the storage chip is removed from the board with hot air and read directly with the CH341A from technique 12. This destroys the board’s original assembly, but the firmware itself is preserved.

You need: hot air rework station (~$15) or heat gun, tweezers, flux, CH341A programmer.

Use it when: nothing else has worked — the chip is underfilled, the board is non-functional, or all interfaces are fused off. There is no software step here; once the chip is removed, it is read exactly as in technique 5.
