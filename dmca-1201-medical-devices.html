    The DMCA medical-device repair exemption is under threat — comments due December 7

    There is a federal rule that says you may pick the software lock on your own medical device to repair it. That rule is an exemption, renewed every three years, and this cycle the Copyright Office has singled it out: it is the only existing exemption the Register intends not to renew automatically. If you own a powered wheelchair — or you repair one — this is the legal hinge under much of the work this site teaches. The first round of public comments is due December 7, 2026. This page explains what the exemption does, what losing it would mean, and where to comment.

    What Section 1201 is

    Section 1201 of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) makes it illegal to circumvent a technological protection measure — a software lock, encryption, or access control — that guards a copyrighted work. The law was written for piracy, but it reaches much further: the firmware inside your wheelchair, your tractor, or your car is copyrighted software, and the lock that stops you reading its fault codes is a technological protection measure.

    Every three years, the Copyright Office runs a triennial rulemaking in which the public can ask for exemptions — specific, lawful uses that the ban should not cover. One long-standing exemption covers diagnosis, maintenance, and repair of medical devices: circumventing a lock to diagnose or repair a device you lawfully own. Without it, reading a wheelchair’s fault codes through a locked interface, or using an owner tool to back up its settings, could be a federal copyright violation even though the chair is yours.

    What is happening now

    In the tenth triennial proceeding, the notice of proposed rulemaking issued on October 28, 2026, the Register of Copyrights intends to recommend renewal of all existing exemptions — except one. The medical-device repair exemption drew meaningful opposition, and it will be treated as a new petition, meaning its supporters must make the case for it from scratch. It is the only exemption singled out this way.

    The timetable:

    • December 7, 2026 — first-round comments from supporters are due.
    • February 22, 2027 — opposition comments are due.
    • March 22, 2027 — replies are due.

    The proceeding is at the Copyright Office: copyright.gov/1201/2027.

    What the exemption does — and does not — cover

    • It covers: circumventing a software lock on a medical device you lawfully own, to diagnose, maintain, or repair it. That is the legal basis for an owner reading their own chair’s fault codes, backing up its settings before a module swap, or using an owner-built tool such as our Chair Control to read what the dealer’s programmer reads.
    • It does not cover: making or distributing the tools that do the circumventing. Section 1201’s anti-trafficking ban has no exemption, and the Librarian of Congress has no authority to exempt it. Owners may legally pick the lock; selling them the pick is still banned. Our PMD law section states this half-gap plainly.
    • It does not cover: piracy, or copying the manufacturer’s firmware. The exemption is for repair of your own device, nothing else.

    What losing it would mean

    If the exemption lapses, the legal ground under owner repair of medical devices narrows sharply. Repairing your own chair would still be protected in some ways — the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act still limits warranty voiding, and state wheelchair repair laws in eight states still require manufacturers to provide firmware and tools — but the federal permission to get past a software lock to diagnose your own device would be gone. The practical effect is to hand the manufacturer a copyright argument against any owner or independent shop that works around a lock, even when the work is ordinary repair.

    The permanent fix, stalled

    A permanent statutory exception would end the three-year cycle: the federal Wheelchair Right to Repair Act (H.R. 5039), introduced August 26, 2025, would write a repair exception into law. It has not advanced — no movement was found in the last two weeks of November 2026. Until it does, the exemption — and the comment deadline — is the fight that matters.

    What to do before December 7

    Comments go to the Copyright Office through its proceeding page at copyright.gov/1201/2027. What helps most is concrete, first-hand evidence: that you own a device, that a software lock blocked a repair, what the lock cost you in time or money, and what happened when the repair was delayed. A short, specific, true account from an owner or a repair shop is worth more than a long general argument. Say plainly that you support renewal of the medical-device repair exemption.
