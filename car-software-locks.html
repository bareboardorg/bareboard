    “Closing Shop”: when the software is the lock

    A new report, published the weekend of November 22–23, 2026, puts hard numbers on a familiar grievance: car makers’ repair software wastes an estimated 83 million hours of independent mechanics’ time a year — about 42,000 full-time technicians’ worth — valued at about $12 billion. The lock is no longer a special screw or a sealed case. It is software: diagnostics, calibrations, and registrations that only the manufacturer’s tool can do, sold back to the people who need it at prices that close small shops. Wheelchair owners know this pattern already. The car numbers are simply the clearest measurement of it yet.

    The report

    “Closing Shop” was produced by iFixit, the U.S. PIRG Education Fund, and FULU, and published through repair.org. Its findings, in plain terms:

    • 83 million hours a year of independent mechanics’ time wasted for lack of manufacturer repair software and tools — about 42,000 full-time technicians, valued at about $12 billion at $140 an hour.
    • 88% of mechanics surveyed turned away repairs in the past year for lack of software or tools.
    • $15,878 a year in software subscriptions for one mechanic to fix five car brands.
    • A $2,178 “entry fee” for a taillight fix: $79 for the repair information, $300 for the diagnostic software, and $1,799 for the adapter and cables.

    The report’s asks are modest: free basic diagnostic software, and a cap of $100 a month or $1,000 a year per car for repair software access.

    The caveat, carried with the numbers. The $12 billion figure is a modelled estimate from a small, self-selected survey — 28 mechanics gave the time estimates it rests on — not a census of the trade. The Autowire flagged this on November 23, 2026, and this page repeats it: the numbers are the best available measurement, not a headcount. What is not in doubt is the mechanism underneath them: the software paywall is real, the subscription prices are documented, and the turned-away repairs are reported by the mechanics themselves.

    The relatable version: a $50 battery that costs $150 to fit

    The report’s numbers are abstract. The social echo made them concrete. On November 23, 2026, a Facebook reel by Garage Goblin about BMW battery registration drew 9,300 likes and 1,100 comments: replacing a car battery yourself is only half the job, because the car must be told — “registered” — that it has a new battery, or it over- or under-charges it. That registration costs $50 to $150 at a dealer, for a procedure that takes minutes. Commenters compared it to John Deere’s locks on farm equipment. The pattern is identical: a routine owner repair made impossible without the manufacturer’s software, and the software sold back at a price.

    The bridge to wheelchairs

    Everything in this report has a wheelchair equivalent, with higher stakes:

    • Dealer-only programmers. A power chair’s settings, fault logs, and configuration live behind dealer-only tools — R-net PC Programmer, LiNX Access, Curtis programmers — in the same way a car’s calibrations live behind the maker’s diagnostic software. Our PMD section documents this lock, and our Chair Control tool is the owner-side answer: read everything, back it up, and change only what is proven safe.
    • Parts pairing. A module swap on a chair can silently take the owner’s clinical fit with it, with no backup ever offered — the same “replace the part, lose the settings” pattern as a car that needs a re-registration after a battery swap.
    • The approval gauntlet. In the same week as the report, wheelchair users were describing a process requiring four separate home visits just to get a repair approved, and chairs costing $20,000 to $30,000 with $350 batteries and $600 to $900 joysticks that owners cannot afford to replace. The car report measures wasted mechanic hours; the wheelchair version is measured in people stranded without the chair they need to leave the house.
    • The law is thinner for chairs. Car repair software is at least the subject of a national report and right-to-repair bills. Wheelchairs have repair laws in only eight states (see The Shield), and the federal DMCA medical-device repair exemption — the legal hinge that lets owners circumvent a software lock to repair their own chair — is itself under threat in the Copyright Office’s current proceeding (see our DMCA 1201 explainer).

    What the report asks for, translated

    Free basic diagnostic software and a capped subscription are the car world’s version of what wheelchair owners have been asking for all along: the manual, the fault codes, and the settings for the machine they own, without a dealer’s permission slip. The car numbers give the argument its scale — $12 billion, 88% of shops — and the wheelchair cases give it its urgency. A taillight that costs $2,178 to fix is an insult. A chair that cannot be fixed is a person who cannot move.
